# Supreme Court on Marcos

Supreme Court of Marcos
Date:       2017-04-16
Publisher:  Philippine Daily Inquirer (Inquirer Opinion)
Source:     https://opinion.inquirer.net/103206/supreme-court-marcos

By: Artemio V. Panganiban | 05:30 AM April 16, 2017
On Feb. 26, I discussed the case of Estate of Marcos vs Republic (Jan. 18, 2017), which labelled as ill-gotten and forfeited in favor of the State the "pieces of jewelry known as the Malacañang Collection."
Plundering regime. I also pointed to two other cases: 1) Republic vs Sandiganbayan, (July 15, 2003) which forfeited $658,175,373.60 in Swiss banks, and 2) Marcos Jr. vs Republic (April 25, 2012) which forfeited $3,369,975 "as of 1983."
Readers (notably, some history teachers) asked: Apart from these, were there other decisions showing how Ferdinand Marcos accumulated ill-gotten wealth? Yes, the Court, in Yuchengco vs Sandiganbayan (Jan. 20, 2006), said:
"In PCGG v. Pena, this Court, describing the rule of Marcos as a well-entrenched plundering regime of twenty years, noted the magnitude of the past regime's organized pillage and the ingenuity of the plunderers and pillagers with the assistance of the experts and best legal minds…"
Ingenious plan. In that early case of PCGG vs Pena (April 12, 1988), the Court cited an example of how Marcos ingeniously hid his ill-gotten wealth: "In the ongoing case filed by the government to recover from the Marcoses valuable real estate holdings in New York and the Lindenmere estate in Long Island, former PCGG chairman Jovito Salonga has revealed that their names do not appear on any title to the property. Every building in New York is titled in the name of a Netherlands
Antilles Corporation, which in turn is purportedly owned by three Panamanian corporations, with bearer shares. This means that the shares of this corporation can change hands any time, since they can be transferred, under the law of Panama, without previous registration on the books of the corporation. One of the first documents that we discovered shortly after the February revolution was a declaration of trust handwritten by Mr. Joseph Bernstein on April 4, 1982 on a Manila Peninsula Hotel stationery stating that he would act as a trustee for the benefit of President Ferdinand Marcos and would act solely pursuant to the instructions of Marcos with respect to the Crown Building in New York. Were it not for this stroke of good fortune, it would have been impossible, legally and technically, to prove and recover this ill-gotten wealth from the deposed President and his family, although their ownership of these fabulous real estate holdings [was] a matter of public notoriety."

In other cases, the Court described the Marcos years as "a dark chapter in our history" (Licaros vs Sandiganbayan, Oct. 18, 2004) and a regime of "national trauma" (Republic vs Tuvera, Feb. 16, 2007). Worse, in Galman vs Sandiganbayan (Sept 12, 1986), the Court charged Marcos with stage-managing court proceedings:
Mockery of judicial process. "The Court … holds … that the then President (code named Olympus) had stage-managed in and from Malacañang Palace 'a scripted and pre-determined manner of handling and disposing of the Aquino-Galman murder case;' and that 'the prosecution in the Aquino Galman case and the Justices who tried and decided the same acted under the compulsion of some pressure which proved to be beyond their capacity to resist, and which not only prevented the prosecution to fully ventilate its position and to offer all the evidences which it could have otherwise presented, but also pre-determined the final outcome of the case' of total absolution of the

(moro-moro) the trial and the close monitoring of the entire proceedings to assure the predetermined ignominious final outcome are without parallel and precedent in our annals and jurisprudence."

## Summary

Responding to reader requests -- especially from history teachers -- for additional SC jurisprudence documenting Marcos's ill-gotten wealth beyond the cases discussed on February 26, Panganiban surveys four categories of decisions. First, the forfeiture cases: Republic v. Sandiganbayan (July 15, 2003) forfeited $658.175 million from Swiss banks; Marcos Jr. v. Republic (April 25, 2012) forfeited $3.37 million. Second, the regime characterizations: Yuchengco v. Sandiganbayan (January 20, 2006), citing PCGG v. Pena (April 12, 1988), described the Marcos rule as 'a well-entrenched plundering regime of twenty years' marked by 'organized pillage'; Licaros v. Sandiganbayan (October 18, 2004) called it 'a dark chapter in our history'; Republic v. Tuvera (February 16, 2007) described it as a regime of 'national trauma.' Third, the concealment technique: former PCGG Chairman Jovito Salonga revealed in the New York real estate case that Marcos owned the Crown Building through a Netherlands Antilles corporation, owned in turn by Panamanian corporations with untraceable bearer shares -- discoverable only because of the Bernstein handwritten trust declaration dated April 4, 1982 on Manila Peninsula Hotel stationery, found among the first post-EDSA documents. Fourth and most damning, Galman v. Sandiganbayan (September 12, 1986) found that Marcos -- code named 'Olympus' -- stage-managed the Aquino-Galman murder trial in a 'scripted and pre-determined manner' that pre-determined the acquittal of the accused, acting through pressure that the prosecution and judges 'proved to be beyond their capacity to resist' -- conduct the Court found had 'no parallel and precedent in our annals and jurisprudence.'

## Notable Anecdotes

### Anecdote 1

Joseph Bernstein's handwritten trust declaration dated April 4, 1982 on Manila Peninsula Hotel stationery -- stating he would act as trustee for Marcos and solely per Marcos's instructions for the Crown Building in New York -- discovered among the first post-EDSA documents; without it, the legal link to Marcos's New York real estate would have been impossible to prove.

### Anecdote 2

The multilayer concealment structure: Crown Building in New York titled in a Netherlands Antilles corporation, in turn owned by Panamanian corporations with bearer shares transferable without registration.

### Anecdote 3

Former PCGG Chairman Jovito Salonga revealing the New York real estate concealment scheme in the Lindenmere estate case -- the PCGG chairman as the primary source of the plunder-documentation narrative.

### Anecdote 4

Galman v. Sandiganbayan's finding that the prosecution and the justices who tried the Aquino-Galman case 'acted under the compulsion of some pressure which proved to be beyond their capacity to resist' -- one of the sharpest judicial indictments of a co-equal branch's coercion of the judiciary.
