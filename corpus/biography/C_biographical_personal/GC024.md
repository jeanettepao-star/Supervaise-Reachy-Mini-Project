# Chapter 4: The Law Student

__CHAPTER 4__

*Though I walk in the midst of trouble, you preserve my life\.*

__*                                                                                           Psalm 138\.7*__

__			__

				__	The Law Student __

Far Eastern University is located in the heart of Manila, but the frenzy of the metropolis quickly dissipates within the premises\.  It is a campus of architectural gems, a distinction later recognized by UNESCO in the 2005 Asia\-Pacific Heritage Awards for Cultural Heritage Conservation\. <a id="_gjdgxs"></a>

<a id="_30j0zll"></a>The Science Building was an eight\-story structure constructed in 1957 by renowned Architect Felipe Mendoza\. His aesthetic brought together form and function, making the best use of natural light and ventilation\. An earthquake damaged it in 1970, resulting in the scaling down of the building to four floors\. Then there was the Arellano Hall which housed the Institute of Law\. Designed by National Artist Pablo Antonio, it was one of two edifices flanking the Administration Building\. Impressed by Antonio’s work, particularly the Ideal Theater on Avenida Rizal, FEU founder Dr\. Nicanor Reyes commissioned him to design several buildings for the university\. The campus became an assemblage of Art Deco architecture, reputedly the largest ensemble of sleek, linear edifices with curved ornamental forms breaking the geometric lines\.

Art spent considerable time on campus, most of his waking hours in these buildings, attending classes and fulfilling his responsibilities as a student leader\. It was, however, in the FEU chapel where he found some quiet moments to pause and reflect\. 

The SCA office was situated beneath the consecrated space of Our Lady of Fatima Chapel\. The second building designed by Felipe Mendoza showcased works of three National Artists\. The Crucifix and the Stations of the Cross were painted by National Artist for Painting Carlos “Botong” Francisco, then a young intern\. Art describes from memory the 14 images depicting Christ’s Way of Sorrow: “The paintings were not visibly separate from each other the way they usually were\. They were big and seemed connected to each other in a continuum, like the Sistine Chapel ceiling paintings\.” Botong Francisco also painted the Crucified Christ which dominated the wall behind the altar\. The oil\-on\-canvas rendition hung above the tabernacle and was the focal point of the interiors\.

There were several life\-sized religious statues which the noted Quiapo *santonero* Maximo Vicente had been commissioned to make\. Among these were the Sacred Heart of Jesus, St\. Joseph with Jesus, and Our Lady of Fatima\. Years later, National Artist for Sculpture Napoleon Abueva would donate a fiber\-glass sculpture of *La Pieta,* inspired by Michelangelo’s masterpiece in Rome\. 

The exterior of the chapel was distinguished by the artistry of still another National Artist for Visual Arts, Vicente Manansala\. On the facade he created a tiled mosaic of Our Lady of Fatima in bright blue hues\. It was a rare work, being the only one of a kind that the Filipino

master had ever done\.

The chapel could accommodate a combined sitting and standing capacity of 300 to 500 people\. It was a small space, given the academic population of enrollees, teachers and university personnel\. Mendoza addressed the limitation by keeping three sides of the building open and constructing the altar at an elevation that allowed the students sitting under the trees to see the altar from where they sat, allowing them to participate in the masses being officiated\. Art had not yet gotten his spiritual moorings, but he was drawn to the chapel and to the Lady for whom it was dedicated\. Decades later, he would have an epiphany of the Blessed Mother’s presence at different times of his life\. Times spent in the chapel had a calming effect much like a mother’s embrace\.

__Teachers’ Favorite__

The university’s curriculum provided a well\-rounded education that exposed Art’s inquisitive mind to different disciplines\. In the course of his university life, the young Art developed life\-changing associations that significantly contributed to who he would become\. “FEU taught me that it was not enough to be an expert in one’s profession,” he says\. “One should also have more than an adequate knowledge of the arts, history, literature, ethics and the humanities in general\.” 

His appreciation for other disciplines beyond the study of law widened in the classrooms of luminaries like Dr\. Alejandro R\. Roces, who was then the newly appointed dean of the Institute of Arts and Sciences\. Dr\. Roces was highly instrumental in passing on the ideals of a truly educated person, one bred in the humanities, anchored on values and moved by principles\. “His words and example influenced my youthful aspirations for a place in society,” Art intimates\.

There was also Dr\. Teodoro Evangelista, the FEU president whom Art describes as outstanding and single\-minded in his educational policies\. “Dr\. Evangelista believed that education in the right sense of the word aims to prepare for work, not as an end in itself but as a means for the enjoyment of a life of the mind and the spirit\.”

Art has memories of other teachers under whose tutelage he would become proficient in the subjects they taught\. “In my preparatory law course, I remember very well Arts and Sciences Dean Francisco M\. Africa who was my professor in argumentation and debate,” he shares\. “There was Dr\. Benito F\. Reyes who expounded on philosophy, Prof\. Angelica P\. Mendoza who explained botany, and Prof\. Rosario P\. Melchor who taught me English\. Up to now, I remember and use the basic knowledge they imparted to me\. I still use the elementary rules of logic I learned from Dean Africa and those of basic grammar from Professor Melchor\.”

Another professor he recalls was Primo Tongco who tried to impress Marxist doctrines on his malleable mind\. Professor Tongco taught in liberal arts and engaged the pre\-law student in thought\-provoking exchanges\.  “He told me communism was Christianity,” Art recalls\. “Of course, at that time I didn’t understand it\.  He said that according to Christianity we must share with our neighbor, we must share everything, as we didn’t own anything\. That was what communism was about, he said\. The proletariat didn’t own anything\. Everything was owned by the State\. He triumphantly proclaimed that the disciples of Christ shared everything, but he twisted that teaching and said the Marxist philosophy of collective ownership was ideal\. He kept on emphasizing, “To each according to his need, from each according to his ability\.” 

“I became Professor Tongco’s favorite\. He loved to argue with me and pierce my brain\. He told me, ‘You are bright\. You do your best when you are a professional, but you should not get paid according to your ability\. Because if you are paid according to your ability, you will be paid more and you will become rich\. That’s bad, because you will deprive others\. You should give to others, and you do that by not receiving more than the others\. Everybody should be given the same thing\.’ That was very attractive to me\. I was very poor and what he said appealed to me and sounded true\.”

Another professor who figured prominently during his preparatory law studies was Dr\. Benito F\. Reyes\. Of him, Art said, “He was not only a scholar, but he was also a good pastor and a good orator\. He would orate in class, repeating an idea in different ways that really impressed me\. He was my professor in my first year and again the following year\. Dr\. Reyes wanted me to follow in his footsteps, but I kept reminding myself my father wanted me to be a lawyer\. I could not be a preacher or even a philosopher\. I had to follow my father, who by that time had already died\. If it were not for him, and if it were not for my ambition to be a chemical engineer, I could have veered towards ‘theosophy\.’ That was how the professor called his teaching\. “He combined religion and philosophy and argued that one finds God not through faith but through reason only\. He was very impressive\.” 

It was the Christian tenets, however, that prevailed under the mentorship of Fr\. Nolan as well as that of another Columban clergyman, Fr\. James Sheehy, SSC\. “Fr\. Sheehy insisted that a good Catholic must not only be personally pure but must also lead others to purity\. In short that meant being Christ to others\. These teachings were enough for me to further my efforts to learn and experience my faith more\. My initial contacts in FEU impelled me to search for God more deeply and passionately all my life\.

	“Because of the influence of these great men, I learned early on that excellence in liberal education and specialization in a career must go hand in hand\. Indeed, excellence in law is not enough; lawyers must also have a good grasp of the arts and sciences in order to perform their work fully\.”

__Legal Luminaries__

	Art’s professors in law were legal heavyweights, several of whom would figure in the nation’s political life\. “In the Institute of Law, I was amply armed with a formidable legal arsenal\. At the top of the list was Dean Jovito R\. Salonga, who became my role model in my chosen career and, more important, in my life as a whole\. Through my esteemed guru, I had the privilege of receiving not only UP\-style education, but Harvard and Yale\-type techniques as well\. Dean Salonga shared with us what he learned in those three schools where he finished his bachelor’s, master’s and doctoral degrees\. I am grateful to him for his legal expertise; but more than that, for his moral courage, high\-mindedness and love of country – values that I have aspired to internalize and emulate\.”

Constituting the law faculty then were  some of the best and brightest lawyers like Neptali A\. Gonzalez, who like Dean Salonga later became Senate President; Edgardo L\. Paras and Salvador Esguerra, who both became Supreme Court justices; Arsenio Solidum, Antonio Lucero, Luis B\. Reyes, Angel Mojica and Jose Leuterio who were later  appointed to the Court of Appeals\. The roster of educators included young Ivy League graduates like Juan Ponce Enrile, Manuel Montecillo, Reynaldo Alejandro and Augusto Caesar Espiritu\. “All of them demanded excellence, diligence and hard work from their students\. Small wonder that during my college days, graduates of FEU Law always landed among the topnotchers in the annual bar examinations\. Indeed, the academic excellence I learned from FEU has made me confident and self\-assured\.”

	Among these men of rank, it was Dean Salonga who chiseled out values that the future lawyer sought to emulate\. Even now, Art holds him in the highest esteem\. “He was not just a good lawyer who topped the Bar exam\. He took his masters at Harvard and his doctorate at Yale\. Not many have finished a masters in Harvard and a doctorate in Yale\. Fewer still are those who can combine the two schools because these are rivals in the United States\. One does not recognize the degrees of the other\. In other words, if you graduate with a Master of Laws in Harvard, Yale will not recognize that for admission to doctoral studies\. But in his case, Dean Salonga was able to convince Yale that he could enroll for his doctorate in that university, even if his masters was from Harvard\. That he was able to do so was quite an achievement\. Indeed, he was a fantastic person, and I am proud to say that he was my professor when I was in law school from 1956 to 1960, and that I became one of his favorite students\.”

The dean would write in a 1994 Foreword to one of the books authored by his student: “Art became my student in Corporation Law and Evidence in third year and Private International Law in his 4th year\. He had become the President of the FEU Central Student Organization during his sophomore year, which was unprecedented, then became the founder and first President of the National Union of Students \(NUS\) during his 3rd year and of the Student Catholic Action \(SCA\) during his 4th year\. As no student of mine could afford to take any subject by sheer bluff in the hope that he would get a passing grade due to his popularity and his many extracurricular activities, I told him the business of a law student was to study and excel for his own good and for the sake of the FEU Institute of Law\.”

Art adds his own recollection: “Although I was active in extracurricular activities, I did not forget my academic studies, because Dean Salonga had taken a liking for me and was monitoring my grades all the time\.” He continued, “It was Dean Salonga who constantly inspired and guided me to excel in all things, but to prioritize my academic studies\. Much like a father, he would tell me often enough, ‘I don’t care if you are the president of the Central Student Council of FEU and head of the National Union of Students of the Philippines\. Your priority as a law student should be your academics\.’ He emphasized that extracurricular activities were merely secondary to formal education\. Gently, he would remind me ‘To whom much is given, much is also required\.’”

__Another Brush with Mortality__

It was a balancing act for the young man who was just as caught up in the NUS as he was in his law studies\. One of the issues that NUS undertook to address was communism\. Responding to the signs of the times, Art organized information campaigns against the political and economic ideologies opposed to democracy\. He himself had listened to persuasive arguments passed on by one of his professors and was aware of the potential sway of that political and economic ideology\. Credit is once again given to Fr\. Nolan for giving him the perspective that strengthened his belief contrary to the communist ideals\. Art made it his responsibility to undertake action informing others similarly situated as himself in the academe\. He recounts, “In 1958, I was leading the NUS delegation to Naga to conduct a seminar for student leaders to help them understand what communism was about and why it was abhorrent\.” 

It was during one of the organization’s out\-of\-town activities that he became sick in the stomach\. After the talks were done, their group took advantage of some free time to tour the vicinity\. Just as they were climbing Mount Mayon, Art experienced severe abdominal pains\. Some of his friends took him down to Tabaco in Albay for medical treatment\. “The only clinic available at that time was St\. Mary’s Maternity Clinic,” Art recounts\. “They brought me there even if it was for women\.” The doctor who attended to him diagnosed his symptoms as the result of indigestion and gave him castor oil\. That remedy only aggravated his condition and he ended up vomiting the medication\. “In desperation, the doctor gave me a shot of morphine, and I slept for the next 24 hours\.” He felt much better afterwards and rejoined the conference as presiding officer\. That evening, he was on the dance floor as if nothing had happened\.

However, two weeks later, the stomach pains recurred with such severity that his classmates brought him to the FEU Hospital on Morayta Street, which was then adjacent to the university\. It turned out he had appendicitis\. His medical condition put him in a situation that confronted an issue he had been opposing\. As a student leader, Art had taken a position against a six\-peso compulsory hospitalization and medical fee being mandated for all students\. He questioned the additional compulsory charges, arguing it was the duty of the university to provide medical help for all enrollees\. 

When Dr\. Ricardo Alfonso who was the surgeon and director of the hospital came to see him, he said, “Artemio, there’s something wrong inside you\. I don’t know what it is, but something is wrong\. I have to open you up because you have poison inside your stomach\. What do you want to do? You want to pay the full hospitalization costs and surgeon’s fees, or do you just want to pay the six\-peso medical fee required for all students?” The point was well\-taken\.  “Of course, I told him I’d rather pay the six pesos,” Art said\. “You win, Doctor\. I will withdraw my opposition\.”

Dr\. Alfonso was aware of the previous episode in Naga\. He said, “You’re very lucky your appendicitis was not located in the usual front right\. It was at the back left on the opposite side, covered with fat tissues that contained the poison and kept it from spreading\.” After performing the procedure, he explained that the toxins had begun to spread and it was necessary to clean up the internal organs\.  “Your appendix ruptured two weeks ago,” the surgeon declared\. “The Lord must love you\.”

“At that time, I did not understand what he meant,” Art said\. “It took decades before I realized that Dr\. Alfonso was saying the Lord had saved me\. “When I look back now, I believe I survived a war, dysentery as a child, falling from a moving vehicle and being almost run over as a boy, and a ruptured appendix as a student because God had a mission for me and thus delivered me from death\.”

Art reflects on his activist days\. “God has intervened in my life in so many ways\. I was young and reckless\. I confronted people, the authorities\. We started rallies long before the demonstrations against Marcos when he imposed martial law in 1972\. As a student leader, I led the demonstrations in FEU\. This took up a lot of my time\. It came to a point when, during my sophomore year in law school, my grades tumbled and disqualified me from continuing as a scholar\. I lost my scholarship and could not enroll anymore for the first semester of my third year\.

 “It was good that my Uncle Ben came to the rescue\. He asked me how much my tuition fee cost\. At the time, it was about 135 pesos per semester\. He said, ‘I will give you 150 pesos to cover tuition for your first semester, your books and your allowance\.’ My books cost only about 10 to 20 pesos\.” His uncle admonished him, “Don’t think of doing anything else other than studying\.  If you don’t get good grades, I won’t support you for the second semester\.” Art took his uncle’s words to heart\. He gave more time to his academics and got his scholarship back for the second semester\. 

__The Death of Patricia__

Art had to deal with more than just the academic load, his responsibilities in the student government as well as in the other organizations\. A personal tragedy during his fourth year of studies added to the load he was carrying\. His beloved *Ima *passed away\. 

Agang lived in Marulas, Polo, Bulacan, in a house that Art, Nardo and Frankie had constructed with their bare hands\. It was a small place they had toiled on together\. They slept on bare ground at the construction site to guard against possible theft of the materials\. When it was finally done, Art recalls his feeling of accomplishment and pride in what he saw as a beautiful house\.  It became the home he shared with his *Ima*,* *brother and uncle\.

Agang had not been well in her last years, her health diminished by tuberculosis\. When there was money to be spared, she would get injections of streptomycin which Nardo administered\. More often, there were no funds for medication and after four years she succumbed to the disease\. Art grieved the loss that seared into the depths of his heart\. 

“I had always blamed God,” he reflects\. “As a child, as a student, as a leader, even as a professional, I blamed Him for the difficulties we experienced\. It was only much later that I realized God does not remove obstacles\. Rather, he gives us the strength to overcome them\.” 

His studies became an outlet for the rage within\.  He became more immersed in his academics and in preparations for the professional licensure examination for lawyers\. “Dean Salonga told me he expected me to be one of the bar topnotchers,” Art remembers\.  The added pressure of his mentor’s expectation took a toll on his health, and he fell sick with a severe case of gastroenteritis and the flu\.

“On the first day of the exams, I was confined at the FEU Hospital\. Disheartened and discouraged, I thought of giving up and not continuing with the examinations\. Dean Salonga, however, was adamant and emphatic in advising me to continue\. So I persevered and had to get a special permission from my doctor to walk through the flooded streets

to the nearby University of the East where the exams were being held\.”

	Dean Salonga recounted that time in his Foreword to Art’s

2007 book, *A Test of Courage:* “He took the Bar Review seriously, but unfortunately, he was hospitalized during the first Sunday of the 1960 bar exams because of diarrhea and flu\. There was a heavy downpour the night before the exams and he had to walk over flooded water on R\. Papa Street that connected FEU Hospital where he was confined and the University of the East, the venue of the bar tests\. He was discouraged by his performance during the first day which was why he came and asked for my advice\. After shaking and holding hands a little longer than usual, I said, ‘Art, take the exam; you will get well\.’ He followed my advice and placed sixth in the 1960 bar exams\. Later, in talking about the same incident, whether at the FEU or at Kilosbayan Forum or elsewhere, I would take the liberty of saying Art might have placed first or second if he had not been sick\.”

Art graduated with the degree of Bachelor of Laws, cum laude\.

## Summary

Chapter 4 covers Art's four-year law course at FEU (1956-1960), framed by Psalm 138:7's 'Though I walk in the midst of trouble, you preserve my life.' The chapter opens with the physical setting: Felipe Mendoza's 1957 Science Building (later UNESCO-recognized in 2005), Pablo Antonio's Arellano Hall housing the Institute of Law (commissioned by Dr. Nicanor Reyes, the FEU founder), and the Our Lady of Fatima Chapel decorated by four National Artists -- Botong Francisco's Stations of the Cross and Crucified Christ, Maximo Vicente's life-sized statues, Napoleon Abueva's fiber-glass *La Pieta* inspired by Michelangelo, and Vicente Manansala's unique blue-mosaic Our Lady of Fatima on the facade. The SCA office sat below the chapel, and Art felt drawn to the chapel even before he had 'gotten his spiritual moorings.' The teachers form a register of named formation: Dr. Alejandro R. Roces (Dean of Arts and Sciences) on the truly educated person; Dr. Teodoro Evangelista (FEU president) on education for the life of the mind; Dean Francisco M. Africa on argumentation and debate; Dr. Benito F. Reyes on philosophy and 'theosophy' (whose preacher-philosopher path Art declined out of filial loyalty to Temi's law wish); Prof. Angelica P. Mendoza on botany; Prof. Rosario P. Melchor on English grammar; Prof. Primo Tongco trying to convert the poor student to Marxism ('communism was Christianity,' 'to each according to his need, from each according to his ability' -- 'sounded true' to Art); and Fr. James Sheehy SSC continuing the Columban influence after Fr. Nolan ('a good Catholic must not only be personally pure but must also lead others to purity'). At the Institute of Law itself, Art's faculty was a roll-call of mid-century legal power: Dean Jovito R. Salonga (the role model -- Harvard MA + Yale JSD, a combination the rival ivies normally didn't permit, later Senate President); Neptali A. Gonzales (also future Senate President); Edgardo L. Paras and Salvador Esguerra (future SC justices); Arsenio Solidum, Antonio Lucero, Luis B. Reyes, Angel Mojica, Jose Leuterio (future Court of Appeals justices); and the young Ivy League returnees Juan Ponce Enrile, Manuel Montecillo, Reynaldo Alejandro, Augusto Caesar Espiritu. Salonga monitored Art's grades 'like a father' and admonished, 'I don't care if you are the president of the Central Student Council of FEU and head of the National Union of Students of the Philippines. Your priority as a law student should be your academics.' Two near-death medical episodes punctuate the law years: in 1958, leading the NUS Naga seminar against communism, Art climbed Mt. Mayon and his appendix burst -- misdiagnosed at St. Mary's Maternity Clinic in Tabaco, Albay (the only available clinic, gendered or not) as indigestion, treated with castor oil and then morphine, he slept 24 hours and was dancing that evening; two weeks later, surgery at FEU Hospital under Dr. Ricardo Alfonso, who told him 'Your appendix ruptured two weeks ago. The Lord must love you,' the rupture's atypical posterior-left location and the surrounding fat tissue having contained the toxins long enough for surgical intervention. He paid only the six-peso student fee he had previously opposed as student leader ('You win, Doctor'). A sophomore-year scholarship loss due to over-extended student-leader time was rescued by Uncle Ben's 150 pesos with strict admonition; the scholarship was recovered the next semester. The Marulas, Polo, Bulacan house Art, his brother Nardo and uncle Frankie built with their bare hands became Agang's last home; she succumbed to tuberculosis during Art's fourth year, Nardo administering streptomycin shots when funds allowed. The chapter culminates in the 1960 bar examinations: hospitalized at FEU Hospital on the first Sunday with severe gastroenteritis and flu, discouraged and ready to quit, Art was reached by Salonga whose hand-shake-longer-than-usual was accompanied by 'Art, take the exam; you will get well.' He walked over flooded R. Papa Street to the University of the East and finished #6 -- Bachelor of Laws cum laude -- with Salonga later writing in the 2007 Foreword to *A Test of Courage* that 'Art might have placed first or second if he had not been sick.' Art dropped the 'Jr.' from his name upon admission to the Bar 'to honor him and his last wish ... so that he would truly live in me.'
