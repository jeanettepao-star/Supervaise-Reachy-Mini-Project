# Chapter 6: The Lawyer

Chapter 6 covers Art's two-year junior-associate period at Dean Jovito R. Salonga's law firm (1961-1963), opening with the upward turn -- cum laude graduation, #6 bar finish, April 1961 marriage to Leni -- and the aborted Yale dream. Salonga had recommended Art for a Yale Law School scholarship (board, lodging, and tuition awarded), but the Fulbright/Smith-Mundt travel grant from the US Embassy was denied because of Art's activist record as student leader. 'A crushing blow,' the chapter calls it -- financial-disadvantage helplessness striking again. Salonga's consolation offer became the actual gift: 'I will thus teach you what I learned from the University of the Philippines, Yale and Harvard.' Art joined Salonga, Ordoñez, Sicat and Associates as junior associate and worked alongside the future leadership of the Republic -- Sedfrey A. Ordonez (Cory's Secretary of Justice in 1987), Pedro L. Yap (Chief Justice of the Supreme Court 1988), Neptali A. Gonzales (Senate President 1992-93, 1995-96, 1998), and Salonga himself (Senate President 1987-1992). Salonga's Harvard MA + Yale JSD -- a combination the two rival ivies normally did not permit -- let him distinguish for Art the case-method orientation of Harvard from the policy-oriented approach of Yale (a lawyer who 'should go beyond' rules and doctrines and 'do what would serve the interests of the nation and the world community'); Art reports gaining 'the best of four educational worlds: FEU, UP, Harvard and Yale.' Beyond legal technique, Salonga gave Art a character architecture: integrity, prudence, fairness, temperance -- the 'life-moving virtues' -- and an ethic of life in which the Cosmopolitan Church layman 'did not smoke or drink or gamble,' lived 'simple, almost ascetic,' indifferent to titles and honors. The chapter quotes extensively from Art's March 14, 2016 eulogy, including the distilled capsule about 'the things that money cannot buy, like excellence, integrity, honor, dignity, and a reverential regard and love for God.' Two courtroom encounters give the chapter its narrative spine. In the first, Salonga fell ill at dawn and telephoned Art to cover a Court of First Instance hearing under Judge Angel H. Mojica (Art's former FEU Law professor) -- against the legendary Atty. Jose W. Diokno, 'Father of Human Rights Advocacy in the Philippines,' 1944 bar topnotcher (the war had interrupted his studies, and the Supreme Court had granted him qualifying-test permission), Secretary of Justice in 1962, Senator in 1963. Salonga had given his word there would be no postponement, so Art appeared alone and unprepared. Diokno, observing the young *compañero*, then did the unexpected: 'in fairness to my young *compañero* and knowing that Atty. Salonga would not tell a lie about his health, I myself am now moving to postpone the hearing.' Art was stunned, relieved, grateful -- Diokno 'now loomed even higher in his esteem,' the chapter recording the lesson: 'He did not belittle me but instead gave me respect.' The second encounter is structural rather than singular: every time Salonga faced Atty. Claudio Teehankee (Ateneo summa 1938 prep, summa 1940 LL.B, 1940 bar topnotcher, future 16th Chief Justice) he would warn his junior, 'You better prepare our trial brief very well because we are going to be up against one of the best lawyers in town.' Art hero-worshipped Teehankee -- 'a social engineer who saw the law as an instrument to build a just society,' who 'reached for the stars to render justice via the all-encompassing rule of law,' stood for liberty 'at the cost of his personal safety and that of his family,' and pioneered the libertarian philosophy in the Philippines. The chapter concludes with a distilled synthesis of these three lineages: 'Independence, integrity, industry and intelligence would become the hallmarks of his judicial career' -- not Art's invention but Salonga's incorruptibility, Diokno's fairness-even-against-self-interest, and Teehankee's libertarian courage combined into the personal-character architecture that CJP would carry to the Bench.

### Anecdote 2

Salonga's consolation offer: 'I will thus teach you what I learned from the University of the Philippines, Yale and Harvard' -- Art accepting and gaining 'the best of four educational worlds'

### Anecdote 3

The Salonga character portrait: Cosmopolitan Church layman, did not smoke, drink, or gamble, 'simple, almost ascetic life,' indifferent to wealth, power, titles, honors

### Anecdote 4

Salonga's predawn phone call: 'I'm unwell, can you cover for me at the hearing today? But do not ask for a postponement -- I gave the judge my word.' Art appearing alone at Mojica's Court of First Instance against the legendary Diokno

### Anecdote 5

The Mojica-Diokno-Art exchange: 'Young man, are you ready to continue the cross-examination of the plaintiff?' -- Art declaring readiness, then explaining Salonga's instructions

### Anecdote 6

Diokno's unexpected response: 'Your Honor, it is true we agreed not to defer today's hearing but in fairness to my young *compañero* and knowing that Atty. Salonga would not tell a lie about his health, I myself am now moving to postpone the hearing'

### Anecdote 7

Art's reaction: 'No objections, your honor' -- 'stunned, relieved and grateful,' Diokno 'now loomed even higher in his esteem'

### Anecdote 8

Salonga's standing warning before Teehankee cases: 'You better prepare our trial brief very well because we are going to be up against one of the best lawyers in town'

### Anecdote 9

Teehankee biographical detail: Ateneo summa in prep 1938, LL.B summa 1940, bar topnotcher 1940 -- 'reached for the stars to render justice via the all-encompassing rule of law'

### Anecdote 10

March 14, 2016 eulogy: 'I can never adequately acknowledge the pervading influence of former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga in my life' -- Art's distillation of nearly six decades of mentorship
