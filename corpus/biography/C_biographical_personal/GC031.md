# Chapter 11: Associate Justice

Chapter 11 chronicles Art's first decade on the Supreme Court (1995-2005) as Associate Justice, opening with the neo-classical Padre Faura building designed by Filipino architect Antonio Toledo under Daniel Burnham's 1905 Manila plan -- its four pillars, its bronze Arellano and Abad Santos statues, and its court seal with scales of justice resting on the Ten Commandments tablets symbolizing 'the origins of the Philippine legal system which draws from Judeo-Christian ethos.' Art entered with 'seemingly boundless energy,' becoming by tenure-end 'the most prolific writer in the history of the Supreme Court' -- coherent full-length decisions, minute resolutions, no case left undecided, all 'mindful to resolve each dispute with fairness and equitability.' Three signature lines of work define the chapter. First, the death-penalty crusade: separate opinions in People v. Esparas and Libed (Aug 20, 1996; 260 SCRA 552) and People v. Echegaray (G.R. No. 117472, Feb 7, 1997) argued Republic Act 7659 unconstitutional and in direct opposition to the sixth commandment, citing Pope John Paul II's Evangelium Vitae that capital punishment is justifiable only in cases 'very rare, if not practically nonexistent.' Even Chief Justice Narvasa's impeachment threat could not dissuade Art, who held that 'his ascent to the highest court of the land was a stroke of the Divine' and he was 'beholden to no one else but God.' Though outvoted on constitutionality, Art produced a procedural workaround -- new jurisprudence holding that imprisonment, not death, was the recourse for cases under review -- and only when President Arroyo signed Republic Act 9346 on June 24, 2006 did 'Art rest his case.' Second, the Family Code Article 36 guidelines: as ponente of Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina (G.R. No. 108763, Feb 13, 1997), tasked by the Office of the Solicitor General with standardizing the interpretation of 'psychological incapacity' as a nullity ground, Art invited Archbishop Oscar V. Cruz -- head of the National Appellate Matrimonial Tribunal of the Philippine Church -- as Amicus Curiae, not from devotional preference but because the concept itself was sourced from Canon Law. Building on Santos v. Court of Appeals (Jan 1995)'s baseline of 'gravity, juridical antecedence and incurability,' Art crafted eight guidelines that drew complaints of strictness but which he defended on the explicit textual ground that 'psychological incapacity as a ground to nullify a marriage was copied by the Family Code from Canon Law.' Third, the church-state threading: Art held uncompromisingly to separation of church and state -- neutrality among denominations, no public funds for any church group, no establishment -- while insisting that 'there is no separation of the state from God,' citing the Constitution's preamble ('We, the sovereign Filipino people, imploring the aid of Almighty God...') and the fact that both chambers of Congress invoked the divine before sessions. Concretely, the doctrine produced the Centennial Prayer for the Courts that Art composed with Chief Justice Davide for the Supreme Court's 100th anniversary -- optional and respectful of non-believing justices, but adopted by a 'significant number.' The chapter closes by introducing Davide as Art's 'praying-magistrate' counterpart -- a 3:30 AM Bible reader, possessed of 'integrity, wisdom, humility, patriotism, keen sense of righteousness' -- and foreshadowing the January 2001 Estrada impeachment crisis they would jointly navigate 'guided by the Constitution and Bible readings,' setting up Chapter 12.

## Notable Anecdotes

### Anecdote 1

Art entering the SC building for the first time: the four pillars, the Arellano and Abad Santos statues, the scales of justice on Ten Commandments tablets -- tenets he 'embraced all these even more closely in his heart as he embarked on his mission to serve God in the Supreme Court'

### Anecdote 2

Reputation by tenure-end: 'authored the most number of decisions, distinguishing him as the most prolific writer in the history of the Supreme Court'

### Anecdote 3

Echegaray separate opinion (Feb 7, 1997): the death sentence was for raping a ten-year-old stepdaughter -- and Art still wrote in opposition, invoking the sixth commandment and the Pope's Evangelium Vitae

### Anecdote 4

Narvasa's impeachment threat -- Art persists, citing divine commission, and 'after all, he believed that his ascent to the highest court of the land was a stroke of the Divine'

### Anecdote 5

Procedural workaround: 'though the Court upheld the Death Penalty Law, new jurisprudence stipulated that imprisonment and not the death penalty was the recourse for cases up for review'

### Anecdote 6

The case 'rested' only when President Arroyo signed RA 9346 on June 24, 2006

### Anecdote 7

Molina case (Feb 13, 1997): Art invites Archbishop Oscar V. Cruz as Amicus Curiae -- 'not from devotional preference but because the concept of psychological incapacity was sourced from the ecclesiastical law'

### Anecdote 8

Eight guidelines crafted by Art -- criticized as strict -- defended on the explicit textual ground that the Family Code copied the concept from Canon Law

### Anecdote 9

Composition of the Centennial Prayer for the Courts with Chief Justice Davide for the SC 100th anniversary -- optional, not mandatory, but adopted by a 'significant number' of justices and judges

### Anecdote 10

Davide as a 3:30 AM Bible reader -- Art's praying-magistrate counterpart and Chapter 12 collaborator

### Anecdote 11

Art's introduction of Davide at a Kilosbayan Forum: a man 'born with' moral courage who 'thrived on it,' 'pure of heart and clear of mind, possessed of integrity beyond suspicion and with no skeletons to hide'
