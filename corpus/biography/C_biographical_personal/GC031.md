# Chapter 11: Associate Justice

\(Chapter 11\)     

Associate Justice

			

*“Be of good courage and *

*  let us play the man for our people *

*                           and for the cities of our God\.” *

*2 Samuel 10:12*

The offices of the Supreme Court of the Philippines, located on Padre Faura Street in Ermita, Manila, was designed by Filipino architect Antonio Toledo\. He adhered to the 1905 Burnham Plan of Manila conceptualized by Daniel Burnham, who had intended it to be part of the University of the Philippines Manila\. Upon the invitation of the new colonial administration, urban designer and architect Burnham from Chicago was invited to the Philippines to draw up a master plan for modernizing the country’s capital\. Together with his associate Pierce Anderson, he included proposals for improvements, mapping out a detailed scheme with a nod to the Beaux\-Arts movement and the antique simplicity of neo\-classical architecture of which the prominent architect was a known proponent\.

The neo\-classical building in the Supreme Court complex has four towering pillars representing the hierarchy of courts in the Philippine Judicial system\. Flanking the entrance are the bronze figures of Chief Justices Cayetano Arellano and José Abad Santos\. Inside, the Court’s seal prominently occupies the center of the main lobby\. Each element in the logo stands for the foundational values and principles of the highest court of the land\. The scales of justice, which universally represent balance and fairness when administering the law, correspond to the supreme duty of providing fair and decisive judgments\. Beneath the scales of justice are two tablets on which are supposedly inscribed the Ten Commandments given to Moses on Mount Sinai\. These symbols reflect the origins of the Philippine legal system which draws from Judeo\-Christian ethos\. The principle of Christian ethics reflects the Christ’s kindness, mercy and compassion and God’s love for all men\. A crown of laurel leaves encircling the Philippine flag represents the honor of upholding the rule of law for country and countrymen\. 

 The tenets were not apart from Art’s own credo, and he embraced all these even more closely in his heart as he embarked on his mission to serve God in the Supreme Court\.

__Death to the Death Penalty__

__	__The newest associate justice of the Supreme Court plunged into the task of unclogging court dockets\. His determination to serve the cause of justice was matched by seemingly boundless energy as he addressed himself to the task of writing decisions assigned to him\. He wrote coherent, intelligible, full\-length decisions and minute resolutions and left no case on his desk undecided\. By the end of his tenure that spanned more than a decade, Art had authored the most number of decisions, distinguishing him as the most prolific writer in the history of the Supreme Court\. 

Even with the volumes of legal paperwork penned, he was mindful to resolve each dispute with fairness and equitability\. Foremost in Art’s work was the desire to align himself with the mind of God, as was apparent in his pro\-poor opinions\.  His opposition to the death penalty further underscored such resolve to adhere to the Divine statutes\. This he demonstrated vigorously in the case of Leo Echagaray, who had been charged with raping his ten\-year\-old stepdaughter\. The accused was meted out the death sentence, to be administered through lethal injection\. 

	There was no doubt in Art’s mind that death was not the just course, being in direct opposition to the sixth commandment “Thou shall not kill\.” He stood in opposition to the Death Penalty Law \(RA 7659\), arguing that it was unconstitutional\. He quoted Pope John Paul II in his encyclical *Evangelium Vitae* \(A Hymn to Human Life\): “\[P\]unishment must be carefully evaluated and decided upon, and ought not to go to the extreme of executing the offender except in cases of absolute necessity; in other words, when it may otherwise not be possible to defend society \. \. \. \(which is\) very rare, if not practically nonexistent\.” \(Separate Opinion in *People v\. Echegaray*, G\.R\. No\. 117472, February 7, 1997\)

	He had earlier penned a Separate Opinion in *People v\. Esparas and Libed *\(G\.R\. No\. 12003, August 20, 1996, 260 SCRA 552\) underscoring the primacy of human life\. He wrote: “Life is too precious to be settled by legalisms, however exalted\. I believe that this court cannot abandon its sacred duty to God and country to see to it that a lower court judgment that takes away life is ERROR\-FREE and can stand THE MOST SEARCHING SCRUTINY\.” 

	The position he had taken was not shared by the majority of his colleagues\. Art was repeatedly outvoted when his fellow justices weighed in on the constitutionality of the death penalty\. Still, he remained relentless to the point that then Chief Justice Andres R\. Narvasa took him to task, raising the specter of his impeachment for his crusade against capital punishment even for heinous crimes\. The possible ousting or loss of position could not dissuade him\. After all, he believed that his ascent to the highest court of the land was a stroke of the Divine\. He was beholden to no one else but God who had commanded that nobody has the right to take another’s life\. 

	His unrelenting contentions put him at odds with his colleagues who held that sufficient time had been given to dissents\. The continuous discussion on the death penalty was, to their mind, undermining the judicial process and confusing the lower courts\. He stood his ground and though the Court upheld the Death Penalty Law, new jurisprudence stipulated that imprisonment and not the death penalty was the recourse for cases up for review\. It was only when then President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo abolished the death penalty by repealing Republic Act 9346 on June 24, 2006, that Art rested his case\.

__Family Code Provision__

__	__Art adhered to the independence of Church and State\. He believed that these institutions, while separate and apart from each other, “walk together in synodal cadence towards the same goal of protecting and cherishing marriage and the family as the inviolable base of the nation\.”\(*Republic v\. Court of Appeals and Molina,* G\.R\. No\. 108763, February 13, 1997\)

	When President Corazon C\. Aquino approved the Family Code of the Philippines, the declaration of nullity of a marriage due to the “psychological incapacity” of one of the spouses, was included in Article 36\. This inclusion effectively gave the courts the power to void a marriage on the grounds of a mental condition existing when the marriage was contracted\. The law, which took effect on August 3, 1988, stated: “A marriage contracted by any party who, at the time of the celebration, was psychologically incapacitated to comply with the essential obligations of marriage shall likewise be void even if such incapacity becomes manifest only after the solemnization” of the union\.  This provision became subject to interpretation and application by law practitioners and the judiciary\. The Office of the Solicitor General sought to standardize the application and required clear guidelines, a task that fell on Art as the decision writer in *Republic v\. Molina* \(Feb\. 13, 1997\)\.

 		He invited Archbishop Oscar V\. Cruz as an Amicus Curiae or a friend of the Court to provide a better understanding of “psychological incapacity\.” It would have seemed that his grounding in faith motivated such an action\.  But in truth, referencing Canon Law was academic and pertinent to the process, since the concept of “psychological incapacity” was sourced from the ecclesiastical law laid down by papal pronouncement\. Archbishop Oscar Cruz was head of the National Appellate Matrimonial Tribunal of the Philippine Church\. The prelate was asked to explain the meaning of “psychological incapacity” to assist the court in creating the guidelines sought for a consistent and uniform application of the law\.

 	The Supreme Court’s earliest ruling on the matter \(*Santos v\. Court of Appeals,* January 1995\) declared that “psychological incapacity should refer to no less than a mental \(not physical\) incapacity, to confine \[its meaning\] to the most serious cases of personality disorders clearly demonstrative of an utter insensitivity or inability to give meaning and significance to the marriage\.” The condition “must be characterized by gravity, juridical antecedence and incurability\.”

	Even as he had prayed for guidance, his responsibility as a justice of the Supreme Court was clear in his mind\.  Following the considerations and recommendations of Archbishop Cruz, Art crafted eight guidelines on the application of the law\. He included the parameters that Canon Law required\. 

	While there were criticisms that the guidelines were too strict, Art remained unperturbed\. He maintained that “psychological incapacity as a ground to nullify a marriage was copied by the Family Code from Canon Law\. It had no prior constitutional, statutory or jurisprudential precedent\.” 

__Separation of Church and State__

__	__He was not troubled by the aspersions on the sternness of the guidelines or the considerable weight that his ecclesiastical leanings might have been given\. He was uncompromising in upholding the separation of church and state\. Art held that the doctrine did not prohibit the clergy and the laity from advocating pro\-Christian tenets and opposing anti\-Christian policies and actions\. He maintained that separation of church and state meant that the government could not interfere in the church, and the church could not interfere in the affairs of the state\. The most fundamental principle holds that the state remains neutral, not favoring any religious denomination\. No public funds shall be appropriated to any church group, and no tenet or dogma shall be favored over the others\.

	While there is separation of church and state, there is no separation of the state from God, he said\. Art explained that “the Philippines is theist, not atheist, not even agnostic\. In fact, it is monotheistic; it worships one God\. That is why our Constitution begins with this significant phrase: ‘We, the sovereign Filipino people, imploring the aid of Almighty God …\.’” He pointed to the fact that both the upper and the lower chambers of Congress invoked the divine before beginning their sessions\. 

	When it was appropriate to bring prayer into the august halls, he did\. During the 100th anniversary of the Supreme Court, Art was designated as chair of the executive committee, responsible for overseeing over a hundred activities in the year\-long celebration\. Together with the active participation of Chief Justice Hilario Davide, he composed the “Centennial Prayer for the Courts\.” Sessions commenced with an invocation of God’s blessings\. This public expression of faith to begin sessions in all courts, including the Supreme Court, was not mandatory\. It was an option given out of respect for those justices and judges who did not believe in God or in prayer\. There was, however, a significant number who began with this prayer:

	“Almighty God, we stand in your holy presence as our supreme judge\. We humbly beseech you to bless and inspire us so that what we think, say and do will be in accordance with Your holy will\.

	“Enlighten our minds, strengthen our spirit, and fill our hearts with fraternal love, wisdom and understanding, so that we can be effective channels of truth, justice, and peace\. In our proceedings today, guide us in the path of righteousness for the fulfillment of your greater glory\. Amen\.”

__Praying Magistrates__

__	__Art was not alone in taking his cue from God\. Chief Justice Hilario G\. Davide was known to begin his day reading the Bible\. Art esteemed him for his integrity, wisdom, humility, patriotism and keen sense of righteousness\. He lauded the Chief Justice for his meticulous work accomplished with fastidious analysis and thoroughness\. 

	Art admired the moral courage of Chief Justice Davide\. During the introduction of his colleague at a Kilosbayan Forum, he described the senior magistrate as a man with abundant moral courage who “was born with it and thrived on it\. To have moral courage means not only theoretical bravery in battle but, *a priori,* a purity of spirit and a clarity of conscience…\. He is pure of heart and clear of mind, possessed of integrity beyond suspicion and with no skeletons to hide…”

	Unknown to him, he and Chief Justice Davide would figure in averting a potential crisis, guided by the Constitution and Bible readings\.  Events in January 2001 were escalating into a national turmoil\. Joseph Ejercito Estrada was the sitting president of the country\. The head of state was allegedly misusing public funds for personal gain, accepting bribes and extending preferential accommodations benefitting his circle of friends and associates\. Foreign investors were losing confidence in the government amid the slew of corruption and incompetence allegations\. In less than two years, the economic gains of the previous administration were dissipating, while the crescendo of public dissatisfaction was rising\. 

	The Philippine House of Representatives passed a resolution of complaint against Mr\. Estrada followed by an impeachment trial in the Upper House in November 2000\. Following the stipulations in the Philippine Constitution, Chief Justice Davide presided over the highly publicized proceedings in the Senate\. His adherence to judicial protocols and his efforts to maintain order significantly contributed to the integrity of a trial marked by political maneuverings and procedural issues\. However, when Estrada allies walked out of the hearing and thus no verdict was reached, the public took to the streets in protest\. The surge of citizens who spontaneously gathered on EDSA augured a repeat of the People Power Revolution of 1986\. It was a time of reckoning in which Art and the chief justice would act upon prompting from the Word of God\.

## Summary

Chapter 11 chronicles Art's first decade on the Supreme Court (1995-2005) as Associate Justice, opening with the neo-classical Padre Faura building designed by Filipino architect Antonio Toledo under Daniel Burnham's 1905 Manila plan -- its four pillars, its bronze Arellano and Abad Santos statues, and its court seal with scales of justice resting on the Ten Commandments tablets symbolizing 'the origins of the Philippine legal system which draws from Judeo-Christian ethos.' Art entered with 'seemingly boundless energy,' becoming by tenure-end 'the most prolific writer in the history of the Supreme Court' -- coherent full-length decisions, minute resolutions, no case left undecided, all 'mindful to resolve each dispute with fairness and equitability.' Three signature lines of work define the chapter. First, the death-penalty crusade: separate opinions in People v. Esparas and Libed (Aug 20, 1996; 260 SCRA 552) and People v. Echegaray (G.R. No. 117472, Feb 7, 1997) argued Republic Act 7659 unconstitutional and in direct opposition to the sixth commandment, citing Pope John Paul II's Evangelium Vitae that capital punishment is justifiable only in cases 'very rare, if not practically nonexistent.' Even Chief Justice Narvasa's impeachment threat could not dissuade Art, who held that 'his ascent to the highest court of the land was a stroke of the Divine' and he was 'beholden to no one else but God.' Though outvoted on constitutionality, Art produced a procedural workaround -- new jurisprudence holding that imprisonment, not death, was the recourse for cases under review -- and only when President Arroyo signed Republic Act 9346 on June 24, 2006 did 'Art rest his case.' Second, the Family Code Article 36 guidelines: as ponente of Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina (G.R. No. 108763, Feb 13, 1997), tasked by the Office of the Solicitor General with standardizing the interpretation of 'psychological incapacity' as a nullity ground, Art invited Archbishop Oscar V. Cruz -- head of the National Appellate Matrimonial Tribunal of the Philippine Church -- as Amicus Curiae, not from devotional preference but because the concept itself was sourced from Canon Law. Building on Santos v. Court of Appeals (Jan 1995)'s baseline of 'gravity, juridical antecedence and incurability,' Art crafted eight guidelines that drew complaints of strictness but which he defended on the explicit textual ground that 'psychological incapacity as a ground to nullify a marriage was copied by the Family Code from Canon Law.' Third, the church-state threading: Art held uncompromisingly to separation of church and state -- neutrality among denominations, no public funds for any church group, no establishment -- while insisting that 'there is no separation of the state from God,' citing the Constitution's preamble ('We, the sovereign Filipino people, imploring the aid of Almighty God...') and the fact that both chambers of Congress invoked the divine before sessions. Concretely, the doctrine produced the Centennial Prayer for the Courts that Art composed with Chief Justice Davide for the Supreme Court's 100th anniversary -- optional and respectful of non-believing justices, but adopted by a 'significant number.' The chapter closes by introducing Davide as Art's 'praying-magistrate' counterpart -- a 3:30 AM Bible reader, possessed of 'integrity, wisdom, humility, patriotism, keen sense of righteousness' -- and foreshadowing the January 2001 Estrada impeachment crisis they would jointly navigate 'guided by the Constitution and Bible readings,' setting up Chapter 12.
