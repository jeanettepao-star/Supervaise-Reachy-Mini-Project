# Chapter 3: The Rise of the Student Leader

__               CHAPTER 3__

*Do not let anyone look down upon you because you are young\. *

*                                                                                   *__*1 Timothy 4:12 *__

__The Rise of the Student Leader __

The Far Eastern University was by no means an inferior choice\. FEU had previously been the Far Eastern College, which was founded in 1919\. Initially offering courses in liberal arts, it expanded with the establishment of the Institute of Accountancy that later became the Institute of Accounts, Business and Finance \(IABF\)\. The learning institution became a university when the liberal arts college merged with the IABF\. Additional course offerings in the coeducational learning institution expanded the curriculum and over the years the school in Sampaloc held its own academically among the leading universities in the Philippines\. President Manuel L\. Quezon was quoted as saying that FEU was “the best non\-sectarian institution in the country\.” Ironically, it was here that Dayunior’s spiritual awakening began\. 

Initially the young man was unhappy about enrolling in a school he had not chosen\. The first day at FEU however brought a change of heart\. He stepped in the classroom and felt immediately challenged by the

unusually strict and demanding professors\. His classes were stimulating and intellectually challenging\. The academic environment invigorated him and the student quickly immersed himself in the pursuit of scholastic achievements\. Whatever initial resentments he had over the inability to enlist in the school of his choice were dispelled as satisfaction from the overall learning experience overtook any misgivings\. 

His early accomplishments led him to the realization that being in FEU did not make him less than his counterparts from the more prominent learning institutions\. “I was an equal of the UP, the Ateneo or the La Salle student,” he would later reflect\. “I could, in fact, compare and compete favorably with the best and the brightest from any school in the country and in the world\.” 

__Marx and the Bible Salesman __

Dayunior was on a scholarship granted by FEU and he studied hard to keep his grant\. Educational subsidy notwithstanding, the pre\-law student continued to earn his keep by selling bibles to his professors and textbooks to classmates\. He applied for work in a company that sold pricey editions of the Douay\-Rheims Bible\. Each copy of the Latin Vulgate translation cost 70 pesos which in 2021 is estimated to be equivalent to 20,000 pesos\.

An enterprising student who was being offered a full\-time position in the company, he opted instead to be a part\-time seller of the leather\-bound, gilded holy book\. He strategically targeted a few people of means, approaching the likes of the FEU president, the FEU treasurer and the chairman of the board of trustees of FEU\. He was an astute salesman who also offered the same expensive edition to some of his professors who were known to be devout, practicing Catholics\. From every Bible sold, Dayunior generated pocket money for a whole month\. Commissions from the sales also allowed him to buy his own textbooks which cost 10 pesos each\. Like a sponge absorbing learnings from readings and educators, he soaked in the intellectual richness afforded by the academe\. 

A few months into his first semester as a freshman, any remaining thoughts of pursuing other than a law degree were cancelled out by the passing of his father\. Temi had been working two jobs with little time to rest, and the physical exertion took a toll on his health\. Dayunior was walking home from FEU on the bright morning of February 17, 1955, when he caught sight of his father being carried down from the second floor apartment in Cataluña to a waiting taxi\. The son hurriedly jumped into the backseat and joined the others to the emergency room of the UST Hospital\. They sat in the medical treatment facility anxiously waiting for one of the ER staff to attend to them\. Sadly it took five hours before anyone assessed Temi who was obviously breathing with difficulty\. He was having an asthma attack, wheezing and gasping for air as nurses and doctors bypassed him\. Dayunior held his father as the bronchospasms accelerated\. He felt helpless and desperate as Temi’s pulse throbbed slower and slower, the pulsation growing fainter at the neck and temple till the heart stopped beating altogether\. When the dean of the UST College of Medicine finally came, Temi was no longer breathing\. He was 54 years old when he expired\. 

Grief and bitterness surged in Dayunior’s heart\. The loss of his father, incomprehensible sadness and an anguished helplessness gnawed at him, stoking his anger over their poverty\. He felt discriminated against in the ER\. It was a busy place with patients coming in succession with different ailments and various conditions\. Dayunior watched as arrivals were attended to by doctors and nurses who bypassed him and Temi even if they had been there ahead\. Once more, the painful realization that being poor relegated them to insignificance\. It seared into his core\. The inequality dealt to the disadvantaged added to his increasing resentment over their station in life\. 

It was discontent that reflected on a wider scale in different parts of the world\. Doctrines developed by Karl Marx in the mid\-19th century had spread across the continents and surfaced in the country\. Marxist

tenets analyzed the economic structure of society and advocated a transformation of the populace and of social consciousness from capitalism to a worker\-driven economic and political system\. It became the foundation of Marxism\-Leninism and Maoism which gave rise to radical labor and socialist movements in western Europe\. Marxist theories also defined communism, which the Soviet Union embraced, to become the first communist government\. Other countries such as China moved towards a society where there was no private property, no state, and every person engaged in the production of goods and services according to individual abilities\. This way of erasing poverty and bringing about economic equality was gradually seeping into the Philippines, particularly into learning institutions in Manila\. It appealed to the poor, struggling student\. The loss of a father could have driven him towards communism, but another father opened him up to another direction\. 

__The Sophomore President __

Temi’s namesake felt honor\-bound to make his father’s wish come true\. He embraced the rigorous life of a working student while still fulfilling responsibilities outside the academic requirements of his course\. An unexpected development added to his widening and agreeable experience of college life\. The year was 1955\. Dayunior had

been recruited to run as the freshman delegate in his first year at the FEU Institute of Arts and Sciences, which would be the equivalent to the College of Liberal Arts\. He won the seat and became a part of the winning bloc\. These blocs were like political parties in the student government\. The freshman representative was immediately thrown into the vortex of extra\-curricular activities and became actively involved in the rich, interactive life of the university\. 

A whole new horizon opened up as he was thrust into student activism and a position of leadership\. It led to a transformation from being timid and shy to becoming bold and confident\. The war and their family circumstances, the angst and the curtailment of childhood privileges had made him reserved\. As a boy, Dayunior already knew he was unlike other children because he did not have the things they owned\. Other than with his cousin Frankie, he kept mostly to himself\. His growing years were filled with self\-doubt and insecurities that remained all through high school\. University life was the game changer\. His high school classmates, who had known him as an introvert, were surprised by the transformation of the once inhibited boy to a self\-assured university man\. 

The academic prerequisites leading up to a law degree required completing two years in the FEU Institute of Arts and Sciences, before four more years in the Institute of Law\. Dayunior proved to be adept at juggling the scholastic demands and his responsibilities as the freshman delegate\. When he completed his pre\-law studies, it was with distinction, graduating as Associate in Arts “With Highest Honors,” equivalent to summa cum laude\. 

His leadership abilities had made him recognizable as a campus figure such that when he moved to the Institute of Law, he was once again recruited to run for the position of freshman delegate to the FEU Central Student Organization \(FEUCSO\)\. Effectively, he was a freshman in the Institute, even if it was his third year in college\. By this time he was no longer called Dayunior, but Art\. 

His capabilities had been noted particularly by Fr\. Michael Nolan,

SSC, the archdiocesan spiritual director of the Student Catholic Action and FEUCSO president Ronaldo Balboa\. Fr\. Nolan, who was also the university chaplain, encouraged Art to contend for the freshman delegate position at the Law Institute\. The Irish priest went so far as to say that thereafter Art could contend for the presidency of FEUCSO, and that he would convince the SCA bloc to support Art’s bid\. 

Balboa, who was then chairman of the Medicine delegation and FEUSO president, also encouraged him to run for the chairmanship of

the Institute of Law\. It was succession planning, as Balboa was in his last year\. He said, “Once you become chairman of the Institute of Law, I can assure you that you will head our bloc in the university and we will support you to get elected president of the FEUCSO\.” 

Art calculated his chances\. His first two years at the Institute of Arts and Sciences had given him a fair grasp of what the position entailed\. If he won as freshman delegate of the Law Institute, he would be eligible to run for chairman of the Law Institute and consequently the FEUCSO presidency\. Emboldened by the encouragement of Fr\. Nolan and Ronald Balboa, he threw his hat into the ring and emerged a winner\. Not only was he elected as freshman delegate of the Institute of Law, but he was also voted treasurer, a post normally held by sophomores and juniors\. The next step was to muster support for his bid for chairmanship of the Law Institute\. 

What he had to contend with were the upperclassmen who were aghast at the temerity of an upstart to challenge the seniors\. It had never happened before that a sophomore would even aspire for the chairmanship\. Traditionally, hierarchy determined that only seniors and sometimes juniors occupied that position\. It was a status quo that Art

would overturn\. He plunged into the campaign systematically and charismatically, going from classroom to classroom to introduce himself\. Art was able to persuade the freshman and sophomore electorate, and a smattering of juniors and seniors, to vote for him\. When the ballots were tallied, he was short by two votes and lost to a senior\. Going by his instincts, he filed an electoral protest and in the recount was declared the winner by five votes\. The incoming sophomore would sit as chairman of the Law Institute\. The next step would be to present himself as a candidate for the FEUCSO presidency\. 

There were seven institutes or colleges and each had five delegates representing the different class years, plus the chairman delegate\. The freshman, sophomore, junior and senior delegates were each elected by their year batch while the chairman delegate was elected by the students of their respective institutes\. The FEUCSO presidency was a battle among the chairmen of the different institutes\. 

The recount had caused Art to lose several days of campaigning for the FEUCSO presidency\. He was not deemed a candidate while the electoral protest was in progress\. Only after he was declared the winner of the chairmanship was he able to launch his bid for the top FEUCSO post\. Meanwhile, the other chairman delegates were already deep into an intense contest that was a macrocosm of national electoral campaigns\.

The FEUCSO was organized like a parliamentary government\. But only the seven chairman delegates were qualified to be nominated and elected president of the entire student council\. However, the other delegates \(freshmen, sophomores, juniors and seniors\) could vote for the president and the other officers of the FEUCSO\. These voters were offered housing with food to keep them from being present on campus prior to election day to prevent them from being “snatched” by the opposing camps\. 

As a member of the majority bloc, Art had good numbers on his side\. When he was finally declared the winner of the Law Institute, he already had Father Nolan in his corner and effectively, the SCA\. The campus\-based organization was committed to the formation of Catholic 

student leaders through whom the founders envisioned transformation of schools, parishes and groups through Christian living\. The SCA was founded in 1936 and nurtured by Fr\. Edward McCarthy during the early

years but it was in the late ’50s to the ‘60s that it became invigorated as a force under Fr\. Michael Nolan, who took an active role in safeguarding the faith and spiritual well\-being of the students\. While the SCA was a minority group, its support would add to the numbers needed to install him as student body president\. He already had the support of “Rocky Balboa” and his bloc, plus his own Law bloc\. 

Art’s strongest opponent was the chairman delegate of the Institute of Accounts or the College of Commerce who already had a sizeable number of supporters\. He expected the SCA’s support to clinch the presidency\. The 10 SCA delegates, however, stood full square behind Art, creating the upset that was nothing less than astounding\. Never in the school annals had there been a student body president who was only a second year law student\. He had made history\. 

__Leading the First Student Walk\-Out __

Art’s feverish pace during the campaign did not let up after his victory\. He spent practically whole days at the FEU Central Student Organization office attending to student affairs\. The headquarters occupied the entire second floor of a two\-storey building built by the owners of the university for the exclusive use of the student council on the second floor and the *FEU Advocate, *the weekly student newspaper, on the first floor\. The second floor was divided into three rooms\. One

served as the reception room, another was designated for the president, and the largest was the conference room\. Like all other presidents before him, Art was given a monthly allowance of P50\.00 which was a sizable amount, considering that the tuition fee then was P130\.00 per semester\. 

There were a lot of expectations of the young president added to the pressure that went with the position\. He plunged into the groove of weekly meetings with the student council delegates, attended activities of the Central Student Organization, and worked to promote the rights and the well\-being of the students\. These included developing programs and activities within and outside the university\. Two main issues confronting the students then were communist infiltration of the campus and government interference\. 

Communism was on the rise in the Philippines with Jose Ma\. Sison and Satur Ocampo eventually leading the extreme left\. When President Carlos P\. Garcia came into office in 1957, a congressional investigation exposed communist infiltration in leading student organizations then, particularly the Student Council Association of the Philippines \(SCAP\) and the Conference Delegates Association \(CONDA\)\. Their leaders were wooed with trips to communist countries where they were accorded special treatment while being indoctrinated\. It was part of the

strategy of the communists to bring young leaders into their fold and thereby gain access to student populations\. 

President Garcia had strongly opposed communism even before assuming the presidency\. During his term, he signed the bill outlawing the Communist Party of the Philippines\. Garcia sought to counter the communist efforts by constituting the Consultative Council of Students composed of student council presidents from selected schools\. The aggrupation of student heads at the behest of the chief of state was aligned with measures to counterbalance seeping communist activity on the campuses\. Members of the council received perks like travels abroad to different countries with doles and other benefits\. The expected reciprocity was further rewarded with partisan political favors\. This arrangement did not bode well for student autonomy\. Art deemed that the independence of the students would be compromised by this matter of governmental overreach\. It was a burning issue together with communist penetration of the students that faced Art as FEUCSO president\. 

“I later learned that Luis Taruc, the leader of the rebels in Central

Luzon, had been my father’s friend,” Art intimated\. “Taruc noted my student activities and wrote me several encouraging letters from his hiding place in the boondocks\. I was most fortunate that when I was

elected president of the FEU Central Student Organization, Fr\. Nolan was already my spiritual adviser\. Otherwise, given my reformist streak, I could have joined the extremist groups and who knows where I would be now,” Art reflected in hindsight\. 

He found guidance and support from Fr\. Nolan\. The Columban priest and the SCA bloc had delivered the swing votes and Art recognized his debt of gratitude to them\. He said, “In my mind, I was no longer the leader of the dominant group\. We were now a hybrid group\. I became friendly with the SCA bloc and began to veer towards Fr\. Nolan\.” 

Fr\. Nolan figured largely in this chapter of Art’s life\. After Art won the elections, the Columban priest provided strong support to the new FEUCSO president to help him address the looming issues of the times\. Art frequented the SCA office and on those occasions, a plan of action evolved to counter the worrisome menaces\. 

FEU was a traditional leader in SCAP and CONDA, owing to the large student population of the university and the support from its administration\. When Art was offered the position of Vice\-President for International Affairs of SCAP, it was intended to simmer down his dissent from the communist and partisan political activities of these

organizations\. Also in the agenda of these groups was to enlist Catholic schools to join them through the FEU connection\. The SCA had gained a foothold in FEU and besides St\. Paul’s College and Sta\. Isabel College, there were no other schools run by religious orders that participated in SCAP and CONDA\. 

Accepting the offer would have given Art the opportunity to travel to China and Russia, Moscow, in particular\. All those who previously held the post had been invited\. Fr\. Nolan felt the time was right to break away from these two major groups\. Having gained Art’s confidence, the priest conveyed the idea of dissociating from both SCAP and CONDA, and organizing a new one\. The priest was bullish about the prospect of establishing a student group independent of government maneuverings and free from Marxist influence\. Fr\. Nolan’s SCA counterpart in the University of the Philippines, Fr\. John Delaney, SJ, had also scored a victory over the dominant bloc in the state university\. Coming together with UPSCA, Fr\. Nolan expanded alliances with other Catholic schools, enjoining them to leave the two biggest organizations that were suspected to be communist fronts\. 

Art’s respect and fondness for Fr\. Nolan, as well as his debt of gratitude to the 10 SCA delegates who had clinched the win for him factored into his decision to lead the charge in bolting from SCAP and

CONDA\. During a Students Council Association of the Philippines convention, he protested government’s interference, arguing that students should be free to criticize the establishment and not be subjugated as an adjunct of the government\. Addressing the concern of communist infiltration, he denounced the trips to Peking, as Beijing was then known, as well as the trips to Moscow, and rejected the vice presidential post offered to him\. After expressing his position, Art turned around and led what might have been the first student walkout in a major national organization in the country, with student leaders from UP, UST, San Beda College, St\. Theresa’s College, National University and Sta\. Isabel joining him\. 

Eventually, with the support of Fr\. Nolan, he co\-founded the National Union of Students of the Philippines in 1957, initially composed of 17 of the largest and most prestigious universities and colleges, like UP and FEU\. “Fr\. Nolan egged me on to organize \-\- along with student leaders from the other leading schools like UP, San Beda, and St\. Theresa’s \-\- the National Union of Students of the Philippines \(NUSP\),” Art recalls\. 

“To strengthen NUSP, Fr\. Nolan helped me convince exclusive Catholic schools like Ateneo, La Salle, Maryknoll \(now Miriam\), Holy Spirit, St\. Scholastica’s, University of San Carlos in Cebu City,

University of San Agustin in Iloilo City and others to come out of their sheltered cocoons and join us\.” Art became president of the NUSP\. He stressed in a speech delivered then: “The NUSP was founded to be the 

answer to the decadent state of student leadership in the country\. The Union is pledged to unite and reflect faithfully student opinion on current national issues, to free the student from undue political interference, to fight communist infiltration on the campuses, and to redirect the youth’s thinking and energy towards constructive idealism\.” 

The SCA worked hard to recruit new leaders\. Its efforts paid off and NUSP grew to become the biggest student organization in the country, a distinction that persists to this date\. “It happened because of the inspiration and assistance of the Student Catholic Action,” Art maintains\. 

__Three Prayers __

Fr\. Nolan’s influence over his protégé extended beyond student affairs\. He enlisted the un\-catechized student to join the SCA\. The priest took it upon himself to educate the FEUCSO president on the rudiments of Catholicism\. “I had grown up a Catholic ignoramus until the SCA introduced me to Christ,” Art often said\. “Fr\. Nolan didn’t impose anything\. He didn’t even tell me to go to Mass every Sunday\. He just taught me three prayers: The Our Father, The Hail Mary and

The Glory Be\. And he gave me booklets about Catholicism, not even a book\. He was a nice Irish priest who taught by example more than by words\. A very prayerful person, he would invite me when he had special Masses, simply saying, “Why don’t you attend?” 

Art acknowledges with gratitude the significant role of this priest in his life\. “ I was most fortunate to have been recruited by Fr\. Nolan into the SCA\. I was a rebellious young man, but he expertly channeled my personal rebellion into constructive endeavors, like organizing the National Union of Students\.” 

The NUSP’s activities then were largely secular, limited to the concerns of students such as improving educational standards, how to become better citizens, and how to enlarge educational outlook through co\-curricular pursuits\. 

Art was able to devote three years of his student life to the NUSP, from1957 till he graduated in 1960\. He was vital in organizing, molding and strengthening NUSP during its formative years\. Even after he had graduated and was working as an assistant in the law firm of Dr\. Jovito Salonga, he continued to secure and provide financial assistance by organizing the Institute of Student Affairs, a nonstock, private organization which he set up\. ISA was registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission and functioned like a foundation to assist student organizations\. 

Fifty years later, in 2007, he once again recalled the ideals of the NUSP at its National Student Congress in Cebu City to mark its golden anniversary\. He said in his keynote address: “The NUSP was born as a rebellion against a decadent student milieu that was prostituted and muzzled by the government then…\[it\] was formed out of a vision that the young must be freed from the political apron strings of the old\. We espoused the idea and ideal that the student can think for himself, knows what is best for him and for his future, and thus must be given the freedom to express himself and to pursue his destiny\. It was founded on the proposition that the best in man and in society can be attained only in freedom, dignity, equality and responsibility\.” 

These principles of the NUSP, articulated by the founder, reflected values that formed the bedrock of the man Art was becoming\. The timing of seemingly unfortunate incidents and episodes, failures and losses, challenges and triumphs, configured to shape his character and choices\. While it was not one event or person that accounted for his transformation, the school he had not chosen figured largely in his pivot towards an enlightenment that would eventually determine the course of his life\.

A quarter of a century later he would say as much before the graduating class of his alma mater\. During his commencement address  on April 5, 2002, Art acknowledged that “going to this university proved to be one of the best turns of my entire life,” He wrote, “While FEU prided itself in being nonsectarian, it was during my FEU days when I met persons who, little by little, initially fed my soul that was in search of faith and of my God\.”

## Summary

Chapter 3 covers Art's three-year FEU undergraduate stretch (1955-1958), framed by the chapter's epigraph from 1 Timothy 4:12 ('Do not let anyone look down upon you because you are young'). Initially unhappy at the place he had not chosen, Art discovers stimulating professors and discovers he can compete with UP, Ateneo and La Salle students -- and so begins the accidental triumph of FEU, the institution Quezon called 'the best non-sectarian institution in the country.' He supports himself selling Douay-Rheims Bibles (70 pesos each, ~20,000 in 2021 money) to FEU's president, treasurer and devout-Catholic professors, and reselling textbooks to classmates for free copies. On February 17, 1955, walking home from FEU, he catches sight of his asthmatic father being carried down to a taxi -- and then spends five hours in the UST Hospital ER as nurses and doctors bypass them, until the Dean of the UST College of Medicine arrives too late and Artemio Sr. expires at 54. The bypass in the ER sears into Art's core -- an inequality witnessed that the biography presents as the origin of CJP's later 'last sanctuary of the oppressed' jurisprudence. Grief and rage make communism a real attraction -- Karl Marx's tenets seeping into FEU through Prof. Primo Tongco, Jose Ma. Sison and Satur Ocampo leading the extreme left, Luis Taruc (the Hukbalahap leader who had been Temi's friend) writing Art encouraging letters from his hiding place. But another father redirects him: Father Michael Nolan SSC, the Columban Irish priest and FEU chaplain who was archdiocesan spiritual director of the Student Catholic Action. After Art is recruited as freshman delegate in the Institute of Arts and Sciences (1955) and graduates summa, he moves to the Institute of Law (1957) and is once again recruited -- this time by Fr. Nolan and outgoing FEUCSO president Ronaldo 'Rocky' Balboa -- to contend for freshman delegate and then for the chairmanship of the Law Institute. He wins the chairmanship by a five-vote recount margin (initially short by two), the first sophomore ever to hold it; then, in an unprecedented upset, the 10 SCA delegates' swing votes deliver him the FEUCSO presidency over the College of Commerce chairman. As FEUCSO president he faces two adversaries at once: communist infiltration via SCAP and CONDA (with their Peking and Moscow training trips offered to the VP for International Affairs post he is offered and refuses), and government overreach via Carlos P. Garcia's Consultative Council of Students. He responds with the first major-organization student walkout in Philippine history, leading delegates from UP, UST, San Beda, St. Theresa's, National University and Sta. Isabel out of the SCAP convention; then, with Fr. Nolan and Fr. John Delaney SJ at UPSCA, he co-founds the National Union of Students of the Philippines (NUSP) in 1957 with an initial roster of 17 schools, later persuading Ateneo, La Salle, Maryknoll, Holy Spirit, St. Scholastica's, USC Cebu and USA Iloilo to leave their 'sheltered cocoons' and join. The founding NUSP mission -- to free students from political interference, fight communist infiltration, and redirect youth energy toward 'constructive idealism' -- would be the doctrinal armature of much of Art's later public life. And throughout, Fr. Nolan catechizes the 'Catholic ignoramus' by minimum imposition: three prayers (Our Father, Hail Mary, Glory Be), booklets not books, never demanding Mass attendance, 'taught by example more than by words.' Art will say at FEU's commencement in 2002 that the place he had not chosen 'proved to be one of the best turns of my entire life.'
