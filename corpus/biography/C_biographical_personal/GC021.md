# Chapter 1: Early Years

__Prologue__

__*Dusk was setting in, extending shadows before nightfall\. A man looked at the sky, quietly watching streaks of light fade into the darkness\. He sat in a room full of memorabilia, souvenirs from travel, books he had written, a collection of honors and accolades reaped over a remarkable career\. There were citations framed on the wall and photographs with men of history, leaders and king makers who counted him their equal\. The accomplishments and regard had mattered enough that he commissioned this private museum built to house such glory\. But without discounting the achievements of an extraordinary life, these no longer held his heart\. Something happened that caused him to see the value of things in the light of eternity\. He watched as stars began to flicker in the night sky\. Tomorrow would be another day but he was happy to sit under a heaven bright with a constellation overhead*\.__

__*Artemio V\. Panganiban pondered the events of his life from the vantage point of 89 years\. Though he had not been schooled in catechesis, a confluence of events led to the firm acknowledgement that Divinity had charted his course\. He deemed that circumstances and encounters, people he met, opportunities as well as failures and losses were alliances of destiny\. These led to a faith life that he now valued more than the successes of a career, material gain and the esteem of men\. He saw that all that had been was an orchestration to realize a plan greater than he had envisioned for himself\.*__

__*There were markers that punctuated the progression to a relationship with God\. Some might have been perceptible, others not as apparent\. Yet when he reflected on his spiritual journey, Artemio believed with certainty that the Christian God he raged against had been with him from the moment he took his first breath\. It may not have seemed so to him then, growing up in humble circumstances, losing a parent, hawking newspapers to pay for his studies and*__

__*sleeping on sidewalks\. Only by understanding with faith did he appreciate that everything that had happened led him to the ultimate purpose of his life\.*__

# EARLY YEARS

__*How does the president of a nation, a pope and a disruptive world situation factor into the life of a newborn who began his existence nondescriptly in a Tondo hospital? Only in hindsight would the interweaving threads of religion, politics, social and economic conditions make apparent the unique tapestry of a remarkable life\. It would scarcely be discernable that a child born in humble circumstances would contribute on a national scale to righting societal ills championed by a commonwealth leader or take up the cause of the Church he nominally knew\.*__

__*His birthplace, where he grew up, what he would become were also affected by decisions and actions taken by his forebears\. There were also the considerations of migration, the social and cultural milieu that would invariably affect his future\. External conditions as*__

__*zeitgeist, environs, events that occurred oceans and continents away, family,	associations	and	the community at large would leave an impression upon him, consciously or otherwise\. to shape his values, outlook and ultimately character\. The distinctive moral and mental qualities arising from these factors formed the compass that would consequently define the person and his chosen path\. Yet beyond all that, there remained a Prime Mover greater than all these\.*__

Artemio V\. Panganiban was born in the year when Manuel L\. Quezon was president of the Philippine Commonwealth, Pope Pius XI was head of the Catholic Church and the world was in the throes of the Great Depression, one of the worst economic downturns in the industrialized world\. The Philippine head of state had mounted a vigorous campaign to institute social justice particularly for the Philippine working class, inspired by the encyclicals of Pope Pius XI’s *Quadragesimo* *Anno *and Pope Leo XIII’s *Rerum Novarum*\. The latter document issued on May 15, 1891 was widely held as a modern Catholic position on the economic, social and political order, addressing the industrial revolution and transitions of governments across Europe\. The progressive tenets aligned the Church with the marginalized and the working man, supporting the rights of labor, rejecting socialism and

communist ideologies and cautioning against unrestricted capitalism\.

Forty years after, Pope Pius XI amplified the ethical considerations for economic justice and the rights of the workers\. He opposed socialist and communist systems, as well as laissez\-faire capitalism\.

The spirit of these Catholic documents resonated with President Quezon who introduced executive and legislative measures to benefit the Filipino working class and agricultural workers, people like the family of Artemio V\. Panganiban, who descended from a generation of tenant farmers\. His father, Artemio, Sr\. was the eldest of eight children born to Wenceslao Panganiban and Honorata Pelayo\. The couple actually had another eight offspring who had either died at childbirth or thereafter\. Wenceslao was a farmer whose parents traced their roots to Batangas\. After he married Honorata, they moved to Barrio Mandili, her domicile in Candaba, Pampanga\. Pursuing the occupation of their forebears, the couple tilled land they did not own\. As tenants, they followed the ancient practice of sharing their harvested crops with the landowners\. Farming was a matter of survival and hardly a means to improve one’s station\.

Agricultural laborers apply themselves to months of backbreaking, manual work and then wait till the crops are ready for harvesting\. During their “dead time,” a period known as *tiempo muerto*, they have no income, no other source of livelihood\. It is a cycle of penury for farming

families, who are unable to shore up savings and inevitably rack up

debts to tide them over till they can gather the yield of their labors\. Even then, what they earn remains insufficient\. There is barely enough for daily subsistence and practically nothing for medical concerns, much less for education\.

# Temi and Patricia

It was fortuitous that the boy Artemio’s father, known to kin as “Temi,” finished high school\. Education likely opened the mind of the young man to possibilities of a future different from that of his progenitors\. Just as important would be the choice of a life partner who would join him in diverting from the agrarian occupation\. Temi had managed to gain the favor of Patricia, daughter of the *cabeza del barrio, *Señor Igmidio Villaseñor, locally known as “Apong Midio\.”

While her family was a little better off than Temi’s, Patricia’s stern and old\-fashioned father did not believe in education\. Midio’s wife had passed away shortly after Patricia was born\. Agang, as she was called, was the younger of two daughters\. While she was growing up, it became her assigned lot to keep house for the family\. Studying was not a priority and she did not finish elementary school\. However, though she spoke no English and could barely manage Tagalog, Agang was adept in the

culinary language of her province, arguably among the best of Filipino regional cuisine\. She prepared *morcon *or *bulanglang *without the benefit of a cookbook, approximating portions of ingredients and seasonings to produce exact flavors that she would confidently serve without even tasting them\. That no one ever complained was a testament to her culinary skills\. She had been brought up to be a homemaker and that was the path she took when she eloped with Temi to Manila\.

Relocation to the city effectively embarked the family on a trajectory away from the tenant\-farmer occupation that had sustained generations on both the paternal and maternal sides of their families\. Temi and Agang’s children would consequently be exposed to a life other than farming\.

The young couple established residence on Cataluña Street corner España in the district of Sampaloc\. Cataluña, now known as G\. Tolentino Street, was neither picturesque nor indistinguishable from other low\-income residential areas\. Proximity to a concentration of Catholic churches and learning institutions made it a convenient place to live, albeit congested and teeming with human activity\. This was where they settled and where Temi’s sisters eventually came to reside with them\. It was not an uncommon arrangement for extended family members to live together under one roof\.

Though only a secondary school graduate, Temi found employ as a clerk in the old Bureau of Lands and was able to provide a decent life for his family on a government worker’s salary\. He may have been among the rank and file, but he would go to the office every day wearing a necktie\. The division chiefs and higher officials sported suits with starched wing\-collar shirts, even if the *americana *was too warm for Philippine weather\. American presence in the country would leave an impression on many facets of Philippine life and culture, not the least in the way people dressed\. The more practical *Barong Tagalog, *which had been discarded in the late 1920s, only resurfaced as formal wear when President Ramon Magsaysay began wearing the untucked, loose Filipino men’s shirt to official functions\. Fashion had become an expression of patriotism\.

The political and economic landscape was greatly determined by Americans who replaced the Spanish colonizers in 1898\. Trade agreements provided for tax\-free import of Philippine raw materials, and the US became the primary market for Philippine exports\. When the Great Depression hit America, the Philippines was indirectly affected by America’s diminished economic capacity\. The colonized archipelago had been made economically dependent on the US and the reduced demand stunted the growth of exports, adversely affecting the supply of currency,

as well as the production and consumption of goods and services\. Stirrings of nationalism were on the rise as was the movement to become an independent nation\. While there were clusters in the provinces expressing patriotism and advocating freedom, most of the

activities pushing for independence were happening in Manila where the Panganibans had chosen to reside\.

# Favorite Child

All the children of Temi and Agang were born in the metropolis\. Their eldest was a daughter named Teresita\. She was followed by Nardo and then Lolita\. The youngest son and namesake, Artemio Villaseñor Panganiban Jr\., was born on December 7, 1936 at the Mary Johnston Hospital in Tondo\. A year prior to the young Artemio’s entry into the world, Manila had just been designated as the capital of the newly formed Commonwealth of the Philippines\. Historically, it had been the seat of power for centuries and an important trading hub\. Access through the Pacific Ocean established it as a center of commerce, bringing worldwide trade particularly during the Spanish era\. Historical notes described the Manila district of Tondo as the Eastern capital, so named by the Chinese for its location southeast of China\. “The polity of Tondo flourished during the latter half of the Ming dynasty as a result of direct

trade relations with China\. The Tondo district was the traditional capital of the empire, and its rulers were sovereign kings, not mere chieftains\.” \(Manila – The Reader Wiki\.com\)

The birth of the youngest of the Panganiban brood happened a few weeks before Christmas\. He was born during the holiday season amidst the festiveness anticipating one of the most awaited feasts of the Catholic Church\. Particulars of his arrival are lost memories although relatives recounted that the infant Artemio refused to suck from Agang’s breasts\. Consequently, Temi’s sisters referred to him as “*anac ning baca*” \(son of a cow\) because he drank canned evaporated milk instead of his mother’s milk\.

Family and close relations called the second Panganiban son *Dayunior*, having been christened with the same name as his father\. The appendage “Jr\.” was registered in his birth certificate, school records, marriage certificate and other early documents and became his nickname from childhood through young adulthood\.

While he had a good relationship with his father, Dayunior was attached to his *Ima, *the Capampangan term for mother\. Agang doted on him and turned a blind eye to the mischief of her little boy\. He was a bit of a rascal who felt he could get his way and inevitably got into trouble\. The spirited child terrorized his playmates for sport\. Despite his lean

frame, he was said to have bullied the children of poorer neighbors who resided in makeshift houses by a nearby sawmill\. However, before he could grow up into a rogue, a cataclysm suddenly descended upon him and his family, his country and the world a day after his fifth birthday\. Japanese forces bombed Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941\. Ten hours later, they attacked the Philippines with aerial bombardments and ground troop assault\. World War II had begun\.

# Escape to Mandili

Following the attack on Pearl Harbor, Temi immediately decided that it was best for their family to move back to Barrio Mandili\. The Japanese were arriving and Gen\. Douglas MacArthur declared the capital an “open city” to forestall destruction by ground or air offensive\. Manila was demilitarized and all defensive efforts were abandoned\. Dayunior had just turned five years old\. He would have no memory of life before the war\.

War brought displacement, abrupt relocation and changed circumstances\. The magnitude of any war’s disruption escalates human capacity to adapt, no matter how young or old a person is, although children are among the most vulnerable in a situation of armed conflict\.

Like any parent, Temi’s instinctive response to the news of the attack

was to move his family immediately to safety\. He quickly entrusted the children to his three sisters to take them posthaste to the province away from impending danger\. He and Agang were to follow with whatever they could bring\. The women and their wards traveled in the cover of night, passing through the town of San Miguel in Bulacan, taking an outrigger canoe to reach their destination\. It was a long trip and the very first Dayunior took without his Ima\. He had never been separated from her before that time\.

The boy might not have understood the magnitude of war and its dire implications but the separation was serious enough to convey horrors he had not previously known\. Being whisked from home at a time when he should have been getting ready for bed was bewildering but the absence of his Ima’s comforting presence left him distraught\. He cried throughout the journey, inconsolable in the arms of his aunts who took turns carrying him in their arms trying to soothe him\. Their efforts were no match for his fears\. Unable to calm Dayunior, they resorted to scare tactics and threatened him with stories of a *kapre*, the folkoric giant who would snatch and kill him if he did not stop his sobbing and whimpering\. Huddled in the *banca*, unable to make out forms in the darkness, he became even more terrorized\. The figure of a monstrous creature lurking in the watery blackness added to his distress and

increased his wailing\. By the time they landed, he had lost his voice\.

The exhausted party made their way to the nipa hut of his grandmother Honorata where they waited till Temi and Agang arrived\. Dayunior had been so traumatized by the departure and separation experience that he became psychologically unresponsive and recessive\. It took the comfort, affection and nurturing of his parents to help him recover\. As soon as they were reunited, the family moved to the larger house of Patricia’s father\.

By this time, Apong Midio had remarried\. The union had produced a son named Frankie, who was about the same age as Dayunior\. Though effectively his uncle, Frankie became Dayunior’s best friend and all seemed well in their world\. They played games together, spent days running around or stealthily clambering up the fruit trees that the stern and taciturn Apong Midio prohibited them from climbing, in fear they would fall or hurt themselves\.

Living in the barrio honed his ability to speak the local dialect reasonably well\. His mother spoke to her children only in Pampango\. He learned to adapt to environs that were different from what he had previously known and likely contributed to building in him an inner capacity to adjust to change\.

# Hideaway Discovered

The family stayed in the remote town for a couple of years\. It was located in the middle of the Candaba swamp and was constantly flooded\. The place, like many backwoods in the country, had no running water\. Barrio folk got their drinking water from a hand pump\. They washed and cooked, sourcing water from a nearby stream\. Hospitals or medical facilities were nonexistent\. Health concerns were brought to *herbolarios*, folk healers who chanted prayers and used indigenous plants to medicate ills, aches and pains\. Barrio Mandili also did not have a church or chapel, much less a priest\. The absence of conveniences and services notwithstanding, it offered a sense of security, however tenuous\. Location and distance from the urban center made the residents feel far removed from the conflict and violence raging in the capital\.

The war, however, caught up with them one day and violently intruded into the community\. Japanese soldiers descended upon the remote village and shattered any illusion of being hidden and unreachable\. Armed and threatening, they immediately rounded up all the able\-bodied men and arrested them on suspicion that they were part of the *Hukbong Bayan Laban sa Hapon, *literally translated as “People’s Army Against the Japanese\.” Widely known as the Hukbalahap or by the

abbreviation “Huks,” this socialist/communist guerrilla movement was originally formed by farmers in Central Luzon to resist Japanese invaders\.

Temi was among the victims who were beaten and threatened by the soldiers\. The Japanese who entered the house saw a photograph of a man in full uniform and concluded he had ties with the rebels\. He was, in fact, an active supporter of the guerillas and a good friend of Luis Taruc, the Hukbalahap leader from 1942 to 1950\. Dayunior was terrified as his father was slapped and kicked mercilessly\. It was a horrifying

tableau unfolding before his eyes as Agang fell on her knees and begged the tormentors to stop\. His parents were at the mercy of these strangers with bayonets, and death was not a remote possibility\. Through tears, his mother implored them to spare her husband, pleading in Pampango, the only language she knew\. The Japanese did not know the dialect but they understood her importuning\. Whether they had been moved or decided he was of no consequence to them, Temi was released\.

# Three Years in Candaba

The episode with the Japanese soldiers was a harrowing experience that left them feeling exposed and unprotected\. The chance to relocate opened up when Servando Sagcal, the mayor of Candaba,

offered Temi the position of mayor’s secretary\. He accepted and moved his family to the town center\. They lodged in the Home Economics Building of the Candaba Elementary School\.

Just like Mandili, the town had no hospital, clinic, pharmacy or practicing doctors\. When Dayunior contracted dysentery at the age of six, his demise was a foregone conclusion since they had no access to medical help\. It was his Ima who took care of him untiringly, sponging his fevered body and rubbing his aching stomach\. She held him in her arms all through the days and nights, giving him water to keep him hydrated as he drifted in and out of consciousness, that he recovered without medicine or medical attention was regarded as a miracle\.

Dayunior was seven years old when he enrolled in first grade\. One of his teachers was the mayor’s pretty daughter Liling who taught her students to sing nursery rhymes that he enjoyed\. The faculty included enlisted men of the Imperial Army who instructed the students to speak Nihongo\. They learned basic Japanese from these soldiers who were friendlier than the ones Dayunior had first encountered in Mandili\.

He studied at the Candaba Elementary School until the third grade\. The war raged for three years but in 1945, American troops arrived and the tide turned against the Japanese\. US aircraft began dropping bombs

which signaled the arrival of liberation forces\. Dayunior witnessed airplanes in a dog fight near their residence\. When an American warplane started a bombing run, he ran with his siblings to the underside of the Home Economics building for shelter\. The small air raid dump was more like a hole in the ground, where he crouched in fear as bombs exploded near them\. When the fighting ceased, they emerged from their hiding place, and onto the threshold of a new chapter in their life as a family and as a nation\.

## Summary

Chapter 1 opens with an 89-year-old Artemio V. Panganiban in his private museum at dusk, looking back on a remarkable career and concluding that 'Divinity had charted his course' -- the providential thesis that frames the whole biography. The chapter proper then situates his 1936 birth in Tondo against the Commonwealth presidency of Manuel L. Quezon, the papacy of Pius XI, the Great Depression, and the Catholic social encyclicals (Leo XIII's Rerum Novarum, Pius XI's Quadragesimo Anno) that animated Quezon's social-justice program and would later define CJP's own 'Liberty and Prosperity' motto. His paternal line -- tenant farmers Wenceslao Panganiban and Honorata Pelayo in Barrio Mandili, Candaba, Pampanga, the eldest of their eight surviving children named Artemio Sr. ('Temi') -- had moved between Batangas and Pampanga in the agrarian *tiempo muerto* poverty cycle; his maternal line through *cabeza del barrio* Igmidio Villaseñor ('Apong Midio') produced Patricia 'Agang,' the Pampango-only-speaking homemaker who eloped to Manila with Temi. The Pacific War defines the chapter: after Pearl Harbor and MacArthur's 'open city' declaration, the family flees back to Mandili in a night canoe through San Miguel, Bulacan -- five-year-old Dayunior separated from his mother for the first time, terrorized by his aunts' *kapre* tales until his voice fails, arriving 'psychologically unresponsive' for days until reunited with Agang. A Japanese raid finds a framed photograph of a uniformed man and beats Temi as a suspected Hukbalahap supporter -- which he was, Luis Taruc being his friend -- until Agang's Pampango plea (which the soldiers could not understand but evidently felt) spares his life. The family then relocates to Candaba town when Mayor Servando Sagcal offers Temi a secretary post, lodging in the Home Economics Building of Candaba Elementary School where Dayunior begins first grade under Liling Sagcal and Imperial Army instructors. At six he survives dysentery with no medicine through Agang's continuous nursing -- a recovery 'regarded as a miracle' -- and in 1945 he watches the American liberation bombs from the underside of the Home Economics building. The chapter ends with the family at the 'threshold of a new chapter,' and with the prologue's claim quietly ratified: that everything that had already happened was 'an orchestration to realize a plan greater than he had envisioned for himself.'
