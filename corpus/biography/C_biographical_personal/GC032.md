# Chapter 12: Guided by the Word

Chapter 12

				 __Guided by the Word__

__*Fo*__*r *__*just as from the heavens the rain and snow come down*__

__*And do not return there till they have watered the earth,*__

__*making it fertile and fruitful,*__

__*giving seed to him who sows*__

__*and bread to him who eats,*__

__*So shall my word be that goes forth from my mouth;*__

__*It shall not return to me void, but shall do my will,*__

__*Achieving the end for which I sent it\.*__

__*							Isaiah 55:10\-11*__

	The maelstrom following the developments at the impeachment trial in the Senate was cause for concern\. The 11\-10 vote against opening an envelope linking President Estrada to a multi\-million peso secret account instigated a call across different sectors for the resignation of the chief executive\. Business and professional groups, civic and religious leaders, civil society and non\-government organizations were massing in the streets demanding he step down\. Government officials, even at the cabinet level, were resigning from their positions\. By the weekend of January 19, heads of the Armed Forces of the Philippines and the Philippine National Police shifted allegiance to Vice\-President Gloria Macapagal\-Arroyo\. The Estrada administration was debilitated and rendered unable to govern the country\. Adding to the dilemma, the vice\-president could not legally take over\. Government was effectively at a standstill\.

	The situation was ripe for anarchy\. Estrada supporters had stationed themselves in Mendiola, close to Malacañang Palace where the president stayed\. The opposing multitude in EDSA were preparing to march in that direction and the possibility of a violent confrontation was not farfetched\. 

	Art monitored the developments as it unfolded on nationwide telecast\. He was aghast, unable to turn in for the night till he fell asleep at 1:00 a\.m\. Three hours later, he was awake again and switched on the television in their bedroom\. The TV host was reporting that Jaime Cardinal Sin, through Monsignor Socrates Villegas, was making earnest appeals to the EDSA rallyists not to proceed to Mendiola\.

	

__Averting Anarchy__

	Art mulled over the worrisome predicament\. Confrontation could deteriorate into a bloodbath\. The president could not avert it without the military that had already withdrawn their support for him\. Mrs\. Arroyo was not in a position to take command, there being a legally sitting president\. Had she done so, the vice\-president would have effectively been usurping power, establishing a revolutionary government and setting aside the constitution\. A vacuum of power would open the probability of military adventurism with the armed forces staging a coup d’etat\.  Either scenario was unconstitutional and meant the dissolution of constitutional offices including the presidency, the legislative bodies and the Supreme Court\. All these would weigh down on the country’s ailing economy, foreboding a devastating collapse\. The nation would be on the throes of civil strife\. 

	These thoughts occupied Art’s mind\. His recourse to the troublesome state of affairs was prayer\. As he reflected, it became apparent that the best and only course of action to prevent bloodshed and chaos was for Mrs\. Arroyo to be sworn in as president of the republic\. Democracy would be upheld and violence averted\. 

	The sun had not yet risen as he continued to pray\. As was his practice, he turned to the day’s gospel and read from the book of St\. Mark, Chapter 3, Verses 20\-22\. Going through the passage, his heart stirred as the words of scripture spoke directly to his concerns\. It spoke of the scribes accusing Jesus Christ of being possessed by the prince of demons\.

	*“   Then Jesus entered a house, and again a crowd gathered so *

*	that he and his disciples were not even able to eat\. When his*

*family heard about this, they went to take charge of him for*

*they said, “He is out of his mind\.”*

*	And the teachers of the law who came down from Jerusalem*

*said, “He is possessed by Beelzebub\. By the prince of demons, he *

*is driving out demons\.” \(Mark 3:20\-22\)*

	The verses gave him the affirmation he needed to do what might have been regarded as unconventional and contrary to the norm\. He was to say later, “ …\[I\]n our community – whether it be of people who are supposed to love us like our relatives, or of those who hate us, like our never\-satisfied critics – we could be misunderstood, even thought of as insane when we espouse unorthodox ideas and actions\. But we should always stand fast and take courage, especially when our eccentricity is born of faith that transcends human wisdom\.”

__Preempted by Isaiah__

	Art was about to make a call to Chief Justice Hilario Davide, another early riser who began his day reflecting on randomly opened pages of the Bible\. Another praying man, he had been up at his usual time of 3:30 a\.m\. for scripture reading and reflections\. He firmly believed that his elevation into office was a divine appointment\. Each morning he put himself at God’s disposal and sought instruction for the day ahead\.  That day he found himself meditating on the “Restoration of Zion” in the book of the prophet Isaiah, Chapter 62\. He contemplated on its meaning as it applied to him and deeply sensed that he had to take action to restore normalcy to the country\. Consequently, when Art’s call came through with his unusual proposal to swear in Mrs\. Arroyo by noon on that same day, he was receptive and agreed to the suggestion\. Each was affirming a deep sense that their mandate was divinely inspired\. Both men were going beyond the normal course of things, taking extraordinary steps to save the Constitution and the country they loved and served\.

	The Supreme Court was the only institution that had the moral authority to avert a governmental catastrophe\. Chief Justice Davide’s performance of his duty as presiding officer of the impeachment court had made him the most trusted Filipino, a fact borne out by a scientific poll survey\. Art believed that he was the one person who could navigate the country away from a national upheaval\.

	The chief magistrate requested Art to make known the action he was about to take\. Immediately, radio and television networks were tapped and the news was quickly disseminated\. A justice who heard the broadcast questioned the proposal, prompting Chief Justice Davide to call an emergency session of the court magistrates\. A dozen jurists attended, while three who were out of town were unable to make it\. Those present had an animated exchange discussing different aspects of the Constitution before agreeing to support the oath\-taking of Mrs\. Arroyo, on the condition that she make a written request to that effect\. In the end, the magistrates of the highest court of the land were all present when she was sworn in by Chief Justice Davide\.

	     

__Visiting Prisoners __

	Art’s role in the historic event was not widely known\. He did not aspire for recognition\. He had elected to be discreet since assuming office, maintaining a low profile and keeping his activities out of the public eye\. While Art mostly shunned company to avoid being misconstrued as fraternizing or conspiring with litigants and their relations or friends, he chose to be among an unlikely set of people\. There was a time when an invitation had been extended to visit the national penitentiary in Muntinlupa\. The Supreme Court’s security department cautioned against accepting, as the facility was populated by persons convicted of serious crimes\. Some of the persons there were deprived of liberty after their sentences had been affirmed by the Supreme Court\. The risk of an unfavorable reception to his presence was a real cause for concern\. 

	He saw the merit and soundness of their reasoning\. Logic and prudence would have prevailed had he not gone to Mass and heard the Gospel according to Matthew Chapter 25\. Scripture verses described the final judgment, with Christ calling to himself those who had fed the hungry, given drink to the thirsty, clothed the naked and shown kindness to strangers\. He was particularly struck by Verse 36 which reads: ”I was in prison and you came to visit me\.” That was all the prompting he needed\. Art decided to accept the invitation\. “Better to risk the ire of the inmates than to risk the ire of the Lord\.”

	The visit happened on a bright, sunny afternoon\. Art could not think of any sitting member of the high court ever entering the National Bilibid Prison\. His presence was certainly a first for a Supreme Court Justice\. When the car turned from Sampaguita Road into the country’s main insular prison, he was astonished by the considerable land area of the penitentiary\. There was the imposing maximum\-security building with high walls and barbed wire for those deemed dangerous and potential escapees\. Surrounding the concrete behemoth were hectares of land with fruit\-bearing trees and shrubs\. It wasn’t at all what he expected\.

	Even more surprising was the reception he received as he entered the makeshift auditorium by the greeneries\. Thunderous applause from around 500 hardened inmates welcomed the rare visitor\. They gawked at him as he made his way into the music of their band playing “Mabuhay\.” The convocation commenced with the Philippine national anthem followed by a solemn invocation\.

	The next hour was a display of the Filipino ability to entertain as inmates performed hip\-hop and folk dances\. The band accompanied singers who rendered tunes popularized by Frank Sinatra, Joni James and Johnny Mathis\. It was an enjoyable show, with Art particularly amused at the comedy skit essaying life in jail\. When he was asked to say a few words at the end of the program, he stood up and surveyed his audience thoughtfully\. There was no prepared speech and he spoke extemporaneously, looking at the faces before him\. 

	He began by asking how many among them had he personally sentenced to life imprisonment or to death\. At the time, the death penalty had not yet been abolished\. About 30 hands went up, prompting whistle calls from the others\. It was a brave call, but Art proceeded and asked how many of them felt they had been wrongly sentenced\. No one raised a hand, much to his great relief\. He then launched into a message of hope\.

	He spoke earnestly from the heart, telling them that while iron bars restricted their movements and their imprisonment subjected them to ostracism and ridicule, they should not hate the world or lose their faith\. He met the indifferent gaze of those who had long been languishing in jail for crimes of murder, robbery, rape, kidnapping and other serious offenses\. 

	Speaking in Filipino, he went on to explain that the world was full of people who were themselves in some kind of prison or another\. He said many rich persons were imprisoned by greed\. They were weighed down by anxieties over the potential reversal of their fortunes and the vulnerability of their children to kidnappers\. Some were locked by incurable illnesses, living in pain with terminal medical conditions\. He mentioned those imprisoned by their minds because of insanity or feeble\-mindedness\. Those kinds of bondage were harder to deal with because of the absence of remedy and cures\. 

	Art referred back to the inmates’ situation, telling them that unlike those he cited who were ensnared by greed, bound by psychological incapacities or diseased bodies, those behind bars had hope\. One day their sentences would have been served and they would then be free\. He said that those with good behavior could have their period of imprisonment commuted, or they could even receive pardon\. They could look forward to an end to their time in jail unlike those condemned to pain and suffering all their lives\.

	It was a message of hope he hoped to impart and by the applause and cheers at the end of his speech, he felt he had been able to give them a perspective that dispelled the pallor of darkness through a shaft of light\. He was happy at the thought that, even in their circumstances, they might have grasped something to live and aspire for\. 

__Temporal and Spiritual Laws__

	As he was leaving, some of the prisoners walked with him towards the exit\. They handed to him their letters handwritten on yellow sheets of paper, pleading for recommendations for pardon\. Among them was a life termer who posed a question to the Justice\. Speaking in the vernacular, the inmate began by asking: Weren’t all the commandments summed up in one sentence: Love God and love fellowmen\. Why then did he, a good Catholic and a good judge, sentence him to life imprisonment as a punishment for his transgressions? It contrasted with the Church’s way of treating sinners\. He had gone to confession and the priest forgave him and asked him to pray the rosary\. 

	The man looked at him with pain in his eyes\. He asked Art why could he not have been more like his confessor and directed him to the right path and a chance to live a new life? Why was he condemned to live his life in prison? Wasn’t the punishment meted out inconsistent with love?

	It was twilight\. A soft glow streaked across the sky as the sun was disappearing into the horizon\. Art was eager to get home before dark, but the brief exchange with the life termer convicted of syndicated estafa provoked a soul searching	through the ride back\. Later in his study he thought about his judicial career, his faith, and the Lord he had come to know as first and foremost a God of love\. How then could he, who was a mere mortal follower of Jesus Christ, take pride in imposing penalties in the name of justice? Was he contradicting the Lord’s commandment to love by dispensing the justice expected of

 and ordained in his profession?  

	Rereading the Gospel of the evangelist Matthew, he found his answer in the very same passage that prompted him to visit the Bilibid Prison\. Christ spoke about separating the sheep from the goats, placing the sheep on his right while relegating the goats to his left\. The goats were those who neglected to feed the hungry or provide drink for the thirsty, who did not clothe the naked or visit the sick or the imprisoned\. These he condemned to eternal punishment\. Those who did feed the hungry and give drink to the thirsty, who did not neglect to clothe the naked or take care of the sick or visit prisoners were rewarded with eternal life\.

	Art concluded that the Lord was making choices, too, because he was a God of justice as much as He was a God of love\. He deduced that just as much as there existed human and physical laws, so too existed spiritual laws\. Violations of the behaviors stipulated by any of these precepts would necessarily be penalized with consequences\.	

These regulations are premised on the intention to promote and protect the welfare of the people\. The civil courts are there to ensure that those who renege, for example, on contracts are liable for damages\.  When criminal laws are breached, the penalty is a jail sentence\. Similarly, transgression against God’s commandments effects a result as severe as eternal damnation\. 

	The scheme of things allows reparation for violations of the law, both temporal and divine\. The path of repentance, restitution and reformation is open to criminals and sinners\. It became clear in Art’s mind that the role and responsibilities of his position were not in conflict with God\.  Both the judge and the man of the cloth are tasked to guide transgressors towards upright living that includes taking responsibility for their actions and making amends\. His faith understanding also acknowledged that given the human condition, these could only be accomplished through grace\. God was the necessary recourse before any amendment or transformation could take place\. Fulfilling his role in dispensing justice was one avenue for the lost to find a way back to the Father\.  

	Art was at peace\. He closed his eyes and said a prayer of thanks for the guidance and asked for continued enlightenment in fulfilling his duties\. He got up from his chair, turned off the light, retired to his room and slept soundly that night\.

## Summary

Chapter 12 narrates two episodes in which Art was 'guided by the Word' through Scripture readings that decisively shaped his judicial conduct. The first is the January 2001 EDSA II constitutional crisis. After the impeachment trial's 11-10 vote not to open the envelope linking President Joseph Estrada to a multi-million peso secret bank account, cabinet officials resigned en masse, the Armed Forces of the Philippines and Philippine National Police defected to Vice-President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo by the weekend of January 19, EDSA crowds began preparing to march toward Estrada supporters massed at Mendiola, and Cardinal Sin made earnest appeals through Monsignor Socrates Villegas to prevent a bloodbath. Art monitored the unfolding situation on television, sleeping at 1:00 AM and waking at 4:00 AM. Praying through the scenarios -- Estrada could not avert violence without military support, GMA could not legally take over with a sitting president, and the absence of action would invite a coup that would dissolve all constitutional offices including the Supreme Court -- Art concluded that the only constitutional path was for GMA to be sworn in immediately. The day's Gospel reading was Mark 3:20-22, in which Jesus's family says 'he is out of his mind' and the scribes say 'he is possessed by Beelzebub' -- a scriptural authorization, Art read, for proposals that initially sound mad. He called Chief Justice Hilario Davide, who had been up since 3:30 AM at his customary scripture reading, meditating on Isaiah 62's 'Restoration of Zion' and already sensing he must act to restore normalcy. Davide was receptive: 'Each was affirming a deep sense that their mandate was divinely inspired,' and 'both men were going beyond the normal course of things, taking extraordinary steps to save the Constitution and the country they loved and served.' Davide called an emergency session of the Supreme Court -- 12 of 15 justices attended, the rest out of town -- and after animated constitutional debate they agreed to support GMA's noon oath-taking on the condition she submit a written request. All present magistrates attended the oath-taking; Davide administered. Art's role was 'not widely known' -- by deliberate choice. The same discretion describes Art's wider judicial conduct: he 'shunned company to avoid being misconstrued as fraternizing or conspiring with litigants and their relations.' The second episode is Art's visit to the National Bilibid Prison in Muntinlupa. Invited but cautioned against by Supreme Court security -- the facility housed persons convicted of serious crimes, some sentenced by the Court itself -- Art was decided by Matthew 25:36 ('I was in prison and you came to visit me'). 'Better to risk the ire of the inmates than to risk the ire of the Lord.' Arriving via Sampaguita Road to a maximum-security building surrounded by hectares of fruit-bearing trees, he was met by 500 hardened inmates with a band playing 'Mabuhay,' the national anthem, hip-hop and folk dances, Sinatra / Joni James / Johnny Mathis covers, and a comedy skit on jail life. He opened bravely: 'How many among you have I personally sentenced to life imprisonment or to death?' About 30 hands went up; whistle calls. 'How many feel wrongly sentenced?' No hands. He spoke extemporaneously in Tagalog of hope, distinguishing the inmates from those 'imprisoned by greed' (the anxious rich), 'imprisoned by illness' (the terminally ill), and 'imprisoned by their minds' (the insane and feeble-minded) -- those without recourse, while the inmates could look forward to commutation or pardon. Yellow handwritten pardon-pleas were pressed into his hand at the exit. One life-termer convicted of syndicated estafa asked him in the vernacular: weren't all commandments summed up in 'love God and love fellowmen' -- so why had Art, a good Catholic, condemned him to life when the confessor had asked only for the rosary? On the twilight ride home and through a night of study, Art rereread Matthew 25 -- and found Christ himself separating sheep from goats and sending some to 'eternal punishment.' He concluded that God is a God of justice as much as a God of love, that 'just as much as there existed human and physical laws, so too existed spiritual laws,' that both temporal and divine codes permit reparation through 'repentance, restitution and reformation,' and that 'fulfilling his role in dispensing justice was one avenue for the lost to find a way back to the Father.' Art was at peace. He said a prayer of thanks, turned off the light, retired, and slept soundly that night.
