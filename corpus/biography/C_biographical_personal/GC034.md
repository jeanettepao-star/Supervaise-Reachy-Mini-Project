# Chapter 14: Liberty and Prosperity (Life after the Supreme Court)

Chapter 14

			           __Liberty and Prosperity__

__*	            He has shown you, O mortal, what is good\.*__

__*		    And what does the Lord require of you?*__

__*		       To act justly and to love mercy and *__

__*                               to walk humbly with your God\.*__

__* \(Micah 6:8\)*__

	Chief Justice Artemio V\. Panganiban completed his term in the Supreme Court at midnight of his 69th year\.  It was a constitutionally mandated retirement which stipulated magistrates at all levels “shall hold office during good behavior until they reach the age of seventy years\.” He had served for over 11 years, the last of which was as primus inter pares of the highest court of the land\.

	Colleagues lauded him for an unsurpassed record of leaving no case undecided\. Upon his assumption as a member of the Supreme Court in October 1995, he immediately went to work on 700 pending cases that had accumulated in his predecessor’s chamber\. The 14 senior sitting members also “unloaded” 280 more cases for him to address, in addition to the 35 or more new cases raffled monthly to his chambers\. 

	Unfazed by the tremendous responsibilities, Associate Justice Art Panganiban worked systematically to reduce the backlog, imposing upon himself a quota of 104 fully written decisions annually–or two per week–despite vacations, official travels, illnesses and other emergencies\. The 1987 Constitution directed that all cases in which the last pleading required by the Court had been submitted must be resolved within 24 months\. That was the guideline by which he measured his output\. He was serving the cause of justice, minding the Christian tenet to act justly as God required\.  

	 Other tasks he had to accomplish included giving short shrift to thousands of petitions that lacked merit at sight by disposing them through “minute resolutions\.” These resolutions were signed by the clerk of court under the authority of the justices\. The pace he kept enabled him to finish the inherited, uncompleted work, as well as his own monthly load\. Eventually, just after a few years from his appointment, only current cases were on his desk\. 

	Not only did Chief Justice Art accomplish his judicial responsibilities, he published one book a year since he became a justice of the Supreme Court\. Each volume reported his activities as a magistrate covering the year from October 10 to October 10, which was the date he took his oath of office in 1995\. The number of books published corresponded to the number of years he had served in the Supreme Court\.	

__Renaissance Jurist__

	When he assumed the position of chief magistrate, it would not have been humanly possible to maintain the quota he had previously set for himself\. Chief Justice Art had to contend with administrative functions attendant to his new designation\. Almost 70 percent of his waking hours was spent managing the entire judiciary and only 30 percent making decisions\. Without cutting his self\-imposed quota, he still produced an average of two full *ponencias* every week\.

Applying skills honed in his previous occupation as a practicing lawyer, law professor, and business entrepreneur, he restructured processes following management principles in order to establish more efficient and productive systems\. While continuing to dispose of the cases assigned to him, he turned his attention to the many old cases pending in the Supreme Court, particularly those that had been assigned to other justices for more than two years\. He initiated a “Zero\-Backlog Program” upon his assumption as chief justice and led in complying with it through the expeditious completion of the work assigned to his chambers\. By the end of his term, there were 59 volumes of Resolutions, Decisions and Separate Opinions credited to him\. Each volume of about 600 pages was tantamount to an average of ten pages a day for seven days a week in a span of over 11 years\. A colleague in the Court, Justice Romeo Callejo Jr\., underscored the feat by declaring, “One book a year and no cases left undecided\. It was an unsurpassed record\.” His other peers led by Justice Angelina Sandoval\-Gutierrez lauded him “not just for being an outstanding jurist but also for his clarity of thought and clearness of prose\.” Justice Antonio T\. Carpio described him as "Undoubtedly the most prolific writer of the Court, bar none\.”

He aimed to influence the direction and pace of the Court’s work following his judicial philosophy, which in his own words was “to safeguard the liberty and nurture the prosperity of our people under the rule of law\.” He posited that liberty and prosperity are mutually inclusive and necessarily bound by the same legal structures\. Adherence to the rule of law promotes an environment conducive to “freedom for the spirit and food for the body\.”  To thrive, it was essential for the flourishing of human capabilities and man’s material wealth to impose no restriction to think, speak and act for as long as these were exercised within the bounds of jurisprudence\. 

“Liberty becomes the guarantee that, free from all undue interference and suppression, we all can conceive of the good life and act according to those conceptions,” he explained\. “In litigations involving civil liberties, the scales should weigh heavily against the government and in favor of the people – particularly the poor, the oppressed, the marginalized, the dispossessed and the weak\. In conflicts affecting prosperity, development and the economy, deference must be accorded to the political branches of the government\.”

__Legacy__

	 The Chief Justice had always hoped that one of his children would pursue a career in law\. They all became accomplished professionals, but none in the field that he had pursued\. The eldest child, Maria Elena, who graduated with a degree in Management Engineering from the Ateneo de Manila and who completed her Masters in Business Administration at the University of California \- Irvine, is the president of the Baron Travel Corporation and its related companies\. Jose Artemio III completed his Bachelor of Music, summa cum laude at the University of the Philippines and shifted magnificently to further studies at the Stanford University, completing MS Operations Research, with distinction, and Ph\.D\. in Engineering Economic Systems\. A pianist by passion and banker by profession, Archie, as he is fondly called, is an Executive Director at JP Morgan in New York City\. Second daughter, Maria Jocelyn, earned her BS in Management Engineering from the Ateneo de Manila, cum laude, and her MBA from the University of Chicago\.  An investment banker, Celine is Chief Financial Officer of White Oak Global Advisers in New York City\.  The fourth child, Maria Theresa, also went to the Ateneo de Manila like her sisters, and finished her MBA at the University of Michigan\. She now teaches finance at the Asian Institute of Management, where her late mother taught for 37 years\. Daughter number four and the youngest, Maria Evelyn, went to the University of the Philippines where she completed her BS Biology before pursuing her MA in Economics at Boston University and Master in Public Policy at Harvard University\. Married to Pete Reagan with whom she has two sons, she works in New York City as Managing Director of New York Life Investors, a subsidiary of New York Life Insurance Co\., NYC\.

	He was undoubtedly proud of his children’s academic and professional accomplishments\. While he hoped there might be a lawyer among his grandchildren, he wanted to ensure that his judicial philosophy would be passed on to succeeding generations of lawyers and citizens\. Creating a foundation became his natural recourse\.

__Foundation for Liberty & Prosperity__

	The intent to promote and propagate “Liberty and Prosperity under the Rule of Law” set in motion an institution that would be known as the “Foundation for Liberty & Prosperity” \(FLP\)\. The Chief Justice envisioned a society that fully appreciated the necessity of freedom and of thriving well economically as essentially

 interdependent and mutually inclusive\. FLP stood to be the Chief Justice’s legacy as well as his contribution to nation\-building with programs supporting the country’s government and business sectors towards economic growth and expansion\. He believed that wealth generated through economic activity should benefit more people as prescribed under the social justice principle of giving more law to those who have less in life and to the encyclical Solicitudo Rei Socialis\. Beyond providing safeguards for the people to freely express their thoughts and actions, he felt it weighed just as much for the law to provide for freedom from poverty and hunger\.   

	He expressed that “libertarianism of the mind, though extremely important, is not enough\. We must also have liberation from extreme poverty; we must have both freedom for the spirit and food for the body, freedom from fear and freedom from want\. We need and deserve justice and jobs, ethics and economics, peace and development, liberty and prosperity under the rule of law, not under the rule of dictators or authoritarians\.” Even as he acknowledged that the task to promote and develop the country’s economy was primarily the charge of the country’s political leadership, he wanted to make his own contribution towards achieving the Philippines’ full potential as a rapidly developing nation\.

	FLP began the task of perpetuating the principle to safeguard the liberty of people, and to nurture their prosperity under the rule of law by sponsoring professorial chairs, law scholarships and dissertation\-writing contests\. The merit\-based programs expanded to students of MBA or equivalent degrees\. Beyond support for the academically astute, FLP created an educational assistance program for underprivileged students with passing grades as the only requirement\.

FLP will also implement its “ultimate” projects that will represent the two sides of its philosophy: liberty and prosperity\.

For the liberty side, FLP will establish a Center for Liberty and

Prosperity that will include a Museum of Liberty and Prosperity–an immersive, interactive, interdisciplinary, AI\-powered and tech\-driven futuristic center for education and research that will also offer an extensive catalogue of archives and artifacts relevant to the grand narrative of “liberty and prosperity under the rule of law\.”

For the prosperity side, FLP’s “ultimate” project is a multi\-billion Fund for Entrepreneurship that will be used to help liberate the poor from extreme poverty by helping them help themselves through private initiative\. The Fund is intended to help micro, small, and medium Enterprises \(MSMEs\) that do not have access to banks or traditional investors\. FLP will prioritize MSMEs with social impact or those firms that are labor\-intensive, agriculture\-centered, and the like, and will help these MSMEs by investing in them and/or providing management assistance through a team of experts\.

__New Journey__

	Chief Justice Art earlier imagined that his retirement years would be spent attending to the foundation, writing a weekly column for the nationally circulated broadsheet *Philippine Daily Inquirer* where he once sat as president, and generally pedaling down to a more relaxed pace\. He saw himself at the golf course, the tennis court, the gym and leisurely travelling with his family\. It was not to be\. Big business enterprises recognized the contributions he could make to their companies with his vast business experience, legal acumen and stature\. He began to receive invitations to join several leading conglomerates as an independent director or an independent advisor\. The offers came from persons who appreciated his work in the judiciary, people he had not previously known\. 

As an independent director, he did not represent any shareholder or vested interest in the boards he joined\. His role, as stipulated under the law, was to assist companies observe the canons of good corporate governance, serving as a check on management and the controlling shareholders\.

Board and committee meetings easily filled up his daily calendar\. He was a valued member of several blue\-chip companies that included Meralco, PLDT, Bank of the Philippine Islands, Metrobank, Petron, GMA Network, First Philippine Holdings Corporation, JG Summit Holdings Inc\., Jollibee Foods Corporation, Metro Pacific Investments Corporation, Metro Pacific Tollways Corporation, Robinsons Land, Robinsons REIT, Double Dragon Properties Corporation, Merry Mart Consumer Corporation, and Asian Terminals, Inc\. This kind of work suited him, being akin to the environment of independence, integrity, probity and competence he enjoyed in the Supreme Court\. 

He no longer restricted himself from interacting with friends and family, a practice adopted when he became a jurist to forestall any perception of undue influence and to protect the integrity of the cases he was handling\. He could now receive into his home visitors who would come to consult him, discuss or exchange views or simply converse with him on various topics, from the hottest AI innovations to the latest economic, social and political controversies\.

__Strengthening God’s Cathedral__

	 It wasn’t just the corporate world that tapped the retired chief justice to assist their companies\. The Catholic Church also sought him for help\. He was appointed president of the Manila Cathedral\-Basilica Foundation, which had been tasked by the then Archbishop of Manila Gaudencio B\. Cardinal Rosales to restore, retrofit and enhance the basilica in Intramuros\. The good prelate felt it was necessary to strengthen the belfry of the cathedral dedicated to the Immaculate Concepcion\. The belfry moved from side to side whenever cargo trucks rumbled by on the street outside\. The possibility of its toppling over in the event of a major earthquake would incur considerable damage to the basilica\. The Foundation responded by hiring engineers who assessed that not only the belfry, but the entire church building, needed to be strengthened\.

	It became an even more daunting task when necessary documents \(“as\-completed plans”\) to start the work could not be found in the archives of the Archdiocese, the National Library, or the Manila City Engineer’s office\. It was crucial to know the pilings or rocks on which the Neo\-Romanesque cultural property stood\. Adding to these concerns was the fact that the cathedral was built on reclaimed land that could liquify and cause it to collapse\.

	When Archbishop Antonio G\. Tagle was appointed following the retirement of Cardinal Rosales, the task to pursue the rehabilitation of the basilica fell on Chief Justice Art\. The latter provided a legal perspective on the liabilities of the prelate, as well as his own as foundation president should the cathedral collapse\. They would be held responsible for any death or damage during such an eventuality, given that they had been forewarned by the structural engineers who had submitted a written report on the stability and integrity of the building\.   

	Archbishop Tagle, in consideration of the safety of the churchgoers, decided to close the cathedral\. The retired chief justice tapped his extensive business network to fund the strengthening and retrofitting of the Metropolitan Cathedral of the Immaculate Conception\. The generous response allowed the extensive work to be completed in three years, the eighth iteration of the basilica since it was founded in 1571\. The chief justice’s active role in raising monetary resources was an offering of gratitude to the Lord and to the Blessed Mother whom he now acknowledged as having loved and guided him long before he knew them\.

__Singing an Ageless Passion__

	Chief Justice Art himself looked at the upturns and downturns of his life and could only attribute to the Lord his ascent from humble beginnings to where he now found himself\. He had spoken many times of how the Lord had been generous to him throughout his dire circumstances that had  given him no intimation of a station so far removed from that impoverished state\. It was a stretch of imagination that the newsboy\-sleeping\-on\-sidewalks would become the president of the largest\-circulated daily in the country\. What, too, was the likelihood that the applicant for a college education who was humiliated for his ignorance of the Catholic faith would become a lay member of the Vatican’s Pontifical Council of the Laity, the highest lay adviser of the Pope\.  And who would have thought that he–who had bitterly suffered the anguish of losing a scholarship in Yale for lack of money to buy a plane ticket, and whose visa application was unceremoniously rejected by the US Embassy due to his known student activism–would be able to send his five children abroad to obtain postgraduate degrees in top universities\. Most astonishing of all was how he, acquiescing to his father’s dream for him to become a lawyer, and forgoing his ambition to be an engineer, would occupy the highest judicial position in the country?

	He saw the remarkable concurrences of his life’s events as acts of the Divine and shared his story openly and unabashedly\. When his 75th and 80th birth anniversaries came around, the story\-telling of his personal history transitioned to another realm: a full\-length libretto with 18 original songs\. The FLP mounted *Ageless Passion*, a musical tribute to the retired chief justice\.

	The musical premiere brought together notable names and faces\. It was a gathering of distinguished guests who watched the dramatization of his seeming defeats turned into successes\.  Equally impressive were the talents that put the show together\. National Artist for Music Ryan Cayabyab composed 18 songs, while the libretto was penned by lyricist and poet Kristian Jeff Agustin\. Bart Guingona directed a cast that included Michael Williams, Topper Fabregas, and Cris Villonco among others\. The 75\-member Manila Symphony Orchestra provided the musical accompaniment\. The entire staging was later telecast in full for two hours on GMA Network\.

	When Michael Williams sang “Ageless Passion,” the words captured the refrain of Chief Justice Art’s life: 

“You have your whole life ahead of you\. 

Enjoy your dreams, watch them come true\!

“Whether we are seven or seventy years old,

Whether we may have ten years

or ten days left,

Believe in your dreams:

Dream what you believe,

Put your heart into it,

Everything you’ll achieve\!”

## Summary

Chapter 14 covers Art Panganiban's life from his constitutionally mandated retirement at midnight of his sixty-ninth year (December 2006) through his ongoing post-Supreme Court life as foundation founder, independent director, church benefactor, and elder statesman. It opens with the discipline of his eleven-year tenure: inheriting seven hundred pending cases, being 'unloaded' two hundred and eighty more by senior colleagues, taking thirty-five fresh cases each month, and yet meeting a self-imposed quota of 104 decisions per year (two a week) calibrated to the 1987 Constitution's twenty-four-month deadline. As Chief Justice, with seventy percent of his time going to administration, he still produces two ponencias a week, leaving fifty-nine volumes of Resolutions, Decisions and Separate Opinions -- averaging ten pages a day, seven days a week, for eleven years -- and no case left undecided. The chapter articulates his judicial philosophy, 'to safeguard the liberty and nurture the prosperity of our people under the rule of law,' with its two-track structure: in civil-liberties cases the scales tilt against the government and toward the poor and marginalized; in conflicts affecting prosperity and economic development, deference belongs to the political branches. The chapter then turns to legacy. None of his five children -- all of them now accomplished professionals abroad and at home -- chose law; so he creates the Foundation for Liberty & Prosperity (FLP) to pass on the philosophy: professorial chairs, law scholarships, MBA scholarships, dissertation-writing contests, an educational assistance program for the underprivileged, and two 'ultimate' projects -- a Center and Museum of Liberty and Prosperity, and a multi-billion-peso Fund for Entrepreneurship for MSMEs without access to traditional finance. Outside FLP, leading conglomerates (Meralco, PLDT, BPI, Metrobank, Petron, GMA Network, JG Summit, Jollibee, the Metro Pacific and Robinsons groups, Double Dragon, Merry Mart, Asian Terminals) recruit him as independent director -- a role he treats as judicial impartiality transposed into the boardroom. The Church taps him to lead the Manila Cathedral-Basilica Foundation; with Cardinal Rosales then Archbishop Tagle, and a network of donors, he raises the funds to retrofit and restrengthen the cathedral on liquefaction-prone reclaimed land -- the eighth iteration of the basilica since 1571 -- in three years. The chapter closes with the FLP-mounted Ageless Passion musical for his seventy-fifth and eightieth birthdays, with Ryan Cayabyab's eighteen original songs and the Manila Symphony Orchestra, telecast in full on GMA Network: a public, lyrical summary of a life whose seeming defeats turned, again and again, into successes.
