# With Due Respect (Vol. 4) -- Ch. 3: Hail to the Court; A Toast to Truth

# Hail to the Court; A Toast to Truth
Date:       2008-02-17
Publisher:  Philippine Daily Inquirer (Opinion)
Source:     With Due Respect (2011)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban


Hail to the Puno court for championing press freedom. By stop ping the National Telecommunications Commission (NTC) from canceling the operating ns Commission of radio and television stations that air the so-called "Hello Garci tapes," the Supreme Court has again lived up to its long-held reputation as the bastion of liberty.

**Prior restraint**

In precise, crystal-clear language, the decision-written by Chief Justice Reynato S. Puno-labeled the NTC threat as "an unconstitutional prior restraint. a naked means to prevent the free exercise of speech. a patent invasion of a constitutionally protected right."

It justified its heady language with vintage Puno prose: A " blow too soon struck for freedom is preferred than a blow too late."

The Court reiterated jurisprudence, culled over the last 100 years, holding that "content-based" restrictions on free speech cannot be allowed, except under the "clear and present danger rule." It held that the NTC miserably failed to prove this exception.

The Court further ruled that the airing of the damning conversation "allegedly between the President of the Philippines and a high-ranking official of the Commission on Elections. does not endanger national security" although it may cause a "diverse effect on a person's private comfort."

May I add that the duty of the judiciary is not to soothe private discomfort but to uphold the Bill of Rights. Imagine the havoc the NTC would have inflicted on us had the Court allowed it to cancel the licenses of radio and television network aired the controversial discs. No self-respecting and independent broadcast station would be spared and our people would have been left to the mercies of media outlets spouting only government propaganda.

Apart from the immediate euphoria it generated, the decision should be compulsory reading for lawyers and journalists alike, because of its scholarly dissertation on media freedom. Adding illumination are the concurring opinions. Relish for instance Justice Angelina Sandoval-Gutierrez's timely invocation of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and her singular citation of Canadian Chief Justice Beverley McLachlin (whom I invited to our country when I was Chief Justice). Enjoy also -Justice Antonio Carpio's view that NTC does not have any "content based censorship power."

So too, read the separate/dissenting opinions of Justice. Chico-Nazario, Presbitero J. Velasco Dante O. Tinga, Minita V Jr., and Antonio Eduardo B. Nachura, because they point out some food for thought (mentioned also in the majority opinions) that only a private citizen-Francisco I. Chavez, not a media -personality-complained of the NTC threat. Worse, the Kapisanan ng mga Brodkaster as Pilipinas issued a "Joint Press Statement" that the "NIC respects and will not hinder freedom of the press."

The moral lesson: media must be more careful in agreeing with constitutionally infirm "guidelines, standards, parameters, or joint statements" that could later be used as legal traps by the government. The parameters are already set by the Constitution. There is no need for any other "guidelines." (February 17, 2008)

## Summary

Chapter 3 of 'With Due Respect' (Vol. 4), CJP's February 17, 2008 Inquirer column, hails 'the Puno court for championing press freedom' after it stopped the National Telecommunications Commission (NTC) from canceling the licenses of radio and television stations that aired the 'Hello Garci tapes' -- once more proving the Court 'the bastion of liberty.' Written by Chief Justice Puno, the decision branded the NTC threat 'an unconstitutional prior restraint... a naked means to prevent the free exercise of speech... a patent invasion of a constitutionally protected right,' capped by the line that 'a blow too soon struck for freedom is preferred than a blow too late.' Reaffirming a century of jurisprudence, the Court held that 'content-based' restrictions on speech cannot stand except under the 'clear and present danger rule,' which the NTC 'miserably failed to prove' -- airing the conversation 'allegedly between the President... and a high-ranking [Comelec] official' endangered no national security, even if it caused 'adverse effect on a person's private comfort.' CJP adds that 'the duty of the judiciary is not to soothe private discomfort but to uphold the Bill of Rights,' warning that had the NTC prevailed, 'no self-respecting and independent broadcast station would be spared' and the people would be left to 'government propaganda.' He commends the ruling as 'compulsory reading' for its 'scholarly dissertation on media freedom,' praising Justice Sandoval-Gutierrez's invocation of the Universal Declaration and of Canadian Chief Justice Beverley McLachlin (whom he had invited to the country), and Justice Carpio's view that the NTC has no 'content-based censorship power.' He notes the dissenters' observation that only a private citizen, Francisco Chavez, complained, and that the broadcasters' association (KBP) had itself stated that the 'NTC respects and will not hinder freedom of the press.' His moral lesson: media 'must be more careful' about agreeing to 'constitutionally infirm "guidelines... or joint statements"' that can become 'legal traps,' for 'the parameters are already set by the Constitution. There is no need for any other "guidelines."'
