# With Due Respect (Vol. 7) -- Ch. 1: Sanctity of Life and of Marriage

# Sanctity of Life and of Marriage
Date:       2010-10-03
Publisher:  Philippine Daily Inquirer (Opinion)
Source:     With Due Respect (2011)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban


If there is one issue in which the hierarchical Church is solidly united, and in which all our Catholic bishops are unanimous in upholding and defending, it is the sanctity of life and the sanctity of marriage, which together complement the sanctity of the family. This explains why the Church passionately opposes the Reproductive Health Bill and the divorce bill pending in Congress, and why it is extra sensitive to President Noynoy Aquinos  pronouncements on these sanctities.

**Sanctity of life**

The basic Catholic theology on the sanctity of life is found in Humanae Vitae (Of Human Life), an encyclical of Pope Paul VI (July 25, 1968) condemning abortion and prohibiting artificial contraception. In his message celebrating the 40th anniversary of Humanae Vitae in 2008, Pope Benedict XVI admitted that this teaching has become controversial in light of present scientific and economic realities but insisted that it was a truth nonetheless, saying, "What was true yesterday is still true today."

To be sure, our 1987 Constitution outlaws abortion, commanding that the "State... shall equally protect the life of the mother and the life of the unborn from conception." Decades earlier, the Penal Code had already criminalized infanticide (killing of an infant less than three days old) and abortion (killing of a fetus in the uterus or its expulsion from the womb resulting in death).

Other countries are still debating the beginning of life to determine when a "killing" can be deemed an abortion. Does life begin at the union of the egg and the sperm? Or is it merely when the fetus is capable of independent life outside the womb? Or is it only at the cutting of the umbilical cord at birth? In contrast, our Constitution settled that issue, saying that the unborn shall be protected "from conception"; that is, from the fertilization of the human egg by the human sperm.

The burning issue being waged by the Church is no longer the beginning of life at conception, but way before that-the prevention by any artificial means of the union of the egg and the sperm. Thus, the Church prohibits the use of medicines, devices, and procedures that artificially stop the released human sperms from uniting with the human e88, including condoms, IUDs, diaphragms, ligation, vasectomy, and "pills."

**Sanctity of marriage**

"What God has joined together, let no man put asunder." Upon this oft-quoted biblical command rests the Catholic dogma of marital indissolubility. Thus, to the Church, no human institution, including the state, can dissolve a valid marriage. Our Civil Code and, later, our 1988 Family Code echo this inviolability by defining marriage as a "permanent union" and an "inviolable social institution."

Nonetheless, the Church recognizes the nullity of a Catholic marriage when one or both partners suffers from "psychological incapacity" at its inception. Canon 1095 states: "Those who ear unable to assume the essential obligations of a marriage due to causes of a psychological nature [are] incapable of contracting marriage."

The Church requires that the psychological incapacity must be (1) grave, (2) incurable, and (3) antecedent, that is, it must have existed at the time of the marriage, and was not acquired only after it had been celebrated. A party who was psychologically incapacitated at the time the rites were held does not contract a valid marriage from the very beginning.

Canon 1095 was substantially adopted as Article 36 of our " marriage contracted by any party who, at Family Code, thus: A the time of the celebration, was psychologically incapacitated to comply with the essential obligations of marriage, shall likewise be void even if such incapacity becomes manifest only after its solemnization."

The early Supreme Court decisions beginning with Santos vs Court of Appeals (January 4, 1995, per Justice Jose C. Vitug) closely hued to the three Church requirements. Republic vs Molina (February 13, 1997, which I penned) instituted eight guidelines to assist parties in availing of psychological incapacity. However, later jurisprudence. tended to stray from these early decisions and became "more liberal."

Responding to critics, the Supreme Court's latest decision (Suazo vs Suazo, March 10, 2010, per Justice Arturo D. Brion) deftly clarified that the early jurisprudence (Santos and Molina) have not been abandoned and are still good case law.

Rankling the Church is the divorce bill of Rep. Luzviminda Ilagan proposing "psychological incapacity" (along with "de facto" separation for five years, irreconcilable differences, etc.) as a ground. Theologically untenable is the bill's proposal that psychological incapacity need not be "antecedent"; it may arise after the rite of marriage, thereby infringing its inviolability.

In sum, I hope P-Noy and the Church can avoid a grisly confrontation. After all, Benedict XVI-in his address before the British Parliament on September 21, 2010— advocated "ongoing dialogue" between "the world of secular ration world of religious belief." (October 3, 2010)

## Summary

Chapter 1 of 'With Due Respect' (Vol. 7), CJP's October 3, 2010 Inquirer column, treats 'the sanctity of life and... of marriage' -- the issues uniting the Catholic hierarchy against the Reproductive Health Bill and the divorce bill, and making it 'extra sensitive' to President Aquino's pronouncements. On life, the 'basic Catholic theology' is Humanae Vitae (Paul VI, 1968), 'condemning abortion and prohibiting artificial contraception,' which Benedict XVI defended decades later: 'What was true yesterday is still true today.' The 1987 Constitution 'outlaws abortion,' protecting the unborn 'from conception,' so unlike countries still debating when life begins, the live Philippine dispute is the Church's bar on artificial means of preventing the egg-sperm union (condoms, IUDs, ligation, 'pills'). On marriage, the dogma that 'what God has joined together, let no man put asunder' makes it an 'inviolable social institution' that 'no human institution, including the state, can dissolve' -- yet the Church recognizes nullity for 'psychological incapacity' that is 'grave,' 'incurable' and 'antecedent' (Canon 1095, adopted as Article 36 of the Family Code). CJP traces the jurisprudence he helped shape: Santos v. CA (1995) and his own Republic v. Molina (1997, the 'eight guidelines') 'closely hued to the three Church requirements,' a 'more liberal' drift followed, and Suazo v. Suazo (2010) clarified that Santos and Molina remain 'good case law.' He faults Rep. Luzviminda Ilagan's divorce bill as 'theologically untenable' for letting psychological incapacity arise after the wedding rather than be 'antecedent,' thus 'infringing inviolability' -- but hopes P-Noy and the Church can avoid 'a grisly confrontation,' echoing Benedict XVI's Westminster call for 'ongoing dialogue' between secular reason and religious belief.
