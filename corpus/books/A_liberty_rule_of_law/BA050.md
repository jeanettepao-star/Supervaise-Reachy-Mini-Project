# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Appendix C: DNA as Evidence

Appendix C

DNA as Evidence

Prof. Pacifico A. Agabin

UP College of Law

1. Introduction

One of the death convicts who has been scheduled for execution is Gerrico Vallejo y Samartino (G.R. No. 144656, May 9,2002). Gerrico is accused of raping and then strangling to death a nine-year old neighbor. The Regional Trial Court of Cavite City, on the strength of circumstantial evidence, convicted him and sentenced him to death. The death sentence was affirmed by the Supreme Court in a per curiam decision last May 9, 2002. One of the links in circumstantial evidence against Gerrico was a DNA analysis conducted by the NBI chemical laboratory. Vaginal swabs taken from the body of the victim showed that the DNA profile matched that of Gerrico's DNA profile. The NBI chemist, testifying for the prosecution, informed the trial court that her findings were definite and conclusive insofar as it showed a positive match between the DNA obtained from the victim's body and that of the accused. The court took this at face value, and the Supreme Court confirmed the conclusion.

With this ruling, it is now settled that DNA profiles are admissible as evidence in judicial proceedings. Before this ruling, our courts have been reluctant to resort to DNA profiling as a means of crime investigation because of the novelty of the technology, the lack of laboratory facilities, the degradation of specimen in the hands of our inexperienced investigators, and the unawareness of the members of the judiciary of recent breakthroughs in biotechnology. These factors should not really stand in the affect the weight, rather than the admissibility, of evidence. As for lack of laboratory facilities, our government agencies should do some explaining to the public, since the National Bureau was reported to have bought a DNA testing equipment in early 1996, which prompted the NBI director to announce that "with the DNA machine, test results could be obtained in a matter of minutes" (Daily Inquirer, January 14, 1996, p.1).

II. DNA Analysis: Testing Principles

In the developed countries, DNA has become a magic word because it started a revolution in the natural sciences. In biochemistry, a new method of cutting and splicing recombinant DNA into new beneficial combinations gave rise to genetic engineering. This method is now being commercially exploited, and is touted as the wave of the future. In biotechnology, technicians can alter existing organisms to perform certain tasks. DNA technology has given hope to cancer patients by founding cancer microbiology and neurobiology, which are new frontiers in medical science. In genetics, the key to human cloning is splitting of the human cell and its DNA.

DNA or deoxyribonucleic acid refers to the chain of molecules found in every cell of the body except in red blood cells. These are rolled up into minute bodies called "chromosomes," which transmit hereditary characteristics among individuals. It is these characteristics which make us unique as individuals. Our bodies contain about 10 trillion cells, and each cell contains 46 chromosomes, arranged in pairs of 22 plus two sex chromosomes (See discussion in People v. Wesley, 533 NYS2d 643, at 645). DNA is the molecule that carries the body's genetic information which is contained in every cell. The genetic information in the DNA can be compared to the listing and information contained in one bulky edition of the PLDT directory.

The basic principle in DNA analysis is that no two individuals have identical DNA, except identical twins, which are few and far between. DNA does not vary from one cell to another; in other words, DNA found in the hair follicles is identical to that found in the semen, the blood tissues, and other parts of the body (U.S. Office of Technology Assessment. Genetic Witness: Forensic Uses of DNA Tests. OTA-BNA-438. U.S. Congress, 1990, p.3).

DNA as a chemical structure contains four bases called nucleotides, and are referred to by their initial letters: A adenine, G for guanine, C for cytosine, and T for thymine ("Comment: Beyond People v. Castro: A New Standard of Admissibility for DNA Fingerprinting," 7.J Contemp. Law & Policy (1991), p. 269). It is easy to remember these nucleotides by recalling the title of the recent movie, GATTACA, which portrays the identification of individuals by their DNA profiles. The manner in which these chemical bases appear in a person's DNA determines his physical traits and characteristics. The DNA structure resembles a twisted ladder, or a double helix (somewhat similar to the Shell logo, with the rungs of the ladder composed to bond between the chemical bases of A, T, C, and G. Each rung contains two bases: A-T, T-A, C-G or G-C, held together by hydrogen bonds. No other combination is possible because only the A-T and C-G combinations are chemically attracted to each other (People v. Wesley, supra, id. at 646). Each rung on the DNA ladder is known as "base sequence" or "base pair." There are about three billion base pairs in a molecule of DNA; in other words, the genetic code in each cell of the human body contains three billion bits of information (People v. Wesley, ibid). The order in which the base pairs appear in the ladder to determine the characteristics of a person (People v. Castro, 545 NYS2d 985 [1989] at 988).

The base pairs are called "genes," which are the basic units of inheritance composed of a segment of DNA and carrying the coded information associated with a specific function. Each gene contains a certain number of base pairs arranged in a certain order. The genetic code lies in the order of the base pairs in the DNA molecule, and this order is passed on from generation to generation. While most base pairs of humans are similar, there are sections in the DNA ladder which are variable, and these sections are called "polymorphic." These take different forms for different individuals.

It is these variable portions of the DNA, called "polymorphic sites" or "loci," which are significant for purposes of analysis. It is these loci that are responsible for an individual's differing characteristics, and these provide the basis for DNA analysis or typing. These allow individuals to be recognized through their DNA profiles or typing. (People v. Castro, ibid.). There are three million loci variations in different areas of the DNA. Obviously, if a person's DNA profile examines all the three million sites of variation, then that person's DNA could be individualized Since this cannot be done, the alternative approach is to discriminate between various persons' DNA by getting their patterns, and this is done by examining their polymorphic sites (People v. Castro, ibid.). Once the polymorphic sites are located and isolated after sampling, the unique pattern of a person's DNA is observed.

Locating the polymorphic sites or loci or variations has been analogized by one author to looking for a needle in a haystack (See Thompson and Ford, "DNA Typing: Acceptance and Weight of the New Genetic Identification Tests," 75 Va. L. Rev. 45 [1989]). Of the three billion bits of information, there are three million sites that vary from person to person. Even three million sites is impossible to examine in a lifetime, so the scientists have to content themselves with getting the pattern of a person's DNA polymorphism. This is like getting a snapshot of the profile of an individual, and not of his whole body. Most of the time, the DNA profile taken from crime scenes come out as a blurred snapshot which is difficult to discern.

III. Procedures for DNA Analysis

There are two basic procedures for DNA analysis: (1) restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) and (2) polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and its variation short tandem repeat (STR), and even a combination of the two, called amplified restriction fragment length polymorphism (AmpFLP).

The first method consists of (a) identifying the fragments containing the polymorphic sites using a genetic probe; and (b) measuring the length of the fragments. Following the earlier analogy of looking for a needle haystack, this process would be like spreading the haystack and using a magnet to find the needle (Thompson and Ford, supra). The magnet to be used here would be what is called a “genetic probe” which is specially constructed DNA consisting of small pieces, with the sequence of base pairs made complementary to the sequence it is designed to lock on in the DNA under analysis. The technician chops the DNA samples extracted from cells taken from a suspect, from the victim, and from the evidence. Cutting is done by an enzyme, which is a protein which chemically cuts a DNA molecule at a specific site. After sorting the DNA pieces by size on a gel, this is washed with a solution containing four types of tiny DNA segments, called probes ("DNA Fingerprints in Court Cases," Science News, April 29, 1992). When comparing two DNA patterns, the technician simply looks at where the fragments of DNA carrying the complementary code for a specific base sequence (or "probe") landed on the two patterns. This is seen through a process starting with what is called "electrophoresis," where the lengths of DNA fragments are measured by seeing how far they move through a slab of gelatinous material under the pull of an electric charge. Shorter fragments with low molecular weight move farther down the slab of gel than longer fragments with high molecular weight (Kaye, "The Relevance of 'Matching DNA: Is the Window Half Open or Half Shut?" 85 J. of Crim. Law and Criminology 676 [1995] at 679). Fragments of about the same size will stop at more or less the same point, coalescing to form a spot or a band. By measuring the location of the bands, it is possible to determine the approximate lengths of the fragments (Kaye, ibid.).

After electrophoresis, a permanent "copy" is made of the DNA fragments in the gel through what is called "Southern" blotting (named after the scientist who discovered the process). This is done by placing a sheet of nylon membrane in contact with the gel, and a Southern transfer is used to transfer the DNA fragments from the gel to the membrane. In this process, the DNA molecules composed of double strands are treated with a chemical which causes the double strands to separate into single strands, like a zipper being opened (Thompson and Ford, id. at 71). The next step is "hybridization,'" which is like passing the magnet over the DNA strands to locate the key polymorphic segments. The genetic probe will lock onto the polymorphic segments, but will not lock onto the other fragments which are not polymorphic in the sample. The probes have been tagged with "markets" so that, after they lock onto the polymorphic segments, their positions on the membrane, or blot, can be determined. After they are located on the probe, the polymorphic segments can be seen through a photographic process called "autoradiography." This is done by placing the blot on an X-ray film, and the energy from the tagged probe exposes the film, producing a pattern of bands, like a supermarket bar code which is known as the DNA print or profile (Thompson and Ford, id. at 73-74).

The other method of analyzing DNA samples is the "polymerase chain reaction" technique, or PCR. This is also known as "molecular photocopying" because in this method the DNA copies itself many times. This method does not analyze the DNA segments directly; rather, it determines whether DNA polymorphic segments exist in a given sample or not. These are called "alleles," or pairs of genes that determine the expression of some particular characteristic. It is these alleles that are copied millions of times, which is why the PCR method is analogized to the photocopying machine. Thus, if the RFLP technique is like using a magnet to find the location of a needle in a haystack, PCR is like using a metal detector to determine whether a particular kind of needle is present, then copying it many times.

This method is used where only minute specimens are found. The reaction enables a single strand of DNA to double immediately, and the process can be repeated several times. After the amplification, the DNA is analyzed by applying it into a membrane using a spotting, or a "dot-blot" apparatus. This consists of a pattern of small holes through which multiple DNA samples are sucked into the membrane using a vacuum, then a radioactive probe is added. If the being sought by the probe is present, the probe will lock onto it, making the spot radioactive. As a result, when the membrane is placed in an X-ray film, a dark dot will appear on the film (Thompson and Ford, supra, id. at 78). While this process is less definitive than the RFLP method, it can yield results faster.

Some laboratories in the United States use PCR testing to look for one particular gene, the DQ alpha gene. This gene is a protein which is part of the immune system. It is polymorphic, and there are six detectable alleles in the DQ alpha system. Each person has two alleles, one from each parent, so each person's DQ alpha test would yield two numbers, alike or different. The six combinations of alleles result in 21 possible genotypes, each of which appears in varying proportions within the population (People v. Lee, 537 NW2d 233, 250).

This discovery led to a specific form of PCR, which is called "DQa typing." This kind of analysis is similar to tissue typing on a genetic level. Tissue typing is used to determine which array of antigens is present in any given tissue. For instance, if someone receives an organ from a donor of a different type, it is likely that the recipient will reject the transplanted organ. It is important that the tissue type of both donor and recipient be determined before any organ transplant is made. In paternity investigations, tissue typing is resorted to determine the similarity of antigens.

In the DQ alpha test kit, a "dot" of each probe correlating to the DNA sequence of each allele under investigation is attached to a nylon membrane at a distinct location. The probe captures complementary amplified DNA, but it will not lock onto any non-complementary DNA sequences. In the DQ alpha test kit and other test systems, the detection system is composed of three molecules; one is a primer and the other two are enzyme conjugates which are added only e during the final steps of the procedure. The primer has a strong affinity and is highly specific to the enzyme conjugate. Thus, if the DNA in the sample and its attached primer is bound to the probe, the enzyme conjugate reacts with a soluble colorless compound to produce an insoluble blue product. The allelic composition of the sample DNA is indicated by the presence of blue spots on the nylon membrane. In addition to the test spots used to identify the discrete alleles, there is a control spot which will turn blue if the DQa genes have been amplified. The intensity of each test dot is compared with that of the control spot: if the control dot is absent, the test is considered unreadable. (McKnight. "The Polymerase Chain Reaction: The Second Generation of DNA Analysis Methods Takes the Stand," 9 Santa Clara Computer & High Tech. L.J. 287 [1993], at p. 306.)

A test kit, patented as AmpilType DQa, amplifies a specific portion of the human genome which is known to code for particular structures on white blood cells called "leukocytes," and which is used to type the person being studied. The test kit concentrates on the leukocytes antigen system. The human leukocyte antigen system (HLA) is composed of proteins ("antigens") which are coded for by a large number of genetic loci present in a specific chromosome. The HLA proteins are divided into two distinct groups, and in the second class there are three families of proteins - DP, DQ, and DR. Each of these classes of proteins is composed of two subunits, "a" and "B," which are separately encoded in the DNA of each gene cluster. It is the DQa which is amplified by the AmpilType test system, with DQA 1, 2, 3 and 4 as the major alleles, which in turn have their own subtypes. While these subtypes differ from each other by one or few nucleotides, they will all bind with the probe of the major type. Following PCR amplification of the evidence samples, the D a types are compared. If the DQa genotype of the suspect is different from that of the evidence sample the suspect is excluded and cannot be the source of the evidence. On the other hand, if the suspect and the evidence have the same genotype, then the suspect is "included" as a possible source of the evidence sample (MacKnight, supra at 310). The probability that another unrelated individual would also match the evidence is equal to the frequency of that genotype in the relevant population (MacKnight, ibid.). ue fi    Thus, in this test, the first step is to identify all of the possible alleles which could be present at the locus of interest, then the DNA sequences of these alleles must be determined, and lastly, relevant population groups must be studied to determine the frequencies of each allele and genotype for various ethnic groups.

The combination method of amplified restriction fragment length polymorphism (AmpFLP) is a recently developed test system in which a DNA sample too small for conventional RFLP analysis is first amplified and then tested by the RFLP method.

IV. Genetics, Statistics, and Lies

In both the RFLP and the PCR test methods, population genetics must be studied to determine the statistical probabilities that a certain person within a particular racial group will have a particular DNA profile or, in the case of PCR DQa testing, that he will have a particular combination of HLA DQa alleles. If DNA evidence is the main evidence to convict a defendant, the probability of a random match of DNA patterns or alleles between the evidence and the suspect is crucial. "Probability" here would refer to the likelihood of a series of independent events which is the product of their frequencies of coincidences. Thus, the probability is low when all the frequencies of its happening are low. For instance, in rolling dice, the probability of rolling a "one" eight times in a row with one die is (1/6)8 = .000000595 or one in 1.6 million.

The problem of statistical probability has given rise to some confusion in determining the meaning of "probability" of a random match. This is illustrated by two fallacies that are common in criminal cases: the "prosecutor's fallacy" and the "defendant's fallacy."

The "prosecutor's fallacy" is to confuse the two conditional probabilities. That is to say that the probability of a match is also the probability that the DNA taken from the scene of the crime came from a person other than the suspect. For example, suppose that there is a likelihood of 1,000 that the DNA sample from the crime scene and that taken from the suspect match; this means that the match is 1,000 times as probable if the evidence and the suspect samples that share the same profile are from the same person as it is if the samples are from different persons. It does not say that the odds that the suspect contributed to the DNA evidence are 1,000 to one (National Research Council, id. at pp. 5-8). In other words, it does not say that if the two samples match, then they are 1,000 times as likely to have come from the same person as from different persons.

On the other hand, the "defendant's fallacy" is to say that in a given population, anyone with the same DNA profile as the DNA crime scene sample is as likely to have left the sample as evidence. This would be correct reasoning if the suspect was discovered only through a DNA database with no other corroborative evidence. It would be false reasoning if the suspect was identified by evidence other than the DNA sample. Thus, if 100 persons in a given area have the same DNA profile as the evidence sample, it would be fallacious to conclude that the probability that the DNA sample at the scene of the crime came from the suspect is only 0.01 (National Research Council, id. at pp. 5-8). This reasoning, while logical, completely overlooks the other evidence pointing to the accused, as in the case of Gerrico Vallejo, who was convicted by circumstantial evidence.

Constitutional and Legal Issues

V.   Admissibility of DNA Analysis as Evidence

As stated earlier in this paper, the case of Gerrico Vallejo opened the way for the admission of DNA analysis as evidence in criminal cases. Before this, a number of civil cases in our trial courts have admitted DNA profiles as evidence in paternity cases but this issue of admissibility had not reached the Supreme Court. In criminal cases, as the Court describes it, when a crime is committed, material is collected from the scene of the crime or from the victim's body for the suspect's DNA. This is the evidence sample. The evidence sample is then matched with the reference sample taken from the suspect and the victim. The purpose of DNA testing is to ascertain whether an association exists between the evidence sample and the reference sample. The samples collected are subjected to various chemical processes to establish their profile (People v. Vallejo, supra). The Supreme Court then stated:

"In assessing the probative value of DNA evidence, therefore, courts should consider, among

other things, the following data: how the samples were collected, how they were handled, the possibility of contamination of the samples, the procedure followed in analyzing the samples, whether the proper standards were followed in conducting the tests, and the qualification of the analyst who conducted the tests.'" (People v. Gerrico Vallejo, G.R. No. 144656, May 9, 2002).

It must be noted from the pronouncement of the high court in this case that the issue of admissibility has not been delved into. In the U.S., the courts use the standard "Frye" test with a three-prong analysis in evaluating admissibility of DNA analysis:

"1. Is there a theory, generally accepted in the scientific community, which supports the conclusion that DNA testing can produce reliable results?

2. Are these techniques and experiments that currently exist capable of producing reliable results in DNA identification and which are generally acceptable in the scientific community?

3. Did the testing laboratory perform the accepted scientific techniques in analyzing the forensic samples in this particular case? (People v. Castro, 545 NYS2d 985).

The second standard , which is the one followed in the Philippines because it is prescribed in our Rules of Court, the relevancy test. This is more liberal than the "Frye test Here, as in the federal courts of the United States, evidence is admissible when it is relevant to the issue and is not excluded by the rules (Rule 128, Sec. 2). Under this test, evidence is relevant when it has such a relation to the fact in issue as to induce belief in its existence or non-existence 128, Sec. 2). In the United States federal rules, evidence is relevant when it has a tendency to make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the without evidence (Rule 401, Federal Rules of Evidence). Evidence, to be relevant, "need not conclusively prove the ultimate fact in issue, but only have any tendency to make determination of the point in issue more probable or less probable than it would be without the evidence," declared the US Supreme Court in New Jersey v. T.L.O. (469 US 325, at 345).

The US Supreme Court, in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals (113 Ct. 2786 [1993]) expressly abrogated the Frye standard insofar as federal courts are concerned, stating that the federal rules of evidence have superseded Frye. Under the federal rule, scientific evidence need not meet the test of general acceptance to be admissible. But "in order to qualify as scientific knowledge, an inference or assertion must be derived by scientific method. Proposed testimony must be supported by appropriate validation, i.e. 'good grounds' based on what is known" (Daubert case, id. at 2795). This derives from the Federal Rules of Evidence that, "if scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education may is testify thereto in the form of an opinion or otherwise" (Fed. ."          Rules of Evidence, Rule 702). This rule requires that scientific evidence "assist the trier of fact," and the Daubert ruling holds that this "requires a valid scientific connection to the pertinent inquiry as a precondition to admissibility."

Thus, the said rule quoted above still requires that scientific evidence be valid, aside from being relevant. Admissibility would depend on a number of factors as (1) whether the theory or technique can be or has been tested; (2) whether the theory or technique has been subjected to peer review and publication; (3) the known rate of error; (4) the existence and maintenance of standards controlling the techniques operation; and 5) whether the theory or technique is generally accepted in the scientific community (Daubert, id. at 2797-98). Relevancy, according to this case ruling, requires that "the proffered connection between the test result to be presented and the particular disputed factual issues in the case" can be shown (ibid.).

There seems to be very little distinction between this holding and the Frye standard in their application. Anyway, even the Daubert standards are not applicable in the Philippines, for our rules of evidence do not require such strict standards and preconditions. Our rule o n expert witnesses is phrased differently from that of the Federal Rules of Evidence. Under our rule, "The opinion of a witness on a matter requiring special knowledge, skill, experience, or training which he is shown to possess may be received in evidence" (Rule 130, Sec. 49).

Under this Rule, it can be seen that the issue of admissibility of DNA as evidence is not much of a legal hurdle. This is especially true where DNA profiles are presented merely as corroborative evidence and not the sole body of evidence against an accused. This is exemplified in the case of Gerrico Vallejo, where a string of circumstantial evidence caught him in the death trap. In this case, the Supreme Court noted that the young victim, on the day of the rape, went to a witness' house where the accused was residing; then the accused and the victim together went back to the latter's house to get a book, after which they proceeded to the accused's residence; from there the victim went to another witness' house to watch television and thereafter, the accused followed and whispered something to the victim, and the two of them went towards a structure in the river bank; later, a couple saw the accused coming out of the said structure with his clothes wet, and the fishing boat which the accused used for dynamite fishing as docked by the seashore; late in the afternoon, the victim's body was found tied to an aroma tree at the part of the river near the structure; the clothes of the accused were bloodstained and the clothes of the victim were also stained with human blood type "A" (the accused has type "O" blood); and the vaginal swabs taken from the victim's body contained her DNA profile as well as that of the accused. In short, the DNA profiles in t case would be admissible evidence as these are relevant to identify the perpetrator of the crime. Such evidence would be relevant with or without statistical support. In fact, in this case, there were no population statistics presented by the prosecution to back up the accused's DNA profile. Under our Rules, even collateral matters are allowed when they tend to any reasonable degree to establish the probability or improbability of the fact in issue (Rule 128, Sec. 4). With such liberal rules of evidence, there should not be any doubt as to the admissibility of DNA profiles in our courts, even without the support of population genetic statistics.

Thus, in a prosecution for abusos deshonestos, our Supreme Court held that it was not error for the trial court to convict the defendant on the basis of a finding of the Bureau of Science that the substance taken from the sex organ of the defendant showed that he was suffering from gonorrhea, like the complaining witness who was infected with the disease as a result of the crime (U.S. v. Tan Teng, 23 Phil. 145 [1912]). This shows that even a mere finding from the government bureau which shows a match between the disease of the defendant and that of the complaining witness can be considered relevant evidence under our rules, as it tended to show the probability of the defendant's physical contact with the complaining witness.

Even in the United States under the Frye test, some courts have admitted DNA match profiles without the statistical back-up. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that "the factual evidence of the physical testing of the DNA samples and the matching alleles, even without the statistical conclusions, tended to make the appellant's presence more likely than it would have been without the evidence, and was therefore relevant" (Commonwealth v. Crews, 640 A2d 395 [1994).

Our rules of evidence are all based on probabilities. "The most that can be reached is a high probability that the fact in question is true," says Wharton, for "it is impossible even to expound the principles and method of induction, as applied to natural phenomena, in a sound manner, without resting them upon the theory of probability" (Wharton, Criminal Evidence, cited in Francisco, id. at 48). "We have, therefore, to content ourselves with partial knowledge - knowledge mingled with ignorance producing doubt. Even scientific conclusions cannot be demonstrated except so far as they. deal with abstractions" (ibid.).

"Probabilities have been with us for years, and a statement of a probability is, by its nature, a statement of partial knowledge, so it is paradoxical to imply that in principle we cannot calculate the probability of an event without further empirical knowledge" (MacKnight, "The Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR): The Second Generation of DNA Analysis Method Takes the Stand," Santa Clara Computer & High Tech. L.J. 287 [1993] at 328).

Of course, the loose relevancy in our rules does not augur well for the protection of the rights of the accused. Scientific evidence tends to mystify even the hard-boiled judges who are ignorant of DNA technology. The scientific jargon may overwhelm them into admitting DNA evidence and give these full credence, unless the accused hires his own expert to challenge the findings. If the defense counsel fails to challenge the weight of such evidence because of ignorance of the procedures for validation, cross-checking and re-testing, or if he fails to challenge the premises of expert witness, the accused would remain at the mercy of the prosecution expert. The relevancy standard in evidence implicitly relies on the effectiveness of the adversarial system and on the right of cross-examination to protect the rights of the accused. Failure of the defense counsel to question the findings of the prosecution expert will place the accused at the short end of due process. The defense counsel in a situation like this should at least have an idea about laboratory testing and sampling procedures, population genetics, reliability of statistics, and laboratory error rates. The accused should have the logistics to hire his own expert so that he can be placed on an even keel with the prosecution.

As for expert opinion on DNA profiles, our rules of evidence admit such opinion as long as the witness proves that he is an expert in a given field. And he can do this by proving that he has knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education in any given field (Rule 130, Sec. 43). Of course, the difficult problem is that the presentation of DNA evidence requires different forms of expertise. The presenter may have to present a laboratory technician, a molecular biologist, a population statistician, and a geneticist. This is because the laboratory technician may not be qualified to testify on molecular biology, the biologist may not be able to testify on statistical probabilities on the frequency of a random match, and the statistician may not be qualified to testify on population genetics. Thus, in Powell v. State (598 SW2d 829[1994]), the defendant challenged the expertise of the molecular biologist to testify on the probabilities of matching DNA patterns because he was not qualified as a population geneticist. (See also Swanson v. State, 823 SW2d 812[1992]). In State v. Lewis, (654 So2d 761 [1995]), the trial court denied expert status to a lab technician who presented herself as an expert in molecular biology; the appellate court reversed because she belonged to invitational professional organizations, received several academic awards, had written 14 articles on DNA analysis, and had been assistant stem   director of a laboratory for more than one year.

The other problem here is that there are only a few experts in the pertinent fields. Experts in statistics, genetics, biology, organic and inorganic chemistry, computers, laboratory testing and protocols, on human hair and sperm, blood and bloodstains, etc. would be needed in this kind of inquiry. They are rare birds, especially here in the Philippines. They therefore do not come cheap, and engaging their services would add to the high cost of DNA profiling and testing, which has been estimated to cost at least P30,000 at pre-devaluation rates. A poor defendant may not have access to such experts. This raises constitutional issues of due process and violation of the rights of the accused. He may not get a fair trial fi he cannot engage the services of such an expert witness, since most defendants in heinous crimes like rape and murder are generally poor, they would not have the resources to pay for their own experts or to import them from abroad. This would result in a one-sided contest in our criminal justice system.

VI. Population Genetics as a Factor in Admissibility

While each person's DNA is indeed unique, it is impossible for any laboratory to examine all three million loci that vary from one person to another. The DNA profile that comes out of an RFLP test is just a pattern or profile of a person's DNA, which profile may coincidentally match with that of another person. Thus, to say that the two profiles match without providing a valid estimate of the frequency with which such matches might happen by chance does not really mean very much especially if there is no other evidence to identify the suspect. It would be of very little help to judge unless it is backed up by population genetics.

Population genetics is important for determining the weight of evidence under the Frye test, or even under the relevancy test where the primary proof of the guilt of the suspect is the DNA profile match. This is because of the constitutional presumption of innocence and the high threshold of evidence that must be met by the prosecution. If we take a situation where a match has been found between a DNA sample from the crime scene and that taken from the suspect. Assuming that there is no laboratory error, there are only two possibilities: (1) that the suspect was at the scene of the crime, or (2) that the sample taken from the crime scene came from somebody else who has the same DNA profile as the suspect. In a case like this, the court will then need to know the frequency of the profile among the population. This gives meaning to the result as it shows the likelihood that & likelihood that an unrelated person in the reference population would, by chance, have a matching profile. If the frequency of a coincidental match among the population is rare, then the probability that the DNA taken from the crime scene came from the suspect would be high. However, if the DNA profile in question is quite common in the population, then the DNA sample could have come from somebody else with the same profile as that of the suspect. This, at least, is the theory. In forensic science, however, there are a number of factors that affect the frequency of matches in a reference population.

To determine the frequency of such a DNA profile, it has to be compared with a reference database. That data base is the population DNA profile, which is an estimate made by using the frequencies of individual alleles (or genes) of a population group or subgroup consisting of a big number of individuals belonging to that group. The database usually consists of samples drawn from blood banks with separate databases for major racial and ethnic groups. Analysts then can combine the estimated frequencies of individual alleles to determine the overall frequency of the DNA profile (Thompson, 84 J. Crim. L. and Crim., at 61). The National Research Council of the United States is of the opinion that "it would not be scientifically justifiable to speak of a match as proof of identity in the absence of underlying data that permit some reasonable estimate of how rare the matching characteristics are" (Id. at pp. 6-23). Many courts in the United States using the Frye standard adhere to this point of view, and have excluded evidence of DNA testing that have not been accompanied by a scientifically acceptable profile frequency. In State v. Canter (524 NW2d 763 [1994]), the Nebraska court held that "the calculation of statistical probability is an essential part of the process used in determining the significance of a DNA match. We hold that evidence of a DNA match will not be admissible if it has not been accompanied by statistical probability evidence that has not been calculated from a generally acceptable method" (Ld. at 782-3).

It has been found that there is a significant variability among population subgroups in the frequency of matching alleles. Critics suggest that among major groups in the US, such as Caucasians, Blacks, and Hispanics, the frequency of alleles may differ among various ethnic groups. An expert, W. Thompson, draws two important implications from these: (1) the samples used by forensic laboratories may be unrepresentative of the population in particular locales, or (2) the assumption that the frequency of alleles is statistically independent would be invalid (Thompson, supra id. at 62).

In the US case of People v. Miles (577 NE2d 477 [1991]), the administration  of DNA evidence, declared the court, involves two types of evidence: (1) evidence of a match between the samples, and (2) statistical evidence of a random match. In this case, an Afro-American was charged with rape. As there was a match found by the laboratory between the DNA samples found in the victim's body and that obtained from the defendant, the court ordered the study of the statistical probabilities of a random sample, where the suspect's DNA "is compared to information in Cellmark's Afro-American database to determine the probability of an Afro-American other than the defendant leaving the semen stain on the bedsheet." The database for Afro-Americans at Cellmark consisted of blood samples taken from 200-300 Afro-Americans in Detroit, Michigan. The Cellmark expert later testified that the probability was one in 300,000.

Some courts have concluded that the procedures for making DNA prints under RFLP are generally accepted and they have admitted these in evidence. In the case of People v. Wesley (533 NYS2d 643 [1988]), the handling of the sample and the procedures used for testing, as well as the statistical basis of the match of DNA profiles under the Frye test were challenged by the defendant. When the technician found a match between the DNA samples, the defendant argued that (1) the laboratory procedures, methodology, and quality control were inadequate, and (2) the statistical analysis used to determine whether the two samples match were inadequate. The trial judge concluded that the techniques and procedures used were reliable, and it adopted the following guidelines for taking blood samples; (1) probable cause to believe that the suspect has committed the crime; (2) a clear indication that the relevant material evidence will be found; and (3) the method used to secure it is safe and cally   reliable. On the reliability of population genetics to determine the power of identity of the probes used in the DNA print identification test, it was noted that DNA from 900 unrelated individuals subdivided into three ethnic groups was obtained and successively hybridized to each DNA probe. The allele (or gene) frequency distribution was determined for each group for each locus of the DNA. Utilizing the mathematics of chance and probability with respect to the three ethnic groups separately, the individual power of identity (allele frequency) for each of the four loci or probes used by the testing laboratory in its print identification test were multiplied to arrive at a mean combined power of identity for the four probes for American blacks of one in 1.4 billion and Caucasoids of one in 840 million. The validity of this multiplication procedure presupposes that each fact observed and entered into the calculation is random and independent of the others. The court concluded that at this hearing, the prosecution should be permitted to assert a power of identity limited to a mean power of one in 140 million for American blacks and one in 84 million for Caucasoids.

But some US courts, especially those under the relevancy standard, do not exclude evidence of a match in the absence of statistical back-up. In Commonwealth v. Crews (640 A2d 395 [1994]) it was held that even without statistical conclusions, evidence of a DNA match tended to make the accused's presence more likely than it would have been without evidence, and was therefore admissible as evidence. In Rivera v. State (340 P2d 933 [1992]), and in St v. Schwartz (447 NW2d 422 [1989]), the court allowed forensic experts to testify that two DNA patterns matched and the judge barred experts from presenting statistics to quantify the rarity of matching DNA patterns or the likelihood of a coincidental match.

While quantitative estimates of profile frequency in a given population would be important, the hard fact is that, as the US National Research Council has discovered, "there is no bright-line standard in law or in science that can pick out exactly how small the probability of the existence of a given profile in more than one member of a population must be before assertions of uniqueness (of a DNA profile) are justified" (Id. at 6-25).

In the Philippines, evidence of DNA match will have to be admitted not only out of necessity (since we have not yet put up the needed data base) but also out of liberality of o rules on evidence. Of course, by so admitting, judges should realize that such a "match" does not really provide a unique identification of the perpetrator of a crime. Further, the j should be on guard against human error in the laboratory, which may produce a false positive to incriminate an innocent person. According to Thompson:

"The probability of a false positive has received profiles, although having an accurate estimate of the probability of a false positive may be more important than having an accurate estimate of the frequency of matching profiles, because the rate of a false positive is likely to be much greater than the rate of coincidental matches. Without an accurate estimate of the false positive, one cannot evaluate the probative value of DNA evidence." (Thompson, supra, id. at 91).

The US National Research Council calls for laboratory error rates to be determined based on proficiency testing and disclosed to triers of facts. Accurate estimates of error rates require proficiency tests that are externally administered, blind, and based on samples that are truly representative of case materials (National Research Council, "The Evaluation of Forensic DNA Evidence," p. 22 [1996]).

VII. Weight of DNA Evidence

Assuming that DNA evidence is admissible, it does not mean that the trial court will give it credence wholesale. The fact that the testimony on DNA came from an expert does not render it immune from attack as to its scientific basis or from the quality of the testimony. In the United States, the cases decided by trial courts show that DNA evidence, even when admitted, were questioned or excluded because of poor handling or contamination of the sample, or because     of errors in following laboratory procedures or in analysis in matching, or in the interpretation of the statistical methods for matching, or in insuring the accuracy and the quantitative   interpretation of genetic typing.

So the basic issue in the use of DNA evidence is not really the validity of the scientific theory but the reliability and observance of the scientific procedures designed to implement the theory. This revolves on the ability and competence of lawyers and experts to present scientific evidence effectively and the ability of judges to understand and to draw reasonable inferences from this evidence.

There are a number of grounds that affect the weight of DNA evidence, especially that obtained from RFLP analysis. Among these are (1) inherent error, (2) testing error, (3) laboratory procedure error, and (4) interpretive error. For example:

In sampling, there is always the possibility of substitution or manipulation. For example, in the case again O.J. Simpson, where the prosecution was able to prove that the defendant left a tell-tale trail of blood and blood-smea clothing that stretched from the scene of the killing to the house of Simpson, and it was proven that these matched the blood of the defendant, it was also shown by the defense that the blood samples were brought by the police to the house of the defendant, and then his lawyers capitalized on this by saying that this trail of blood was planted by the police, who earlier obtained samples of the defendant's blood, and later intentionally or accidentally spilled the samples from Simpson on the other blood samples, making the real killer's blood look like Simpson's blood ("How the Jury Saw It," Newsweek, Oct. 16, 1995, p. 20). At the trial of the criminal case, the counsel for Simpson moved to exclude the DNA evidence, not only on scientific and technical grounds, but also on the ground that the blood samples were planted by the biased policemen.

The specimen may be contaminated. This is the curse of the PCR method because of its extreme sensitivity. Contamination of the DNA present in a sample will be amplified and may cover the true DNA of interest. There are many potential sources of contaminating DNA in PCR, including those contributed by the victim, by bystanders, and even the technician (MacKnight, supra, id. at 316). There is also concern that previously amplified DNA will contaminate the test DNA sample as it is being processed (MacKnight, ibid.). In samples that are too old or that have been poorly preserved, for example, the long DNA fragments tend to break into shorter fragments. This may be caused by digestion by enzymes, the presence of chemicals which damage DNA, physical breakage due to shearing, and exposure to ultraviolet rays (Marx, "DNA Fingerprinting Takes the Witness Stand," 240 Science 1616 [1988]). Samples may also be diluted with dirt, water, oil, or even other body fluids coming from other sources. These contaminants appear in most forensic samples because they are commonly red   found on floors, carpets, car seats, and furniture. Even small amounts of contaminants may alter the band pattern of DNA in RFLP analysis. It has been observed by scientists that samples contaminated with DNA from another person may produce a double print in which two DNA patterns are superimposed. This is common in rape cases, where semen may be mixed with other body fluids. And spurious DNA from nonhuman sources may produce false bands in the tests. Other contaminants may broaden the bands, or cause the bands to smear, and this is likely to lead to error in interpretation because the technician would be unable to tell of    which part represents the true band in RFLP analysis (Thompson and Ford, supra, id. at 93, fn. 212, 213). Of course, this alteration of band patterns will not falsely incriminate an innocent person because such will not cause two prints to match, but it will also exculpate a guilty suspect in the absence of other convincing evidence.

We should not also discount contamination with other genetic material in the laboratory, like bacteria, plant or animal secretions, etc.

There is also the question of reliability of testing procedures in the laboratory. This is especially true in the analysis of samples taken from body fluids which can be mixed with other forms of body fluids. In RFLP analysis, in the phase of the Southern transfer, an error in transferring the DNA prints to the membrane may cause certain bands to disappear, creating a different pattern. The use of different restriction enzymes or different genetic probes to make two prints would also produce different patterns. This will also result in exculpating a guilty defendant. Or if the technician makes an error in placing the wrong sample on the gel, or mixes the samples without realizing it, this will result in a print that will let the guilty suspect go free.

During the hybridization phase of the RFLP analysis, the genetic probes may lock onto the wrong DNA sequences if the probes have been degraded due to contamination. Errors in labeling the probe with the wrong radioactive marker, or use of a wrong chemical composition, will affect the probe's ability to lock onto the polymorphic sequences (Thompson and Ford, supra, id. at 95). If the probe DNA is cross-contaminated with the DNA samples, this will produce aberrant bands once the DNA locks onto itself. This will cause a false identification even if just a small amount of probe DNA contaminates the samples or any of the reagents used to analyze the samples. All such samples exposed to the probe will have common bands. Thompson and Ford, supra).

In PCR, there is the problem of the so-called "allelic drop-out," that is, the preferential or differential amplification of alleles where the procedure greatly favors one of the two alleles present in a heterozygous individual such that the results would lead one to conclude wrongly that the individual was homozygous at the locus examined (MacKnight, supra, id. at 314). This may happen if the temperature of the reaction is substantially below 94 degree C which causes selective denaturation (Ibid.).

The defense can also question the quality controls in the laboratory. Each laboratory should have a quality control program to make sure that the quality of the DNA is appropriate to the test; that the enzyme digestion is correct; rent   that there are controls on the quality of fragment separation, the DNA probe, and the data analysis; and that there should be monitored on equipment maintenance throughout the test, and reagent preparation (See People v. Wesley, 533 NYS2d 643, at 665). Results from laboratories that have not undergone blind external proficiency testing are not as reliable as those that have undergone such tests. If results have undergone such testing, the court should assess the finding by looking at the laboratory's false-positive rate of error (Deck, "Prelude to a Miss," 20 V.t L. Rev. 1057 [1996]).

Laboratory controls are more particularly crucial in PCR analysis where carryover contamination is a recurring problem all throughout the testing process. A single PCR reaction produces an enormous number of copies of DNA that can potentially contaminate samples yet to be amplified. Since the number of copies of amplified DNA in a completed PCR reaction is so high, inadvertent transfer of even a minute volume of yet to be amplified sample by splashing or by aerosol may result in the amplification and typing of the contaminating DQa. It is said that PCR is so sensitive that if one vial of blood is opened in proximity to another, the open vial can create an aerosol that can actually contaminate another sample, making the two seem identical (Tyler, "Fundamental Misunderstanding About DNA Contamination: Does It Help or Hurt the Criminal Defendant?" 31 Beverly Hills B.A.J. 15 [1996] 27). It is possible that if a contaminating sample is amplified by PCR to the exclusion of the specimen sample, this can create a false positive which may incriminate an innocent person (Tyler, id. at 29; see also People v. Lee, 537 NW2d 233).

Errors may also come in the interpretation of the results of the test. If this happens, technicians will be substituting their own judgment for that of the trial judge. This subjective judgment of the technicians is crucial. For instance, in RFLP analysis, the bands may come in clear and sharp, but in most cases, they may be fuzzy and hazy. In some instances, there  may be disputes as to whether there is a band or none at all. Laboratory anomalies may produce spurious bands or missing bands. These can degrade the probative value of a match.

In PCR, discrepancies can arise in the reading of dot blots. In order for the test to be deemed "readable," the intensity of color at a test dot must be at least as intense as that of the "control dot." If the control dot is more intense than the other dots, then the results need careful analysis.       This is more true in sexual assault cases, where "mixed evidence" samples are tested with "pure" evidence samples collected from the suspect and the victim. Because each person has two alleles, a maximum of two alleles should be identified in the sample. However, if the sample is mixed, it    is likely that three or more alleles will be detected. If two people have the same DQa type, there will be only two alleles identified, even if the samples are mixed. However, they will also be found to have the same types in the pure samples. The results would mean that the suspect can neither be included nor excluded from the pool of potential suspects based on PCR testing (MacKnight, supra, id. at 319).

In the other method of testing DNA, known as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), there are also a number of grounds to contest the weight of the evidence presented. As in RFLP, there is always the problem of a coincidental match,  and it is more serious under this testing method. A               coincidental match will depend on the number of allele systems for which they match, and whether these systems are independent of each other. There is also the possibility of an erroneous call or wrong declaration by the lab technician. PCR consists of a series of dots which indicate the presence of specific alleles. Some factors can blur the distinction between a positive call and a negative call if the dots are very faint, or the dots may be difficult to distinguish in a dark area, which can occur in an X-ray film. This is caused by sloppy procedures in PCR testing. And if there is contamination of samples, this will cause a dot to appear where it should not, or to disappear where it should appear. If the spurious appearance or disappearance of a dot will cause a match between patterns from two persons, an innocent suspect could be falsely incriminated. But if it hides a match between DNA patterns coming from one person alone, a guilty suspect could be exonerated. Even if just a small amount from the suspect's DNA contaminates the PCR reaction, the result will incriminate the suspect. Other errors are common to both RFLP and PCR analyses, like contamination of probes, adding the wrong probe, or putting e test samples in the wrong slots, etc. (Thompson and Ford, pra, id. at 99).

These problems in DNA profiling show how dangerous it is to just base the conviction of a suspect on DNA evidence alone. In People v. Castro (545 NYS2d 985 [1989]), the court excluded evidence because the laboratory hired to conduct the DNA test did not even follow its own procedures. In this case, the defendant was accused of two counts of murder, being alleged that he stabbed to death a woman tenant and her two-year old daughter. A wrist watch owned by the defendant was seized. What appeared to be bloodstains on the watch was noted by the detectives, but the defendant said that the blood was his own. The prosecution, intending to prove that the bloodstains were those of the adult victim and not those of the defendant, sought to introduce evidence of the DNA identification tests. While the panel of prosecution experts proved that there was a match between the samples by using scientifically accepted tests and techniques, the defense, in a piercing attack upon each DNA molecule presented, demonstrated that the testing laboratory failed to conduct the "sex test" to determine whether the sample came from a male or a female. The court said that the sex test as a form of control should be used in forensic cases when the sex of the test DNA's donor is unknown. The court thus ruled that the DNA evidence was inadmissible as a matter of law.

IX. Constitutional Issues in Obtaining Evidence

A. Privilege against self-incrimination

Under our Constitution, "no person shall be compelled to be a witness against himself" (Art. III, sec. 17). The rationale for this guarantee are (1) public policy and (2) humanity (U.S. v. Navarro, 3 Phil. 143 [1903]). "Of policy, because if the party were required to testify, it would place the witness under the strongest temptation to commit perjury; and of humanity, because it would prevent the extorting of confessions by duress" (id. at 148).

As early as 1903, our Supreme Court had already enshrined the privilege by striking down as unconstitutional a provision of the Spanish Penal Code which penalized any person "who illegally detains another and fails to give information concerning his whereabouts, or does not prove that he set him at liberty" (Art. 483. par. 2, Spanish Penal Code). Our Supreme Court, invoking the Philippine Bill of 1902 which carried the privilege, held that "this provision of law has the effect of forcing a defendant to become a witness on his own behalf or to take a much severer punishment" and therefore violated the privilege (People v. Navarro, supra).

This issue of violation of the privilege against self-incrimination crops up in the method of obtaining evidence in the form of DNA samples from the person of the suspect. For even if samples are available at the scene of the crime, this is meaningless if it is not compared with samples taken from the body of the suspect. While a blood or serum test may be considered a "search" under the search and seizure clause of our Constitution, the compulsory obtaining of blood samples from the body of a suspect does not violate the privilege against self-incrimination. While our Supreme Court has rigidly adhered to the strict command of the privilege against self-incrimination, it has always ruled that the privilege is applicable only to testimonial evidence. The right is simply a prohibition against legal process to extract from the defendant's lips, against his will, an admission of guilt, held by the Court in U.S. v. Tan Teng (23 Phil. 145 [1912]). This case, which was a prosecution for abusos deshonestos, involved the use of substance taken from the sex organ of the defendant, which showed that he had gonorrhea like that of the complaining girl, who was also infected with the disease. During the trial, the defendant had objected to the admission of the finding of the Bureau of Science that he was suffering from gonorrhea, invoking his privilege against self-incrimination, but the Supreme Court held that the privilege is just a prohibition of the use of physical or moral compulsion to extort communication from the defendant, not an exclusion of evidence taken from his body when it may be material (Id. at 150).

This doctrine was followed in later cases. Thus, the Supreme Court held that forcing an accused to discharge morphine from his mouth (U.S. v. Ong Siu Hong, 36 Phil. 735 [1917)), or forcing a woman accused of adultery to submit her body, against her will, for examination by a physician to determine if she is really pregnant (Villaflor v. Summers, Phil. 62 [120]), does not violate the privilege against self-incrimination. The rationale given by the Court for the privilege is self-defeating: it stated that while this case "is a shock to one's sensibilities, we must nevertheless enforce the constitutional provision in accord with the policy and the reason thereof," which is that "the constitutional protect the innocent (id. at 69.) With this kind of reasoning, the exclusionary rule in evidence would be thrown overboard by the Court. This goes against the grain of the constitutional principle that its provisions protect the innocent and the guilty alike. Other cases also hold that coercing the accused to place his left foot over a footprint for purposes of comparison (U.S. v. Zara, 42 Phil. 308 [1902]), requiring a witness to put on a pair of trousers to determine its ownership or conducting a paraffin test on the accused (People v. Contadora, 47 O.G. 6156), or requiring the accused to submit to a footprinting test (U.S. v. Salas, 25 Phil. 337 [1913]) are not violative of the privilege against self-incrimination. The Supreme Court recently had occasion to clarify that the privilege covers only evidence which is "communicative in nature" taken under duress (People v. Olvis, G.R. 71092, Sept. 30, 1987). However, these general doctrines on the privilege against self-incrimination will have to be dovetailed with the law on searches and seizures.

B. Right against unlawful searches and seizures

The more formidable challenge against the compulsory obtention of DNA samples from a suspect comes from the search and seizure clause of our Constitution. The guarantee provides:

"The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects against unreasonable searches and seizures of whatever nature and for any purpose shall be inviolable, x x x." (Art. I , sec. 2)

In a leading case that involved the interplay of these two guarantees against the backdrop of the due process clause, the US Supreme Court ruled that the taking of blood samples through compulsion from the body of a driver who was arrested for drunken driving did not violate any of these guarantees. On the question as to whether the procedure compelled the accused "to be a witness against himself," the Court held that the privilege "offers no protection against compulsion to submit to fingerprinting or photographing, or measurements, to write or speak for identification, to appear in court, to stand, to assume a stance, to walk, or to make a particular gesture x x x. The privilege is a bar against compelling 'communications' or 'testimony,’ but that compulsion which makes a suspect or accused the source of 'real or physical' evidence does not violate it" (Schmerber v. California, 384 US 757 [1966]). The blood test evidence, although an incriminating product of compulsion, was neither petitioner's testimony nor evidence relating to some communicative act or writing by the petitioner, and it was not inadmissible on privilege grounds, the Court concluded.

But the U.S. Supreme Court, while adhering to the same doctrine, underlined the basic value behind the privilege, and invalidated a compulsory procedure of yielding physical evidence that is shocking to the sensibilities of civilized society. It held that forcing a defendant to regurgitate capsules he had swallowed by pumping his stomach violates the right against self-incrimination. Rochin v. California (324 U.S. 165 [1952]) was a case where the police officers, having information that Rochin was selling narcotics, went to his residence and entered the premises illegally. They found him partially clothed, sitting on the side of the bed, and saw two capsules lying on the nightstand. When asked, "whose stuff is this?" referring to the two capsules, Rochin reached for the pills and swallowed them. After efforts by the officers to force the suspect to regurgitate the capsules failed, they took him to a hospital where they ordered his stomach to be pumped. A report confirming that the capsules contained morphine was used to convict the defendant. When the case was appealed to the Supreme Court, the latter reversed the decision, holding that "we are compelled to conclude that the proceedings by which this conviction was obtained do more than offend some fastidious squeamishness or private sentimentalism about combating crime too energetically. This is conduct that shocks the conscience. Illegally breaking into the privacy of the petitioner, the struggle to open his mouth and remove what was there, the forcible extraction of his stomach's contents - this course of proceeding by agents of government to obtain evidence is bound to offend even hardened sensibilities. They are methods too close to the rack and the screw to permit constitutional differentiation."

This ruling is a watershed for the trend of thought behind the privilege, for it emphasizes that the basic value behind the provision is respect for the individual, not the search for truth as the Philippine Supreme Court had put it. The ultimate value underpinning the rights of the accused is not really the discovery of truth, but the upholding of human dignity, regardless of the guilt of the suspect. With more reason should this ruling be applicable in the Philipp context, because the provision of our Constitution prohibiting unreasonable searches and seizures is cast in stronger terms than that of the US Constitution. While the latter merely proscribes "unreasonable searches and seizures," our Constitution contains the blanket qualifying clause "unreasonable searches and seizures of whatever nature and for any purpose." Thus, while the American constitutional provision has been construed in some instances not to cover certain administrative searches and seizure (e.g., home visitation by caseworkers to insure compliance with state regulations by persons receiving welfare payments (Wyman v. James, 400 US 309 [1971)); inspections to assess compliance with federal health and safety standards under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 (Marshall v. Barlow's Inc., 436 US [1978]); inspection of stone quarries by federal mine inspectors under the Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977 (Donovan v. Dewey, 452 US 594 [1981]), such administrative searches and ing   seizures may not be allowed under a strict construction of our Bill of Rights.

However, no definitive case has been decided by our Supreme Court interpreting the new qualifying clause to our provision against unreasonable searches and seizures. But it seems clear that "fishing expeditions" for DNA fingerprints would fall under the prohibition. A "fishing expedition" is exemplified in the English case of Regina v. Pitchfork (The Times, London, Jan. 23, 1989).

In this case, after the rape and murder of a 15-year old girl in Leicestershire, England in 1983, what the police did was to go to every residence in the nearby villages and ask male residents aged 13 to 34 to perform blood tests so that they could compare these with blood tests on semen recovered fro a vaginal swab of the victim. Three years later, another 15-year old girl suffered the same fate in an adjacent village. The police again embarked on a "voluntary" blood testing among all male residents of three villages, and they made "house calls" on any male who failed to appear for blood testing. A resident named Colin Pitchfork was afraid to give blood because he had a criminal record for "flashing," so he convinced another to give a sample using Pitchfork's name and identity card. He received a clearance based on the negative result. But 5 months later, the substitute talked and the police got wind of it. Pitchfork was arrested and confessed to both murders.

The general rule is that the obtention of samples from a suspect like hair follicle, blood, semen, and other fluids, even against his will, does not violate his right against self-incrimination, for the right against self-incrimination covers only communicative evidence. The qualification of the general rule is that in obtaining samples from the suspect, the degree of invasion of privacy and the methods used must not be offensive to the sensibilities of a civilized society; otherwise, it would be considered unconstitutional. This rules out fishing expeditions. Cases decided by the US Supreme Court list some factors to determine "reasonableness" of a search or seizure: the government and the privacy interest should be weighed in the balance to determine the need for an exception to the warrant requirement, discounted by the identified privacy interest.

It is important to note that if compulsory procedures are used to obtain DNA samples from the body of a suspect, there must be either probable cause or an arrest warrant, as provided in the constitutional provision. This is because obtaining a blood sample, a hair follicle, or semen from a suspect requires a serious intrusion on his personal security and dignity, not to mention his freedom. In one federal case, the US Supreme Court held that, without a warrant or probable cause, the police cannot force a person to go to the police station for fingerprinting as that would be tantamount to arrest (Hayes v. Florida, 470 US 84 [1985]). While the Court declared that it may be constitutional to seize a person on less than probable cause for fingerprinting, the prohibition against unreasonable searches and seizures is triggered if police activity becomes too long in duration. However, it was earlier held by the same Court that where a suspect is found to have blood or skin residue under his fingernails, and the police have probable cause to believe that such evidence is incriminating, the exigency of the circumstances dispenses with the necessity of obtaining a warrant (Cupp. v. Murphy, 412 US 291[1973]). This case involved a defendant who telephoned the police that his wife died of strangulation, after which he voluntarily went to the police station for questioning. Soon after he arrived at the police station, a policeman noticed a dark spot on his finger and, believing that the dark spot might be blood and knowing that evidence of strangulation is often found under the fingernails of the assailant, the police requested the defendant that they be allowed to take a sample of scrapings from his fingernails. At this time, the defendant was not under arrest, although he was detained and there was probable cause to believe that he had committed the murder. The defendant refused the request, but the police, over the defendant's objection, took samples , which turned out to contain traces of skin and blood cells and fabric from the victim's nightgown, admitted in evidence during the trial. On a habeas corpus application to the Supreme Court, the latter held that "considering the existence of probable cause, the very limited intrusion undertaken incident to the station house detention, and the ready destructibility of the evidence, we cannot say that this search violated the Fourth and the 14th Amendments."

In Schmerber v. California, supra, where a blood sample was taken by compulsion from the body of the defendant who was arrested for drunken driving, where it was contended that the chemical analysis should have been excluded from the evidence as the product of an unlawful search, the Court noted that delay in obtaining a warrant threatened the destruction of the evidence, that the police officer who arrested him was faced with an emergency, that the test was performed in a reasonable manner by a physician in a hospital according to accepted medical procedures and without risk of infection or pain, and it concluded that there was no violation of the guarantee against unreasonable searches and seizures.

The US Supreme Court, however, has lowered the "probable cause" standard laid down in earlier cases to "individual suspicion" (Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1[1968]) and even lower to the "special needs test" (Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives Association, 489 US 602 [1989]). In the Terry case, the Court held that a "stop and frisk" made by a police officer on suspicion that the suspects were casing a store preparatory to robbery and to search for hidden weapons was a "reasonable search" under the Fourth amendment. This kind of police conduct is less intrusive than an arrest, according to the Court, thus implying that "reasonable cause" can be replaced at times by a balancing test. In that case the Court further said that the policeman must be able to point to specific and articulable acts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion. It was pointed out that the government’s interest in crime prevention satisfied the requirement and outweighed the suspect's individual interest.

By shifting to a balancing test, the courts can use the less stringent "individualized suspicion" or even dispense with it. It can be seen in later cases that the US Supreme Court did exactly just that. In the Skinner case (supra), the Court stated that "individualized suspicion" is not the constitutional floor below which a search must be deemed unreasonable." In limited circumstances, where the privacy interests implicated by the search are minimal, and where an important governmental interest furthered by the intrusion would be placed in jeopardy by a requirement of individualized suspicion, a search may be reasonable despite the absence of such suspicion," said the Court (Skinner case, supra, id. at 624). Here the Court upheld the constitutionality of a mandatory blood and urine testing of all railroad employees involved in certain accidents. In a companion case (National Treasury Employees Union v. Von Raab, 489 US 656[1989]), the Court likewise validated the mandatory drug testing of all US customs service employees who had applied for positions that required them to carry firearms. In this case the Court dispensed with the probable cause requirement before searching an employee, as it felt that the government's need to conduct suspicionless searches outweighed the privacy interests of the employees.

From such pronouncements it is just a short step to suspicionless blood or serum testing for matching DNA evidence. The government's interest in crime investigation will always be held to outweigh the privacy rights of an individual. The rampant cases of rape, murder and paramount governmental interests as against isolated individual rights. This may even involve fishing expeditions and saturation drives usually conducted among residents of slums and squatter areas. In cases where the investigation has begun to focus on a suspect, the standard of "individualized suspicion" comes in handy to justify intrusion on an individual's privacy.

Yet even if the right to privacy is described as “a broad abstract, and ambiguous” concept (Black, dissenting in Griswold v. Connecticut, 381 US 479, at 509 [1965]), it is, nonetheless, a legitimate one (Ibid.). It contains a component consisting of the capacity to maintain aspects of one’s life apart from public awareness. It was Charles Fried, former Solicitor-General of the US, who defined this right as "the control we have over information about ourselves" (Fried, Harv. L. Rev. [1968]). The trend in the jurisprudence of search and seizure, in the US as well as in the Philippines, has shifted the rationale for the protection in the Constitution against unreasonable searches and seizures from property rights to privacy. The search and seizure clause "protects people, not places," declared the US Supreme Court in the leading case of Katz v. US, 389 US 347 [1967]). "What a person knowingly exposes to the public, even in his own home or office, is not subject to Fourth Amendment protection, but what he seeks to preserve as private even in an area accessible to the pub may be constitutionally protected" (Id. at 350351). The constitutional provision protects the privacy and sanctity of the person himself (Bernas, The Constitution, 1987 ed., p. 85)

In their seminal work on the right to privacy, Warren and Brandeis noted that "recent inventions and business methods call attention to the need for the protection of the person, and for securing to the individual... the right to be let alone (Warren and Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy," 4 Harv. L. Rev. 190 at 195 [1890]). In the case of Whalen v. R (429 US 589 [1977)), the US Supreme Court recognized the e of the threat to informational privacy: "We are not unaware of the threat to privacy implicit in the accumulation of vast amounts of personal information in the computerized data banks or other misuse of government files . . . much of which is personal in character and potentially embarrassing or harmful if disclosed" (id. at 605). In his concurring opinion Justice Brennan noted that there are limits on both the type of information that the state may gather and the means by which this may be gathered" (id. at 607).

Since a DNA profile is unique to an individual, and since a person's genetic data can be determined directly, the right to informational privacy crops up in the gathering of DNA profiles. DNA profiling may be considered stigmatizing, if samples are taken only from suspects in criminal investigations or from litigants in paternity suits. And the way in which information from DNA is used would have an adverse effect on a person's ability to get employment,  insurance, credit and other valued benefits in our society. would also affect an individual's personal freedom, since disclosure of personal information can have a negative effe on a person's sense of autonomy and human dignity. This is because DNA profiles may include medical and genetic data which the individual may want to withhold from the public, like evidence of disease, genetic disorders, and identification of behavioral and mental characteristics like the genetic bases of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or Huntington's disease. Scientists speculate that some probes used in forensic science locate alleles that lie near a disease locus; thus there may be an association between the sample DNA and the disease locus. According to the Office of Technology Assessment of the US Congress, "the possibility exists to test DNA acquired specifically for identification purposes for disease information and to include that information in the database" oe (OTA, id. at 132). If so, DNA information can be used not only for litigation in civil and criminal cases but also for employment, insurance, and security purposes. Thus, informational privacy concerns acquire some urgency, especially because, as time goes on, the number and types of probes for genetic disorders, which indicate mental disorders and aberrations, increase.

Privacy concerns will raise issues on (1) what information should be stored; (2) how it should be stored; (3) who will have access to information. To protect such concerns, a DNA database should include only information that reveals an individual's identity and nothing more. Such information will be similar only to that gathered from fingerprints, or from a tax information number, or from a passport.

C. Due process rights of the accused

There are a number of due process concerns that are raised in connection with DNA profiling. On due process, It   our Constitution provides:

"In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall be presumed innocent until the contrary is proved, and shall enjoy the right to be heard by himself and counsel, to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him, to have a speedy, impartial and public trial, to meet t witnesses face to face, and to have compulsory process to secure the attendance of witnesses and the production of evidence in his behalf" (Art. IV, sec. 14[2]).

The presumption of innocence is sometimes invoked against the obtention of DNA samples from suspected criminals. It is contended that obtaining DNA samples from a suspect creates a presumption of guilt. The presumption of innocence protects individuals from having to prove their innocence when the state decides to start a criminal investigation. The character of a DNA profile as a unique identifier of suspect in certain crimes, like rape and murder, even when no other physical evidence is available, gives rise to apprehensions of violation of the constitutional presumption. This is especially true of repeat offenders whose DNA profiles have been taken and stored in the investigation of the first offense or when they were already in jail. Some states in the US require convicts to give their DNA samples as a precondition to probation or release.

One of the more important aspects of procedural due process that crops up in the use of DNA evidence in criminal trials is the right of the accused to prepare for trial (See People v. Valte, 43 Phil. 907; Montanilla v. Arellano, 89 Ph 434). Under this right, the accused, besides being allowed time to prepare for trial, must be given adequate freedom of action to access evidence (Arnault v. Pecson, 87 Phil. 418 [1950]). The use of DNA evidence involves complex technical, scientific, and statistical questions containing voluminous reports, documents, and materials unavailable to the suspect or his counsel. The requirements of due process dictate that he or his counsel should have an opportunity to examine all papers, reports and documents before trial so that these could be given to another expert for an independent review. To withhold any such relevant materials would violate due process. Thus, in US v. Yee (129 FRD 629), the court allowed discovery of matching criteria, environmental studies, population data, and proficiency tests since these are predicate materials essential to the defense which must rebut DNA evidence. The same ruling has been followed in several state cases in the US (see People v. Davis, 601 NYS2d 174 [1993]; State v. Feldman, 604 A2d 242 [1994]). Where a laboratory disclosed its protocol, laboratory notes, autoradiograph, and frequency tables in a discovery proceeding, but refused to supply more specific information on its methodology and database, the court excluded its s findings as evidence, stating that access to the data, methodology, and actual results is crucial for an independent expert review (State v. Schwartz, 447 NW2d 422 [1989]).

In fact, American courts have gone so far as to rule that nse,    not only does the defendant have the right to examine the evidence and test results of the prosecution, but further, that this right includes the right to test or retest the DNA sample in the prosecution's control (US v. Butler, 988 F2d 537 [1993]). Some state courts in the US construe their discovery rules to include mandatory retesting of DNA samples. This right to retest is considered a constitutional right under the due e.g. process clause. (Moore v. State, 748 P2d 732 [1987]). If the sample has been consumed, the prosecution is obliged to preserve as much documentation as possible to allow a full and fair examination of the results by a defendant and his experts (State v. Thomas, 421 SE2d 227 [1992]). Where retesting was refused, the accused must be protected by in affording him to fully cross-examine the prosecution's expert (Frias v. State, 547 NE2d 809; People v. Bell, 253 NW2d 726 [1977]). One authority on evidence proposes that proficiency testing of laboratories by an independent external agency be made a prerequisite to admissibility of DNA evidence (McCormick on Evidence, 4th ed., p. 875). A related issue here is whether or not a defendant's counsel who has acquired control of a DNA test or retest would be required to introduce this as evidence if such results were adverse to this client's cause. This issue has been answered by a state court in the negative, saying that to require the defendant to do so would violate the right to counsel guarantee in the constitution (State v. Melvins, 382 A2d 925 [1978]; Prince v Supreme Court, 10 Cal. Rep. 2d 855 [1992]).

Another' due process issue is the defendant's right to access expert witnesses. The introduction of DNA evidence calls for the testimony of not just an expert, but of many experts. A laboratory technician is needed to testify on the process, the protocol, and the procedures followed in testing. A molecular biologist must testify on the results of the tes A population geneticist must testify on the estimate of population frequencies and the estimate procedures. In a country like the Philippines where 95% of criminal defendants in capital crimes are lower class individuals, the problem is how they can have access to all these experts. Will the government provide for the assistance of experts to defendants in much the same way that it appoints lawyers to help the indigent accused? If we are to follow the lead of the US Supreme Court, the answer would be yes. In Ake v. Oklahoma (470 US 68 [1985]), the defendant, who was charged with a capital offense, relied on insanity as a defense and sought the assistance of a psychiatrist. The trial court refused to appoint one, but the Supreme Court reversed, holding that the constitutional guarantee of due process required that indigent defendants be provided with funds to retain suitable experts for their defense.

Some state courts have followed this ruling with regard to DNA evidence. One court has ruled that it is violative of due process not to provide an expert to refute the testimony of a witness on DNA so as to test the samples independently, to question whether the results showed a match or to explain that scientific opinion may be divided (Dubose v. State, 662 So2d 1189 [1995]). The Florida court held that the trial court abused its discretion under a statute by denying a defense to    request even though there was no showing of a specific need for an expert (Cade v. State, 658 So2d 550 [1995]). Another court has proposed criteria as to whether the state should provide a defendant a counter-expert or not, like (1) the importance of the scientific issue, and (2) how much help the expert can give to the resolution of the issue (Husske v. Commonwealth, 448 SEd 331 [1994]). Other state courts, however, have denied the defendant's request for an independent expert, distinguishing the issue in the Ake case g.    cited above with that in the trial court, the state court holding that the Ake case was concerned with the expertise of a very subjective nature, but the DNA results involve precise physical measurements by an independent laboratory, and the defendant had not made any showing of what the e     laboratory might have done inaccurately (Harrison v. State, 644 NE2d 1243 [1995]). This distinction made by the court here seems to rest on a shaky premise. Issues as to whether the laboratory followed its own procedures in testing, whether such procedures are adequate to reduce the risk of error, whether its controls are adequate to insure accuracy of results, what are its standards for declaring errors and for nse   determining the probability of a coincidental match and for finding out the relevant population data, and its standards for record keeping, are all subjective issues which can be threshed out only in an exchange of opinions between experts speaking on the same wave length. As one commentator puts it, the circumstances which have     prompted courts to deny defense requests for experts' services in ordinary cases should not apply to DNA evidence, first, because this evidence is almost always a pivotal issue as it goes directly to the issue of guilt or innocence; second, the only way to show a lack of general consensus in the scientific community is to have a defense expert testify; third, cross-examination is rarely effective with a complex technical subject if the lawyer is unaided by an expert; and fourth, a defense counsel who does not hire an expert would be rendering ineffective assistance of counsel tantamount to deprivation of the right to counsel (Fontg, "Comment: DNA Fingerprinting: A Guide to Admissibility and Use,' 57 Mo. L. Rey. 501 [1992]).

Summing Up

DNA is a very powerful tool which can be used in the investigation and detection of crime. As such, it can be used as hard evidence either for the prosecution or for the defendant. And the methods which test and analyze DNA are becoming more sophisticated and sensitive with new developments. These methods involve science, statistics and issues in population genetics which are very complex and technical, and which may even be beyond the ken of judges. Even among scientists, there is considerable disagreement on how a DNA profile should be read and interpreted. And the potential for error in the laboratory is difficult to estimate, especially in a country like ours where our equipment and facilities for testing are obsolete; and experts in biology, genetics, statistics and biochemistry are hard to find even in the urban areas.

These give rise to problems of admissibility and weight of DNA evidence, not to mention basic constitutional issues. The unique method of obtaining DNA from the body of a criminal suspect is fraught with constitutional issues of privacy, self-incrimination, and due process. In the Philippines, DNA evidence will continue to be on trial, both as to admissibility as well as weight, until we develop atru scientific culture not only in our laboratories but also in courts.
