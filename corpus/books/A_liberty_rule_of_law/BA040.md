# A Centenary of Justice -- Ch. 20: Social Weather Stations v. Comelec -- May Election Surveys Be Banned?

Chapter 20, the final chapter of 'A Centenary of Justice,' analyzes Social Weather Stations v. Comelec (GR 147571, May 5, 2001, per Mendoza, J.), which struck down Section 5.4 of RA 9006 (the Fair Elections Act) -- the ban on publishing election surveys within 15 days (national) or 7 days (local) of an election -- as 'an unconstitutional abridgment of freedom of speech, expression and the press.' CJP frames the case as a sequel to his own ABS-CBN v. Comelec ponencia (which protected exit polls), confirming the Court's capacity to meet 'new paradigms of free expression.' The Court reasoned that the ban is a prior restraint; that the Comelec's power is limited to ensuring 'equal opportunity, time, space, and the right to reply'; and that the ban fails the O'Brien test, because its interest is 'not unrelated to the suppression of free expression' -- it silences an entire class of speech (surveys) while letting columnists and commentators opine on the same races -- and is broader than necessary. Justice Melo concurred (prior restraint bears a heavy presumption of invalidity; the possibility of abuse cannot justify suppression); Justice Puno concurred (the ban fails clear-and-present-danger and is overbroad, since 'there are less drastic means'); and CJP concurred, restating that free expression is a 'preferred' right limitable only by a clear and present danger the Comelec never showed, and that 'the general interest of the state in insulating voters from outside influences is insufficient.' Justice Kapunan dissented, reframing the issue as free speech versus the constitutional guarantee of 'equal access to opportunities for public service' and defending Section 5.4 as a permissible legislative response to the 'bandwagon effect' -- a 'self-fulfilling prophecy' aggravated by misinformation -- noting that many countries impose similar bans and that the need for regulation is for Congress, not the Court, to judge. CJP closes by explaining that the ABS-CBN dissenters voted to strike down the ban here because their earlier dissents rested only on mootness.

## Notable Anecdotes

### Anecdote 1

Surveys vs. columnists: the Court's decisive point was that the ban suppressed a 'whole class of expression' (surveys) while leaving 'newspaper columnists, radio and TV commentators, armchair theorists, and other opinion makers' free to opine on the very same races -- exposing a 'bias for a particular subject matter.'

### Anecdote 2

SWS as Exhibit A for overbreadth: Justice Puno noted that the ban swept so broadly it would silence even Social Weather Stations -- 'one of our more reliable... social research institutions with a no-nonsense record' -- without distinguishing scientific surveys from sloppy or misleading ones.

### Anecdote 3

The bandwagon argument: dissenting, Justice Kapunan explained the 'bandwagon effect' as a 'self-fulfilling prophecy' -- a survey 'lulls the voter into thinking that the election is over but the counting' -- and catalogued the many countries (Turkey and Luxembourg at 30 days, South Africa at 42, down to one-day bans) that restrict pre-election surveys.

### Anecdote 4

The mootness footnote: CJP explains that Melo, Vitug and Mendoza, who had dissented in ABS-CBN, voted to strike down the ban in SWS -- because their ABS-CBN dissents rested only on the ground that the case was decided after the election and so was 'technically moot,' an argument the ABS-CBN ponencia rejected because free-expression implications 'transcend the past election.'
