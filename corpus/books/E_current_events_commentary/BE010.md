# With Due Respect (Vol. 3) -- Ch. 8: Jolting Assault on JBC

# Jolting Assault on JBC
Date:       2009-08-02
Publisher:  Philippine Daily Inquirer (Opinion)
Source:     With Due Respect (2011)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban


As a retired Chief Justice and ex-officio chair of the Judicial and Bar Council (JBC), I resolutely protest Malacañang's assault on the JBC's integrity and independence. The JBC gauntlet took the form of a jolting letter, signed by Executive Secretary Eduardo Ermita, returning without any legal justification the council's list of nominees from which the President is mandated to fill up the two vacancies in the Supreme Court.

**Selection of justices**

Under the constitution, “the Members of the Supreme Court and judges of the lower courts shall be appointed by the President from a list of at least three nominees prepared by the Judicial and Bar Council for every vacancy... Any vacancy shall be filled within 90 days from the occurrence thereof."

To fulfill this mandate, the JBC-after a protracted screening process-sent the Palace, many weeks ago, a list of six nominees, three for each of the two vacancies caused by the retirement of Justices Ma. Alicia Austria-Martinez and Dante O. Tinga. Clearly then, the JBC has done its constitutional duty.

The nominees are no pushovers. They include four senior career jurists and two private lawyers; namely Court of Appeals Justices Martin Villarama Jr., Hakim Abdulwahid, and Mariano del Castillo, Sandiganbayan Justice Francisco Villaruz, Dean Roberto Abad of the University of Santo Tomas College of Law and 1967 bar topnotcher Rodolfo Robles, himself a former law dean. At least two of them certainly deserve elevation to the high court.

By rejecting all of the six, Ermita virtually accused the JBC of not doing its job, an assault on its integrity. By telling the council to add more names acceptable to the Palace, Ermita challenged its independence. The Constitution clearly placed the JBC "under the supervision of the Supreme Court," not under the President.

By returning the list, Malacañang has crossed its constitutional duty to fill up the vacancies within 90 days from their occurrence, considering that Martinez retired on April 30 and Tinga on May 11. If the JBC revises the list and submits it beyond this 90-day period, it would, like the Palace, be violating the Constitution.

**Check and balance**

The JBC was created by the Constitution as a check on or as a limitation to the appointing power of the president. To be faithful to its checking mandate, it must refuse to add more names. During its earlier session when the six were chosen, the JBC had already agreed to submit only six nominees. Should it capitulate now, its raison d'etre of being an independent check on judicial appointments would be completely eroded.

For too long, the council has been criticized for its alleged timidity in screening nominees to the Supreme Court and for its alleged subservience to the Palace. Now is the chance of the JBC to prove its critics wrong and to assert its independence and integrity by refusing to buckle in the face of this jolting gauntlet from the Palace.

The present JBC is composed of Chief Justice Reynato 5. Puno as ex-officio chairman with the following members: Justice Secretary Agnes Devanadera, Sen. Chiz Escudero, Rep. Mat Defensor, plus four full-time members appointed by the President with the consent of the Commission on Appointments, namely: former Dean Amado Dimayuga, retired Justice Regino Hermosisima Jr., lawyer J. Conrado Castro and retired Justice Aurora Lagman. (August 2, 2009)

## Summary

Chapter 8 of 'With Due Respect' (Vol. 3), CJP's August 2, 2009 Inquirer column, is a sharp protest -- written 'as a retired Chief Justice and ex-officio chair of the Judicial and Bar Council' -- against 'Malacañang's assault on the JBC's integrity and independence.' The trigger was a 'jolting letter' signed by Executive Secretary Eduardo Ermita that returned, 'without any legal justification,' the council's nominee list for two Supreme Court vacancies. CJP recalls the constitutional mandate: justices are appointed by the President 'from a list of at least three nominees prepared by the Judicial and Bar Council for every vacancy,' and 'any vacancy shall be filled within 90 days.' The JBC, after 'a protracted screening process,' had sent six nominees -- three each for the seats vacated by Justices Austria-Martinez and Tinga -- a slate of 'no pushovers' (four senior jurists and two private lawyers, including a 1967 bar topnotcher), so it had plainly 'done its constitutional duty.' Ermita's move, he argues, was a double blow: rejecting all six 'virtually accused the JBC of not doing its job,' and demanding names 'acceptable to the Palace' 'challenged its independence' -- even though the Constitution places the JBC 'under the supervision of the Supreme Court, not... the President.' By returning the list, Malacañang also breached its duty to fill the vacancies within 90 days (Martinez retired April 30, Tinga May 11), and CJP warns that a JBC which revised and resubmitted beyond that window would itself violate the Constitution. Because the council exists as 'a check on... the appointing power,' it 'must refuse to add more names'; to 'capitulate now' would 'completely erode' its raison d'être. Long faulted for 'timidity' and 'subservience to the Palace,' the JBC now had its chance to 'prove its critics wrong' and 'assert its independence and integrity by refusing to buckle' -- and he names every member so readers know who must hold the line.
