# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Ch. 1: Enter the Bio-Age

PART I

Chapter 1. Enter the Bio-Age

Many years ago, Dr. Christian Barnard thrilled the world with the first successful heart transplant. Even before that, however, kidneys had been routinely excised from healthy donors and surgically implanted in patients with renal failures. Only recently, a Filipino senator was the recipient of a liver transplant from his son.

These astounding surgeries, however, are always hounded by the danger of rejection. The human body is naturally conditioned to expel any foreign object artificially embedded in its loins. Whether it be an organ donated from another human or an artifice made of plastic or titanium, the net response of the body is to reject the foreign even if well-intentioned invader.

Healing Miracles

Via the Biosciences

Would it not be marvelous if, instead of transplanting organs and tissues via complicated surgeries and medical procedures, the heart or the pancreas may be coaxed to repair and regenerate itself internally? This is precisely the thrust of new sciences - to enable our body or any of its many organs and tissues to repair itself or to accept new parts made from its own cells. Now, stem cell research shows credible evidence of lasting cures - not only for defective body organs but also for Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and countless other deadly diseases.

Scientists have amply demonstrated "manufacturing" or "repairing" body parts by means of cells from - either embryonic or adult stem cells —"incubated" from the same person is not only possible but is in fact already being experimented on in many places in the world.

Researchers have also shown that hair-width electrodes attached to the motor cortex of the brain can carry electronic signals detectable by a computer? This phenomenon makes it possible for more sophisticated gadgetry to bypass spinal cord injuries and make paralyzed people move their arms and legs again.

Modification of

Human Conduct

But the biosciences extend beyond the healing of physical diseases. Their incursions into the mental faculties are even more dramatic, because they enhance knowledge of and the ability to manipulate the brain - the source of all human behaviors. For this reason, they are able to influence, if not control, social conduct and human relationships.

By healing diseases, prolonging human life, relieving pain and manipulating behavior, the new sciences can change demographics, population densities and economic indicators. Moreover, by tinkering with human behavior through genetics, they may also trigger major societal and political changes. Indeed, intelligence, crime proclivity and sexuality - three higher-level behaviors with genetic roots - may be enhanced or depressed via manipulations of the brain.

Already, there are brand-name medicines that alter human conduct by re-circuiting neurotransmitters in the brain. Some of these are Prozac, manufactured by Eli Lilly; Zoloft, by Pfizer; and Paxil by Smithkline Beecham. These antidepressants effect miraculous changes in the "most central of political emotions, the feeling of self-worth, or self esteem." In this sense, Prozac is a kind of "happiness pill" for millions of people with aggression or depression syndrome.

Another drug, Ritalin, increases attention span, creates a sense of euphoria and builds up energy levels.' It is useful for restless young people who cannot sit still in classrooms. It nudges them into more socially correct behavior.

I will not exaggerate by advocating that the State should exercise social control through genetic engineering and miracle medicines reminiscent of the Boys from Brazil, but it is equally possible to imagine that mind-altering drugs could be used or even misused by social players other than the State - parents, teachers, employers and others who have an interest in how people behave.

This is not to say that bio-genetics is the be-all and end-all of the human essence. The perennial debate about which is more potent - nature or nurture - is not settled as yet. Not to be overlooked is the ethical and moral issue of whether genetic modification should be undertaken at all. The point for now, however, is that humankind is able to change itself not only by improving the environment, but also by altering genetic make-up. In other words, our uniqueness as individuals is dictated by our genome (the totality of our genetic information) which in turn is affected by nutrition, upbringing, environment and education.

Enhancement of

Food Production

Moreover, biotechnology makes it possible to enhance agricultural products through genetic modification. Already, this scientific advance is evident in the Philippines through successful experiments on hybrid rice and corn, which are claimed to be superior not only in nutrition but also in their easy and short planting requirements. This mutation translates to benefits for both producers and consumers in terms of lower prices and greater product availability.

I realize that the use of genetically altered organisms and foods is still controversial. One set of advocates rejects these products because of the risks they pose to the health of those who ingest them, whether they are people or livestock. On the opposite camp are those who claim that these genetically modified (GM) foods are the answer to the urgent need to feed the burgeoning world population. In the Philippines as elsewhere, this controversy goes on not only in scientific laboratories but also in street marches, legislative deliberations and even in pulpits.

There are basically three science issues in this debate: allergenicity, gene transfer and outcrossing. The first concerns the possible allergic effects GM foods may bring to consumers; the second involves the possibility of gene transfer from GM foods to the cells of the consumers, particularly if antibiotic-resistant genes used in GMOs are transfused; and the third refers to the mixing of genes from GM plants with those "in the wild," such as when GMOs good only as feeds for livestock appear in products for human consumption.

The biotechnological controversy is also relevant to environmental protection. For example, a genetically modified bacterium may be applied to the banks of a river to clean up waste and mining sludge. But after slurping the waste, what would the organisms do to the environment? Will they devastate it and produce a problem more difficult to assess and to solve than the original one?

Judicial Awareness

of the Biosciences

These controversies aside, it is obvious that the gigantic strides in life sciences and life technologies will change human behavior and social interaction. That is certain. These resulting alterations will, in turn, require new modes of governance and, for us in the judiciary, new jurisprudential norms without precedents. For this reason, the Supreme Court has been monitoring these epochal transformations, wrought by biosciences and biotechnologies, and the need to keep our judiciary attuned to them. How has this awareness of the Philippine judiciary come about?

First, the Court invited Dr. Franklin M. Zweig, president of the Einstein Institute for Science, Health and the Courts (EINSHAC), to orient it on the new sciences and how they could affect judicial decision-making. As a participant in the Supreme Court Centenary Lecture Series, Dr. Zweig delivered his talk entitled "Life Technologies and the Rule of Law" on November 14, 2000.

Our guest lecturer explained a brief overview of the “unprecedented rise, expansion and pervasiveness of biological technologies,” which will demand jurisprudential answers with no precedent or analogy. The study of human genetics may show “significant  limitations to human abilities to exercise free will." Thus, the "variations in the degrees of freedom that chemistry confers upon individuals" are likely to provide new defenses in criminal and civil cases. "My genes made me do it," can scientifically become a "respectable claim in criminal justice proceedings.”

On another level, the distinguished lawyer-scientist added that genetic tests might be used to pre-screen appointees or candidates for public office. The possibility is imminent, considering that the new sciences may arguably show a predisposition to Alzheimer's disease, cancer, diabetes or other defects and illnesses.

On the whole, his lecture opened new vistas that may indeed alter our traditional concepts of dispute settlement.

Second, at EINSHAC's invitation, several Supreme Court justices participated in two international “conversations” on biosciences and biotechnologies as they affect the courts. One was held in Kona, Hawaii from June 30 to July 4, 2001; and the other, in Ottawa, Canada from June 17 to 21, 2002. As of this writing, there is a more than even chance that one of these international "conversations" may be held in the Philippines in the foreseeable future, perhaps in a year or two.

During these international gatherings, scientists and academicians well-versed in the new sciences freely conversed with jurists and explained how DNA, as the elemental building block of nature, could be altered to change life-styles and societal phenomena, which in turn would necessitate new jurisprudence. In A Centenary of Justice, which came out in 2001, I included as Chapter 10 the talk I delivered during the Kona round entitled "Ready for the Bio-Age." Likewise, in Reforming the Judiciary, published in 2002, I incorporated, as Chapter 7, "Transparency, the Key to Sound Judgments," my remarks during a panel discussion in the Ottawa meeting.

All in all, these two international meetings provided the Supreme Court delegation with sufficient introductory knowledge that excited and enabled it to appreciate the relevance of the biosciences to the work of the judiciary.

Supreme Court

Decisions on DNA

Third, in Tijing v. Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court (Second Division) for the first time - even if only as an obiter - said that "we now have the facility and expertise in using DNA tests for identification and parentage test.” It, however, cautioned that "being a novel scientific technique, the use of DNA test as evidence is still open to challenge. Eventually, as the appropriate case comes, courts should not hesitate to rule on the admissibility of DNA evidence. For it was said that courts should apply the results of science when competently obtained in aid of situations presented, since to reject said result is to deny progress. Though it is not necessary in this case to resort to DNA testing, in future it would be useful to all concerned in the prompt resolution of parentage and identity disputes."

A year later, in People v. Vallejo, the Court en banc was more definitive in unanimously acknowledging the probative value of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) testing as a means of identification. "When a crime is committed, material is collected from the scene of the crime or from the victim's body for the suspect's DNA. This x x x evidence sample x x x is then matched with the reference sample taken from the suspect and the victim," the Court added.

Then the Court discussed the possible results of DNA testing and its forensic uses, as follows:

"The purpose of DNA testing is to ascertain whether an association exists between the evidence sample and the reference sample. The samples collected are subjected to various chemical processes to establish their profile. The test may yield three possible results:

"1) The samples are different and therefore must have originated from different sources (exclusion). This conclusion is absolute and requires no further analysis or discussion;

"2) It is not possible to be sure, based on the results of the test, whether the samples have similar DNA types (inconclusive). This might occur for a variety of reasons including degradation, contamination, or failure of some aspect of the protocol. Various parts of the analysis might then be repeated with the same or a different sample, to obtain a more conclusive result; or

"3) The samples are similar, and could have originated from the same source (inclusion). In such a case, the samples are found to be similar, the analyst proceeds to determine the statistical significance of the similarity.

"In assessing the probative value of DNA evidence, therefore, courts should consider, among other things, the following data: how the were collected, how they were handled, the possibility of contamination of the samples, the procedure followed in analyzing the  samples, whether the proper standards and procedures were followed in conducting the tests, and the qualification of the analyst who conducted the tests." (Citations omitted.)

In the Philippines, "DNA has been successfully isolated from biological materials such as blood and bloodstains, saliva, semen and seminal stains, vaginal smears/ swabs, tissues and organs, bones and hair with follicles." In trial courts, these specimens have been used in cases - both civil and criminal - involving questioned parentage and identities of suspects.

With the aid of "powerpoint" audiovisuals, I discussed the legal issues arising from the scenario. Thereafter, an open forum and a group discussion followed. Here are the "facts" of the case.

Human Cloning, Genetic Engineering,

Cell-Line Development and Patenting of Genes:

A Fictional Case Scenario

In 1998, Pedro Santos (a Filipino) and Maria Mancini (an Italian) exchanged marital vows in a well-publicized wedding held at the Santuario de San Antonio in Makati. Pedro was a biological software engineer working for a multinational pharmaceutical firm, while Maria was a jeweler who catered to the rich and famous. They lived in a beautiful house in Forbes Park and enjoyed all the blessings and bounties that money could buy. Everything seemed perfect, until they were told by their obstetrician that Maria was incapable of conceiving a child. The young couple was devastated. The revelation caused a lot of frustration, anxiety and pressure on their relationship as husband and wife. As a result, they decided to separate for a while, although the marriage remained.

After zealously researching on the Internet, Maria learned of a new development in reproductive technology. That innovation was known as somatic cell nuclear transfer, otherwise referred to as reproductive cloning. The technology involves the extraction of a cell nucleus containing virtually the entire cell's DNA from a donor's tissue - for example, skin or mammary tissue. This extracted nucleus is then inserted into an egg cell whose original nucleus has been removed. After chemical and electronic stimulation successfully coaxes the reconstituted cell to divide, it can then be implanted into a human uterus.

Amazed and inspired by this new technology, Maria told her husband Pedro of her interest in undertaking this alternative reproductive method. Pedro vehemently opposed the idea and expressed his apprehensions regarding Maria's plan. Wanting so much to have a baby, Maria, accompanied by her best friend, Katrina Bond - a British lady married to Juan de la Cruz, a Filipino physician - went to a fertility clinic in Hong Kong to avail herself of the new reproductive procedure, even against her husband's wishes.

In Hong Kong, Maria submitted herself to pre-implantation genetic tests. The initial testing revealed no known inherited diseases or genetic predisposition for developing any other defects or ailments. After a cell nucleus had been extracted from Maria's skin tissue and inserted into an egg cell that had already been denucleated, the resulting zygote or fertilized egg began to divide. Later, over the vehement objections of Juan, it was transferred to the womb of Katrina who served as a surrogate mother for the developing fetus.

Shortly after the baby was born, Maria and Katrina, together with the cloned child Carmina, returned to the Philippines. They resided in Ayala Alabang, separately from their Filipino husbands. The cloned child was raised jointly by the two women, who decided to live together.

Three years later, an emergency arose. Carmina, now a toddler, was left for a week in the care of Pedro, because Maria and Katrina had to attend a conference in Europe for a work-related convention. There had only been sporadic prior contact between the child and Pedro, and no legal arrangements had been made for custody or child support. On the second morning thereafter, Pedro noticed that the child manifested an unusually large amount of cranial hair loss. He systematically collected hair follicles from the child's bed clothes and pillow during the week that she was in his care. Maria and Katrina returned from their European trip and took the child back from Pedro. They had no idea that he had taken hair and follicle samples from Carmina.

Shortly after this baby-sitting episode, Pedro accepted a position in New Delhi, India, with a major Indian pharmaceutical corporation, IndoPharma. He then relocated to India and immediately assumed the functions of his new job. Working with a computational biologist, a structural biologist and a toxico-geneticist at IndoPharma, he analyzed Carmina's hair follicle cells from carefully preserved samples that he had brought with him from the Philippines. His scientific research team found abundant amounts of a previously unreported protein, which they named Rejectin-1 They demonstrated that it was encoded by a gene located on chromosome 6. This gene had a single-base mutation in Carmina. Private animal studies earlier conducted by IndoPharma had suggested that Rejectin-1 limited progesterone production during pregnancy.

Working with another IndoPharma reproductive technology team, the group found that Rejectin-1 was present in 80 percent of the women tested for spontaneous reproductive rejection. IndoPharma won India and US patents for Rejectin-1 and its gene-precursor. With the drug firm's approval, Pedro and his research colleagues formed an Australian-based company called AustroPharma, the primary business purpose of which was to license Rejectin- 1-producing cell lines for research derived from Carmina's hair follicles, the process for which an Australian patent was granted. AustroPharma was 40 percent owned by IndoPharma; the remaining 60 percent was owned equally by Pedro and Juan.

Two years after its founding, AustroPharma developed a gene therapy procedure to repair Rejectin-1 mutations. The firm obtained from the California Institute of Technology (CalTech) a license for "antisense oligonucleotide," a small piece of DNA that would bind to and neutralize the mutant gene, thereby infecting the cell by means of a lentivirus that carried the DNA "repair kit." CalTech reported this molecule as a successful repair mechanism. With its viral vector in a supportive solution, AustroPharma patented a vaccine named "ARO." The vaccine not only reduced Rejectin-1 levels, but also detected the latter's expression in uterine tissue by micro-array analysis. AustroPharma's confidential laboratory and clinical findings reported that uterine tissues could be fully targeted by one administration of intramuscular ARO. Further research determined that ARO administration substantially reduced implantation rejection among rabbits and mice with induced Rejectin-1 mutations.

On a vacation trip to the Philippines, Pedro decided to visit Carmina. During this visit, he administered the ARO vaccine to the cloned child without the knowledge and consent of Maria and Katrina. A few weeks later, Carmina's hair loss ceased and replacement hair grew. After a year, she was diagnosed by a doctor to be afflicted with B-Thalessemia. Prior to the administration of the ARO vaccine, Maria had secured a life insurance contract for Carmina valued at P50 million, with Pedro as beneficiary.

In the meantime, AustroPharma generated massive income from sales of the cell lines and their derivative products and procedures. With these earnings, it bought Gentox, a laboratory located in the United States and incorporated in the State of Delaware. Gentox had had much experience in applying to the Philippine and the US Food and Drug Administrations (FDAs) for clinical trials of investigational biologics with human subjects. Gentox applied for a phase-one clinical trial permit to test ARO's toxicity on human beings for both infertility treatment and hair restoration purposes. Through Gentox, the Philippine and the US FDAs were provided all of AustroPharma's data. A large number of fertility and in-vitro fertilization clinics at   and dermatologists organizations endorsed the proposal and offered their participation.

In the course of issuing its approval for a phase-one clinical trial, the FDAs posted on their respective websites substantial information regarded by AustroPharma and Gentox to be their trade secrets. Specific, non-patent intellectual property regarding uterine targeting was  disclosed. AustroPharma valued this disclosure at an operating loss of $US 1.5 billion over the next five years. The second disclosure was that a cloned child was the source of the cell line, from which the gene therapy vaccine had been derived. Media frenzy ensued. Legal systems geared up for criminal prosecutions and civil actions, including damage/ injury/liability suits and reviews of administrative decisions by the Philippine and the US FDAs.

The Legal Issues

During the conference, I took up the legal and judicial issues arising from the foregoing fictional case scenario, as follows:

Is a Clone

a "Person"?.

To start with, is Carmina, the cloned child, a "person" in legal contemplation? Or is she merely an "extension" of a human cell and tissue that have evolved into human form?

Under Article 40 of the Civil Code, "birth determines personality." But this determination seems to refer only to a "conceived child." Is Article 40 then not applicable to one who was not "conceived"; that is, one who was not the result of the normal fertilization of a human egg by a human sperm? Since the human clone is first a fetus before being born, is it not entitled to be accorded "birth" and "personality" - qualities that are "favorable to it" under Article 41?

If Carmina is a "person," when was she conceived? How was her "intra-uterine" life determined? Who is her mother? Maria from whose cell she originated? Or Katrina from whose womb she was physically carried, nurtured and born?

Who is her father - Pedro? Juan? Is she legitimate or illegitimate? How can her filiation be established? Will a DNA test sustain her identity and parentage?

If Carmina is merely an extension of an original person, are we prepared to say that a being that is capable of breathing, thinking, talking and working like any other human can be considered merely as an object? Or is she a sister of Maria and therefore, like her, the daughter of Maria's parents, since she possesses the chromosomes of these forebears?

In view of the new science of cloning, are we prepared to classify parents as legal, biological, surrogate or adoptive? ?

Filiation of a

Human Clone

The Family Code provides that the filiation of children may be by nature or by adoption. It also states that children conceived or born during the marriage of the parents are legitimate. If the child is conceived through artificial insemination with the sperm of the husband or a donor, the child shall likewise be considered a legitimate child of the husband and the wife, provided that both spouses authorized or ratified the insemination process in a written instrument executed before the birth of the child. On the other hand, it also mandates that children conceived or born outside a valid marriage are considered illegitimate.

In our case study, the means by which Carmina was born - reproductive cloning - can hardly be described as natural. The procedure utilized for the conception of the cloned child was not artificial insemination, as it did not involve the sperm of Pedro or any sperm for that matter. Neither was there a written instrument, ratifying or authorizing such a reproductive procedure, executed by Pedro or Maria prior to the birth of Carmina. In view of the foregoing, was the conception or birth of Carmina within or outside the validly existing marriage of Pedro and Maria? How about Juan and Katrina?

Another important consideration of the child. Article 15 of the Civil Code states that laws relating to family rights and duties; or to the status, the condition or the legal capacity of persons, are binding upon citizens of the Philippines, even though living abroad. The Philippine law on citizenship adheres to the principle of jus sanguinis. Hence, a child follows the nationality of the parents regardless of the place of birth, as opposed to the doctrine of jus soli which determines nationality or citizenship on the basis of the place of birth. Is Carmina a Filipino? Assuming that both Italy and Britain follow jus soli, may she still be considered a Filipino? Does she possess dual citizenship under our laws?

Parental Authority

Over a Human Clone

After the resolution of these underlying issues come several other civil law questions. Specifically, who should exercise parental authority over Carmina? Who should have custody over her?

Note that under the Family Code, the father and the mother jointly exercise parental authority over their children; but in case of disagreement, the decision of the father prevails. Corollary to this, Article 220 of the Family Code provides that parents exercising parental authority have the right, among other rights, to keep their children in their company.26 Verily, parental authority stands to include the right and the duty to the custody of the child - excepting, of course, only what might otherwise be best for the child's welfare. The paternity issue could become even more complicated if, in a suit for support, the putative father refuses to acknowledge or recognize the child for having been conceived and born without his sperm or his adoption. On the other hand, may the fact of "living together" on the part of Maria and Katrina be characterized as " scandalous" and disqualify them from exercising parental authority over the clone?

Ownership

of Derivatives

As narrated in the facts, Pedro was able to obtain discarded hair follicle samples from Carmina - samples from which were discovered and developed derivative products and procedures. Given this factual scenario, does Carmina have a cause of action to file a suit for damages against Pedro, Juan, IndoPharma, AustroPharma and Gentox for the conversion of her "property" into cell lines and gene therapy protocols and vaccines? How about Maria and Katrina - can they, instead, sue on the premise that the cell lines derived from Carmina, which were presumably Maria's, were unlawfully converted to commercial use by Pedro and by companies involved in the development of the vaccine and of the gene therapy procedures?

Looking at the other side of the contention, can Pedro argue that he is entitled to the hair follicles, considering that Maria and Carmina are his wife and daughter respectively? In this connection, I refer you to the Family Code provision with respect to legal guardianship of the child’s property - both parents exercise legal guardianship even without a court appointment to that effect. And just like the rule on parental authority, the decision of the father generally prevails.

There is also the need to determine whether the discarded hair follicles, the cell lines and their derivative products/procedures are susceptible to ownership under our laws. Will the Civil Code provisions on property and ownership apply to the peculiar circumstances in the case study? Will the hair follicles not be considered as abandoned and thus capable of being owned by the finder? How about the cell lines, can they be deemed abandoned also? Your analyses and conclusions on these points will establish the property rights of the protagonists in our story and determine the course of their respective legal actions.

Causes of Action

for Damages

Insofar as entitlement to damages is concerned, the Civil Code provides that actual or compensatory damages may be awarded to any person suffering pecuniary loss. 03 Likewise, compensable damages include not only the value of the loss suffered, but also the profits that were not realized.13 Did Carmina or Maria or Katrina suffer pecuniary loss or compensable injuries from the acts of Pedro and his associates as would warrant indemnification for actual or compensatory damages? Can a cloned child actually suffer compensable personal injury? Can the three ladies singly or collectively sue for violation of their privacy rights or for damages under Article 26 of the Civil Code?

Assuming arguendo that Carmina does have a cause of action for damages against Pedro, does she have the legal or juridical capacity to sue? Should she be represented by Maria or Katrina, or should Maria or Katrina file the case in their respective personal capacities?

Insurance Subjects

and Beneficiaries

In connection with the insurance policy taken on the life of Carmina, may I pose the following questions: Can a cloned child be the subject of a life insurance policy? Or should the clone be insured as personal property of the donor? Does Maria or Pedro have an insurable interest in the life of Carmina?

One is said to have insurable interest when one has a relationship or connection with the insured; or a concern that one will derive pecuniary benefit or advantage from its preservation and will suffer pecuniary loss or damage from its destruction, termination or injury by the occurrence of the event insured against. Likewise, Section 10 (1) of the interest in one's life and health, spouse and children.

Other questions on the subject of insurance include the following: Was it proper for Maria to designate Pedro as the beneficiary? Can Maria subsequently revoke his designation as such? What if Carmina dies as a result of B-Thalessemia, which she developed after the ARO vaccine was administered to her? Can Pedro still recover the proceeds of the insurance contract? To answer these questions, there is need to consult the Insurance Code.35 Under Section 12 thereof, the interest of the beneficiary may be forfeited if he willfully brings about the death of the insured.

Laws on Patents and

Intellectual Property

Moving on to patents and intellectual property laws, may I pose the following inquiries: Can Pedro apply for a patent to the Rejectin-1 protein, the ARO vaccine and the gene therapy method here in the Philippines? Can Maria, Katrina or Carmina file an action to invalidate or cancel these patents if ever they are acquired? Can Pedro argue that the derivative products and procedures (the ARO vaccine, the gene therapy method, and so on) were merely inspired by the early analysis of Carmina's hair follicles, but were developed mainly through his own industry and scientific research?

Under Philippine law, the right of ownership over intellectual creation is recognized. Specific provisions in the Civil Code and special laws afford the necessary protection against possible infringement thereof. Specifically, Article 721 of the Civil Code provides that scientists and technologists acquire ownership of their discoveries or inventions. Likewise, Article 722 states that such ownership exists even before the patents are granted. On the other hand, Sections 21 and 22 of RA 82933 (the Intellectual Property Code) defines patentable and non-patentable inventions.

Can we now say that ownership of the foregoing patentable products and procedures belongs entirely to Pedro, even when he could not have developed these without the cell lines of Carmina and Maria? Take note that per Section 61 of RA 8293,37 a patent may be cancelled if it is "contrary to public order or morality."

Another related incident with considerable legal consequences is the Philippine Food and Drug Administration's posting on its website of information on the ARO vaccine and its development - information that AustroPharma and Gentox consider trade secrets. Did the disclosure of such secrets amount to an infringement of intellectual property? Can the courts compel the Philippine FDA to compensate AustroPharma and Gentox for their alleged losses?

To be able to answer these questions, it would be advisable to look into the provisions of the new Intellectual Property Code, which has superseded and / or expanded the coverage, the application and some of the provisions of the Patent Law.

Criminal Liability

We now come to the criminal law aspect of our case study. From the given set of facts, Pedro took hair follicle samples from Carmina without her knowledge and consent.

Did this "taking" constitute theft? Can there be theft of abandoned or discarded hair follicles? Was the alleged theft committed against the clone Carmina or the donor Maria? Who would be the complainant? In the first place, can Pedro commit theft against his "daughter" or his wife?

Before you even begin to identify the elements of the crime of theft, you may want to refer to Article 332 of the Revised Penal Code which exempts spouses, ascendants and descendants from any criminal liability for theft, swindling and malicious mischief.

Relevant to the civil and the criminal actions against Pedro are the rules on evidence. Can Maria, the wife, testify against him, given the prohibitions/ disqualifications in Section 22 of Rule 130 of the Rules of Court? Suppose the case is instituted by Maria as the legal representative of Carmina; will these disqualifications and prohibitions still apply?

In administering the ARO vaccine to Carmina, is Pedro liable for physical injuries? Can his act be considered criminal, considering that his intentions were noble, having been done for the benefit of the child? Of particular interest in connection with this inquiry is Article 264 of the Revised Penal Code, which punishes the act of knowingly administering any injurious substance or beverage to another even without any intent to kill.

What if the Carmina dies from the disease developed? Can Pedro be held criminally liable for the resulting death? Would the charge be for murder, homicide or parricide? Is the killing of a clone considered destruction of human life or merely of property?

Even the provisions of our tax laws will be affected. Can cloned children be considered dependents, for which additional exemptions can be claimed by a taxpayer? Can the nucleus derived from a donor's tissue, which is not susceptible of appropriation, be considered as a donation subject to a donor's tax?

Conclusion

Given the wide and deep range of legal issues I have raised, you - the judges-participants - may by now be suffering from an overdose. You may be more bewildered now than when I first started this talk. Consequently, during your group discussions, there will probably be a wide divergence of views and spectrum of theories that you may not be able to reconcile.

However, do not despair or be disheartened, for even the Supreme Court may not have ready answers to the legal dilemmas posed by our case study - at least, not yet. Remember that our purpose in this exercise is to be able to understand the intricacies of the new sciences and to be able to assess the applicability of existing laws and jurisprudence as well as the readiness of our courts to meet the exciting challenges of the dawning bio-era.

To this date, I am unaware of Philippine laws or statutes that have been proposed, much less enacted, to address these legal challenges. In some jurisdictions, current legislations refer only to restrictions on the development of cloning technology and the provision of criminal sanctions for violations of declared prohibitions. Moreover, I have yet to find any local jurisprudence that can directly guide us in the resolution of novel issues on human cloning, genetic research and other life technologies.

Dr. Franklin M. Zweig, in his lecture entitled "Life Technologies and the Rule of Law, "41 explained the dilemma in this wise:

"Imposed on our global society by the unprecedented rise, expansion and pervasiveness of biological technologies, we should anticipate, analyze and deliberate on cases and controversies for which historically we have no precedent, [and] no analogy.”

Indeed, legal issues involved in cloning and stem cell technology are inherently complex. There are many baffling areas where we may not have firm answers as yet. But we must still grapple with the realities of the new bio-sciences and learn to cope with the long-term and far-reaching effects they will have on our legal system and dispensation of justice. After all, under our Civil Code, the silence, the obscurity or the insufficiency of our laws is not an excuse to avoid rendering judgments in appropriate cases.

## Summary

Chapter 1, 'Enter the Bio-Age,' surveys the biosciences and biotechnologies — organ transplants (opening with Christian Barnard's first heart transplant), gene therapy, cloning, stem cells, and DNA — transforming society, and the legal and ethical questions they pose for the judiciary. CJP touches on DNA evidence (People v. Vallejo, Tijing v. Court of Appeals) and intellectual property (RA 8293), arguing the incoming 'bio-age' will test the law as profoundly as the information age.
