# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Ch. 4: The Essentials of Legal Education

Chapter 4

The Essentials of Legal Education

There are a few things in life that senior citizens in their mid-sixties like me relish and enjoy. One of them is the opportunity to relate with young, promising and talented people like all of you tonight. In this light, I wish to thank your affable and illustrious Dean Antonio H. Abad Jr. for inviting me to this gathering. Dean Abad and I finished our legal education in the same University - the best law school on Quezon Boulevard, Manila!

Many, many years ago when most of you were not yet born, we were law partners. I can therefore attest to the brilliance and the high ethical conduct of Dean Abad, whose one and only lifetime inspiration is my comadre, Atty. Marina Dalena-Abad.

Having thus personally known Dean Abad for almost half a century, I can openly tell you that my fervent wish is for him to be a member of the Supreme Court! But he would not hear of it, because his sense of delicadeza would not allow him to sign the usual application forms required by the Judicial and Bar Council from judicial applicants. Indeed, Dean Abad is so sensitive about judicial ethics that avoids talking with me. I attest to and truly appreciate the fact that in spite of our lifetime close professional, personal and family ties, he has never - at any time, anywhere - spoken with me about any matter pending in the courts, especially the Supreme Court. He is someone special who needs no reminder that a magistrate's independence and confidence should always be respected and preserved.

Tonight, we are gathered in this elegant ballroom of the Hyatt Hotel to break bread and to celebrate the 61st anniversary of the Adamson University College of Law. On this occasion of warm felicitations and glorious jubilation, allow me to share with you a few thoughts on a subject matter that constitutes the very cornerstone of every law school in this country - legal education.

In preparing this address, I reviewed the statement of vision, mission and values of Adamson University in general and your College of Law in particular.

I must say that I admire your University's express devotion to be a "leading center for quality education particularly for the socially disadvantaged." I identify most avidly with this philosophy, because during my school days I belonged to the socially disadvantaged. To support myself in elementary and high school, I hawked newspapers, shined shoes and peddled cigarettes to jeepney drivers while they waited for the green light at traffic intersections. During my law school days, I sold bibles to my professors and textbooks to my classmates so I could own one myself as my sales commission.

But let me go back to my topic tonight. In its most simplistic sense, legal education can easily be defined as the teaching of the law. Moreover, it is the formal training that law students undergo in preparation for the bar examinations and their eventual induction to the legal profession. Indeed, the standard by which any law school is judged is the "quality" of legal education that it offers.

To achieve this standard, many law schools have adopted educational frameworks that are geared largely, if not entirely, to the mastery of the law. They emphasize the need to memorize the constitution, the codes and the statute They stress the necessity of repeating judicial doctrines and many times, of Latin maxims they hardly understand.

To my mind, such overzealous focus on pure legal craftsmanship, although advantageous in some respects, tends to neglect the other essential aspects of a legal education. It is important to note that its primary purpose is to turn law students into great lawyers, not into mere walking encyclopedias or computer chips of statutes and decisions.

On this note, let me briefly discuss with you what I consider as the three fundamental paradigms in the training and development of every law student. For convenient reference and recall, I have labeled them as the three Ls of legal education: language, law and life.

Language

First, let me talk about language. It has been said that "knowing the law" is one thing and "speaking the law" is quite another. In my experience as a law practitioner, law professor and jurist, I have encountered many lawyers who, despite their exemplary knowledge of the law, are unable to express themselves in plain and simple English.

A lawyer's deficiency in "linguistic aptitude," so to speak, can easily be detected in poorly drafted pleadings as well as in dismal oral arguments. Effective legal communication requires the mastery of four Cs = correct, complete, clear and concise. "Correct" refers not only to proficiency in law, but also to accuracy in grammar. Nothing is more frustrating than to read or hear ungrammatical presentations of misplaced law. "Complete" means that all relevant issues and aspects of a problem are presented and addressed. "Clear" refers to the use of simple but elegant words, phrases and sentences to express an idea and to make it understandable. We must avoid the use of hackneyed, highfalutin language that merely calls attention to itself and       not to the subject being discussed. "Concise" requires the use of as little time and as few words as we can to express an idea.

The basics of oral and written communication ought to have been learned by law students in their pre-law courses or even as early as in high school. Still, it would be advisable that some relevant reminders in this regard be included in the teaching of every law subject by requiring the use of these four Cs at every opportunity - during classroom recitation, discussion and examination.

Law

As mentioned earlier, mastery of the law is not the endall and be-all of legal education. The ultimate objective is not so much to know the law but to understand its rationale and application. Certainly, there is a vast difference between merely stating the law with utmost precision on the one hand and comprehending its purpose and meaning on the other. Thus, law students should be trained to look into the history and the rationale of statutes, to study the development of jurisprudence on important legal issues, and to think and argue logically on the basis of their analysis of the law as applied to specific factual situations.

Indeed, the measure of the talents of lawyers is how they can invoke a favorable rule of law in one case and provide a substantially meritorious exception in another. To be able to do this, their grasp of the law should transcend mere memory work. It should involve the ability to analyze the interaction of different, yet related, legal provisions as well as their implications to a case under consideration. Clearly then, it is not simply a matter of how much you know about the law but rather how you decipher, interpret and apply it to a given factual milieu.

Indispensably, it is both the duty and the function of law professors to sharpen the minds of their students by asking incisive and probing questions that involve more than just a cursory appraisal of legal principles and concepts. Whether it is a change in the school curriculum or the teaching styles of professors, what is important is for law students to be trained to think critically and independently. By providing them with training modules that test their analytical techniques and logical reasoning more than just the reading comprehension and memory capacity, we can be sure that they will be better prepared for the many different challenges that they may encounter in their profession.

Hence, aside from the typical Socratic method of conducting recitations in class, I would suggest that a free-flowing or an open-forum type of discussion be conducted from time to time. In this way the students will be encouraged to speak their minds freely on legal issues and case studies without the pressure and the fear of humiliation or low recitation grades. This method will evoke creativity and analytical thinking on their part and will certainly build their confidence in intellectual discourse and public speaking.

Life

Finally, we should remember that in any field of study or specialized discipline, the purpose is not only to prepare the student for a professional career, but also for the journey of life itself. To realize this goal, every institution of learning should promote more than just academic excellence or scholastic aptitude in its students. It should aim to develop their full potentials so that they will become noble members of society, country and humanity.

Law students are, first and foremost, human beings who pursue their respective dreams amidst the many realities of life. Some of these realities may not be as appealing as one might wish, and they may demand more than just technical expertise in the law. Mere intellectual capabilities may not be sufficient to enable a law student to survive the trials and tribulations of life. Certainly, there are many questions in this world that cannot easily be answered solely by the finite expanse of the mind.

Long ago, I thought that success in one's profession and in life in general is assured for people with high intelligence quotients. Later on, however, I observed that there were many summa cum laudes and bar topnotchers who could not hack the jungle of life and the rigors of the profession, and they ended up as mere clerks in large corporations or as also rans and wannabes elsewhere. On the other hand, I also noted that there were many students of average intelligence who rose to become great advocates, jurists, educators, business entrepreneurs and government leaders. So I kept on asking myself this question: why is it that some people with high IQ flounder, while many with modest 1Q do surprisingly well?

Two years ago, my wife Leni, who was associate dean at the Asian Institute of Management asked me to read Daniel Goleman's Emotional Intelligence.

This book explains that to succeed in life, we must rely not merely on our intelligence quotient or IQ; we must also develop our emotional intelligence or EQ. EQ is the proper development of emotions - the ability to express the proper emotion with "the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right purpose and in the right way." It includes self-control, zeal and persistence, and the ability to motivate oneself.

Mr. Goleman says that new scientific discoveries about the brain's emotional architecture offer an explanation of those baffling moments in our lives when feeling overwhelms rationality. Understanding the interplay of brain structures that rule our moments of rage and fear or passion and joy - reveals a lot about how much our emotional habits can undermine our best intentions, and what we can do to subdue our more destructive or self-defeating emotional impulses.

Just recently, my wife again showed me a book entitled Spiritual Intelligence by Danah Zohar and Ian Marshall. This book explains that apart from IQ and EQ, there is a third aspect: spiritual intelligence or SQ, which is our ultimate intelligence. It says that human beings are essentially spiritual creatures who are driven by a need to ask "fundamental" or ultimate questions, like why was I born? What is the meaning of life? Why should I go on when I amit so tired or depressed, or when I feel beaten? What makes all worthwhile? In short, human beings are driven by a longing to find meaning and value in what they do and experience.

Let me quote directly from the book. "SQ gives us our ability to discriminate. It gives us our moral sense, an abi to temper rigid rules with understanding and compassion and an equal ability to see when compassion and understanding have their limits. We use SQ to wrestle with questions of good and evil and to envision unrealized possibilities - to dream, to aspire, to raise ourselves out of the mud."

This book on Spiritual Intelligence was first published in the year 2000, but it became an instant hit in the United States after the 9/11 terrorist attacks at the World Trade Center and the Pentagon building. All of a sudden, Americans were awakened from their complacency and sense of security when so many thousands could be killed so brazenly in their favorite sanctuaries. Hence, they started to ask: What is the meaning of all these? Why is human life so brittle? What is the purpose of life? Raising these questions does not mean that Americans are becoming religious or liturgical. Spiritual quotient is not measured or enhanced by plain religiosity. Going to church regularly or mumbling repetitive prayers is not the equivalent of high SQ.

All in all, these new readings on EQ and SQ tell me that we need to develop three kinds of intelligence: intellectual, emotional and spiritual. The intellectual gives us vast academic knowledge; the emotional allows us to judge what situation we are in and then to behave properly within it through the discrete use of our knowledge; the spiritual enables us to ask if we want to be in this particular situation in the first place and to dare us to create a better one.

In short, IQ deals with the mind, EQ with the heart, and SQ with the spirit.

Indeed, our schools should prepare us not only for the present life, but also for the hereafter and forever. No matter how the world turns or how civilizations progress or how events affect our very existence, never for a moment think that our lives on earth are the only ones we have. Rather, believe that there is life after death, and that there is eternity where our final destiny lies.

Just as there are human and physical laws, there are also spiritual laws. And just as there are consequences for violations of human and physical laws, so are there penalties for the transgression of God's commands. For example, civil laws protect contracts, and a party who breaks them may be liable for damages. In the same manner, one who injures another violates criminal laws and is jailed as a consequence. So, too, a violation of the laws of physics impels consequences; hence, one who jumps out of a window falls in accordance with the law of gravity.

By the same token, spiritual laws operate on our spirits, whether we like it or not. When we transgress the Ten Commandments or the beatitudes proclaimed by our Lord Jesus, our spirits suffer the necessary consequences by being damned in eternity. Just as there are reparations for violations of human and physical laws, there is also salvation from our sins - by repentance, restitution and reformation.

Even as we feast and celebrate on this beautiful evening, isn't it wonderful to know that God is present with us, here and now, in this beautiful ballroom? He is amongst us to share our joy, above us to inspire us, before us to lead us, beside us to guide us, behind us to protect us, and within us - in our minds, our hearts and our spirits - to possess us and bring us to His everlasting Kingdom!

Epilogue

To close this address, let me summarize. I believe that every great school of law must teach its students the three Ls of legal education; namely, language, law and life. Looking over your school brochure, I see that these triple Ls have already been taken into account. Furthermore, Adamson University is committed to the core values of, first, academic a excellence which involves the IQ; second, social responsibility which your school defines as the "building of a society based on justice, peace, respect for human dignity and the integrity of creation" - all of which are really encompassed by EQ; and third, inspiration of St. Vincent de Paul in pursuing  gospel values, a pursuit that develops the SQ.

In short, let me again congratulate all of you tonight on the 61st anniversary of the College of Law of Adamson University - an institution of higher learning that inculcates in its students the three Ls of quality legal education.

Mabuhay ang Adamson University!
