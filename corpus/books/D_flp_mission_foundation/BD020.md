# Justice and Faith -- Ch. 1: On Developing My Decision-Writing Style

**JUSTICE**

**On Developing My Decision-Writing Style**

**Prologue**

To commemorate my second year as a member of the Supreme Court, I thought of setting down the main features of the decision-writing style I have been developing. Being a non-career jurist – my appointment to the Supreme Court being my first full-time government job – I took my oath before President Fidel V. Ramos on October 10, 1995, without having authored a single judicial decision. Hence, I had to put together and evolve rather speedily my own decision-writing style and format. It did not take long, however, for me to produce my first opinions and decisions. My first extended opinion was my Dissent from the court’s en banc Resolution (denying the Motion for Reconsideration) in Imelda Marcos vs. Comelec promulgated 15 days after I assumed office. My first ponencia, on the other hand, was the unanimous en banc case of Ty vs. Trampe, promulgated 52 days after my said assumption. This case is significant not only because it was the first decision I penned, but also because it nullified an unreasonable increase in real estate taxes. Since then, I have written over 230 full-blown signed ponencia, 11 separate opinions (including dissenting and concurring ones), about 140 extended resolutions and some 675 minute resolutions disposing of cases. The ponencias and opinions have been bound into 11 volumes consisting of about 500 pages each or a total of 5,500 pages. Each ponencia averages 25 pages and each opinion, 12 pages. The unsigned extended resolutions constitute another volume. The minute resolutions, each consisting of only a few sentences, have not been collected and bound.

—-------------- 

I started preparing unsigned extended and minute resolutions right after my assumption into office. 

An inventory by my staff showed I "inherited" over 700 pending cases from my predecessor. An additional 280 cases were "unloaded" to me by the other incumbent members of the Court when I assumed office. Thus, I was initially assigned about one thousand cases, excluding administrative matters and complaints against lawyers numbering about 100 more. Every month, each member of the Court, including me, receives, by raffle, about thirty-five (35) new judicial cases plus about 10 administrative cases (Because of their other responsibilities, the Chief Justice, the two senior justices who are Division chairmen and the next senior justice who is a "working chairman" receive about 50% less). 

The longest is 72 pages, Tañada vs. Angara, G.R. No. 118295, May 2, 1997; while the shortest is four (4) pages, Cruz vs. Correctional Institute for Women, 262 SCRA 552, September 27, 1996. 

The longest is 32 pages - Separate Opinion in People vs. Echegaray, G.R. No. 117472, February 7, 1997; and the shortest is three (3) pages - Separate Dissenting Opinion in Manila Prince Hotel vs. Government Service Insurance System, G.R. No. 122156, February 3, 1997. 

Most of them consist of one or two pages. The longest is 12 pages.

—--------------

Even though my decisions are written primarily to resolve legal issues and are thus emblazoned with a considerable amount of endeavored to write in sim legalese, I have ple English, comprehensible as much as possible to a high school graduate. I want to be understood not only by the legal profession but also by the parties themselves, especially by persons accused of crimes, and by the public at large as regards cases involving interpretation of the Constitution. As I wrote in my Dissenting Opinion in Marcos: 

"The Constitution is the most basic law of the land. It enshrines the most cherished aspirations and ideals of the population at large. It is not a document reserved only for scholarly disquisitions by the most eminent legal minds of the land. The Constitution is not intended for lawyers to quibble over, nor to define niceties and articulate nuances legal about, in the ascertainment of its import. Its contents and words should be interpreted in the sense understood by the ordinary men and women who place their lives on the line in its defense and who pin their hopes for a better life on its fulfillment."

 

More than lingual elegance and even legal pugnacity, I strive for simplicity, clarity and precision in nuances and shades of meanings. I believe that the trend in all professions and occupations - be they medicine, theology, science or music - is to be understood not just by the members of a particular discipline but by the public at large. While specialization cannot be avoided, generalization of public understanding is at the same time demanded in this age of mass communication, globalization and universal education. Indeed, no principle is so complicated and no mystery so deep that cannot be explained in clear and simple language to be capable of general understanding.

In my decisions, I also endeavor to answer and settle all relevant issues and questions raised by the parties. As a former practicing lawyer, one of my worst nightmares was to read a decision disposing of a case I argued based merely on one or two of the many issues I raised - or worse, on issues other than those raised. Sometimes, courts would narrate facts not backed by the records or the evidence. To avoid these — now that I am on the backside of the judicial bench - I strive to state facts as accurately as I can, in the words of the parties if possible, and to resolve all issues raised.

The features of the writing style presented here are progressive and cumulative. Thus, some of them may not be found in my first writings. They are collected and used gradually and increasingly as time goes by. The perceptive reader will find, for instance, that in my earlier ponencias, I did not capitalize the "R" in "Respondent Court,” or the “A” in "Appellant Juan dela Cruz.” Neither did I separate a topic under the title "Statement of the Case” or “The Case” as much as I did toward the latter part of the two-year period. Prospectively, I shall use much, if not all, of the features described here. In fact, it is possible that in the future I shall use more features than what are now included here. Thus, an update of this work may be needed from time to time.

—---------------

I must concede, however, that there are technical fields, like remedial law, which laymen may find difficult to comprehend. See, for instance, Arcelona vs. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 102900, October 2, 1997, dealing on the grounds for voiding a final judgment; and Mondonedo vs. Court of Appeals, 252 SCRA 28, January 18, 1996, reiterating the doctrine that certiorari is not a substitute for a lost appeal. 

In Municipality of Candijay, Bohol vs. Court of Appeals (251 SCRA 530, December 28, 1995), Cal us. Court of Appeals (251 SCRA 523, December 28, 1995), Jao & Company, Inc. us. Court of Appeals (251 SCRA 391, December 19, 1995) and Yu vs. Court of Appeals (251 SCRA 509, December 26, 1995) , I did not use many of the features explained in this article. These ponencias were originally intended as unsigned extended resolutions but my colleagues in the Third Division thought the Resolutions merited the signatures of the justices who unanimously concurred with them. 

By “style," I do not refer to a distinctive form of prose or literary expression, as in "the style of Mark Twain," or “the style of St. Paul.” Rather, it is my own preference of subject presentation, typographic arrangement and display, use of tenses, punctuation, abbreviation, capitalization, etc., which I have adopted in writing my decisions. 

—--------------

Each justice and judge in the Philippines or elsewhere has his/her own style of writing which characterizes pathways in his/her mind. By and large – except for glaring lapses in grammar, sentence construction, tenses, numbers, or typing - I believe there is no right or wrong style. And any claim of superiority or dominance in writing style by anyone would be pure hubris.

May I also express my appreciation to my wife, Leni - who was herself a college editor and now a dedicated professor at the Asian Institute of Management – for her generous critique of some of my peregrinations after they have been released or promulgated. 

Finally, may I express my deep appreciation to my colleagues in the Court, present and past, for their comments, suggestions, observations and questions: Chief Justice Andres R. Narvasa who is my current chairman in the Court's Third Division, Justices Florentino P. Feliciano (who December 17, 1995) who was my first retired on chairman, Teodoro R. By their concurrence with and sometimes even by their dissents from my ponencias, they have inspired me to strive for excellence in doctrinal content and for improvement of my writing style. Whatever merit there may be in my ponencias, I ascribe to their kindness, grace and patience with this new jurist. But all the errors and faults are mine and mine alone. **PART I. FEATURES OF MY DECISION-WRITING STYLE** 

A distinct visual feature of my decisions is the use of topical titles or nomenclatures for their different parts. In general, the main parts of these decisions consist of: (1) an introduction or prologue, (2) the statement of the case, (3) the statement of facts, (4) the issues or assignment of errors, (5) the Court ruling in which each issue is separately considered and resolved, followed finally by (6) the dispositive portion. In cases in which controversial issues are involved or where new principles are introduced, an epilogue is included just prior to the disposition. In discussing my decision-writing style, however, I differentiate between criminal and noncriminal cases.

A. CRIMINAL CASES 

**1. Introduction, Prologue or Opening Statement **

Decisions involving criminal cases usually open with a concise statement of the principal factual or legal issue/s of the case. For example: 

"In seeking acquittal, appellant insists that the complainant was his sweetheart who willingly consented to the sexual congress. 

or

"The appellant admits to having sexually molested the ten-year-old victim in this case, but claims that he merely used his fingers and is thus guilty only of acts of lasciviousness and not of rape."

or

"The accused-appellant admits having killed his wife but insists that he did so only after surprising her in the very act of sexual intercourse with another man. However, he fails to substantiate the stringent elements required by law to absolve him of criminal responsibility. His defense appears no more than an amalgam of confusion, contradiction and concoction.”

In some instances, the opening statement is an iteration of a legal maxim or principle most relevant to the resolution of the case. Thus, in People vs. Quinao, et al.," I wrote: 

"In resolving this appeal, this Court once more stresses these general rules: alibi and denial are weak defenses which cannot prevail over the prosecution witnesses' unhesitating, positive and firm identification of the accused as the persons responsible for the crime; and conspiracy, like any element of the offense, must be proved as clearly and as convincingly as the crime itself."

In People vs. Binamira, I said: 

"In acquitting the accused, this Court stresses two doctrines: (1) a violation of the accused's right to retain a counsel of his own choice during custodial investigation renders his extrajudicial confession inadmissible even where such confession was extracted on October 3, 1985, i.e., before the effectivity of the 1987 Constitution, and (2) to sustain a conviction anchored on circumstantial evidence, the prosecution must convincingly prove an unbroken chain of events from which only one fair and reasonable conclusion can be inferred - that of the guilt of the accused beyond reasonable doubt. Where such circumstances can be the subject of two possibilities, one of which is consistent with innocence and the other with guilt, then such evidence has not fulfilled the test of moral certainty and the constitutional presumption of innocence must be upheld." In other instances, I state my view on the nature and implications of the crime involved, such as in People vs. Julian:

"Rape is chilling, naked sadism. It is marked by the savagery and brutality of the assault on the helpless victim's privacy. Thus, a severe penalty is meted out by the State, as parens patriae, for this abhorrent crime, revealing the clear legislative intent to protect clear legislative women against the bestiality of persons who cannot controlled their libidinous proclivities." 

The objective in placing an introduction to my ponencias is to show concisely but comprehensively the decisive point that disposes of the case. It also serves to acquaint readers with the specific nature of the controversy and the issues involved. With it, readers can determine right away whether further reading is useful or helpful to them. 

However, the introduction or opening statement is not indicated by a title since it often consists of only a single sentence or paragraph. 

**2. Statement of the case or The Case** 

The introduction or opening statement is followed by a legal statement of the nature of the action. Criminal cases elevated to the Supreme Court normally involve ordinary appeals from trial court decisions imposing the penalty of reclusion perpetua or life imprisonment, and automatic review of decisions imposing the death penalty. The following are contained in this part of my ponencias: 

a. the specific charge against the appellant/s and his other co-accused, if any (Thus, the accusatory portion of the information filed in court by the public prosecutor is always quoted in full). 

b. the court of origin, the name of the judge who penned the decision, and the date of the appealed decision (Where the ponente is other than the judge who tried the case, both are named, usually in a pertinent footnote). 

c. the criminal case number in the trial court 

d. the plea of the accused 

e. the dispositive portion of the assai led decision 

—-------------

Sometimes there are decisions of the Court of Appeals or the Sandiganbayan which impose the same penalties. They are thus reviewed in general like those of trial courts. Where the penalty involved is less than life imprisonment, the proper remedy is a petition for review under Rule 45 of the Rules of Court. These latter cases are included in the topic, “Non-Criminal Cases.”

—------------

In some instances, I likewise indicate the names of the public prosecutor and the counsel de oficio or de parte. Naming the judge and the lawyers involved serves to encourage our public and private legal officers to render their best in the performance of their duties and functions in the administration and dispensation of justice. Their competence (or incompetence) is often demonstrated or evaluated, wittingly or unwittingly, in the disposition of the appealed case. Such competence refers not only to knowledge of substantive legal matters or procedural techniques, but also to their command of language or lack of it) as can be observed from the transcripts, pleadings and the assailed decision itself. 

The verbatim reproduction of the criminal information guides this Court on the nature and the gravity of the offense for which said accused may be found culpable. As a rule, courts cannot convict an accused of a crime different from or graver than that charged. Unless for an "offense proved included in that which is charged" (Section 4, Rule 120 of the Rules of Court). 

Mentioning the court of origin and the case number originally assigned to the particular case helps in facilitating the consolidation of the records of the case in both the trial and the appellate courts after entry of final judgment. 

The plea of the accused is important to ensure that due process was accorded him. Absence of or irregularity in the arraignment may render the judgment void, and further consideration by this Court would be futile. In some instances, I may refer to the conduct of a preliminary investigation because this is a right granted by the Rules to the accused,  without forgetting of course that the failure of the accused to question defects in the proceedings may mean a waiver thereof. Also, in some instances I mention the fact of appellant's detention in order to dispose of the preliminary query in appealed cases not subject to automatic review: whether or not the appellant has abandoned his appeal by escaping from detention.

Finally, the direct copy of the decretal portion of the assailed decision informs the reader of how the appealed case was decided by the court a quo. After all, this is the essential subject of review by this Court. 

—-----------

In People vs. Ortega, Jr. (G.R. N o. 116736, July 25, 1997), Appellant Garcia could not be convicted of homicide for "attack[ing], assault[ing], and stab[bing] repeatedly with a pointed weapon x x x different parts of the body of one Andre Mar Masangkay" as alleged in the information filed in the trial court, because the prosecution evidence showed that he had nothing to do with the stabbing solely perpetrated by his co-accused. Neither could Garcia be convicted for the death of the same victim through drowning, which was proved to be the latter's immediate cause of death, because such was not charged in the information. The Court ruled, "To convict him of an offense other than that charged in the complaint or information would be a violation of [his] constitutional right [to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him]." 

In People vs. Lacerna (G.R. No. 109250, September 5, 1997), on the other hand, we held that illegal possession of a prohibited drug (penalized under Section 8 of the Dangerous Drugs Act) is deemed included in a charge of violation of Section 4 of the same law which penalizes “any person who, unless authorized by law, shall sell, administer, deliver, give away to another, distribute, dispatch in transit or transport any prohibited drug, or shall act as broker in any of such transactions.”

—------------

**3. Statement of Facts or The Facts**

The versions, of both the prosecution and the defense, of the facts of the case are usually presented in my ponencias. If they have been written clearly, accurately and sufficiently in the parties' respective pleadings or in the assailed decision itself or in the solicitor general's brief, I simply reproduce them verbatim. Otherwise, I restate them for better comprehension and appreciation. 

Both the version of the prosecution and that of the defense are presented in the interest of fairness and due process. The resolution of most appealed criminal cases, unlike in civil and other cases, depends to a large extent on factual issues and the appreciation of evidence. The plausibility or incredibility of each version can sometimes be initially gleaned from a reading of the facts. The Court’s detailed evaluation of the contentions of the parties appears in the discussion of our ruling at the latter part of the decision. 

The bases of the trial court in arriving at its findings and conclusions are also presented. Again, this apprises the reader of the reasons why the trial judge gave credence to the prosecution evidence while discounting that of the defense or vice versa. The fact that the assailed decision fully, intelligently and correctly resolves all factual and legal issues involved may partly explain why this Court finds no reason to reverse the lower court’s findings and conclusions. Conversely, a patent misappreciation of the facts or misapplication of the law by the trial court would aid in a better understanding of the reversal or the modification of its ruling.

—----------- 

In my Separate (Concurring) Opinion in Esparas, I agreed with the majority that this Court has the inescapable duty to review lower courts' decisions imposing the death penalty even though the accused escaped detention. I further opined that such review should be undertaken “only after the accused is re-arrested and taken back into the custody of the law.” 

Although normally representing the national government, its agencies and instrumentalities, the Solicitor General is expected to remain independent and without bias. 

—-----------

**4. Issues or Assignment of Errors **

The appellant's assignment of errors as contained in his brief is usually copied verbatim. By stating the assigned errors, the Court is guided on what specific issues to delve into. The purpose in reproducing in toto the appellant's words is to avoid motions for reconsideration of the final decision on the ground that the Court failed to consider all assigned errors that could affect the outcome of the case. 

Where the appellant presents repetitive issues or where his assigned errors do not strike at the main issue, I restate them in clearer and more coherent terms. In some instances, I include additional issues which, though not specifically questioned by the parties, are deemed important in order to render substantial justice. I exercise this discretion since appealed criminal cases are given de novo review in contrast to noncriminal cases in which this Court’s review is generally limited to issues specifically raised in the appeal.  

—-----------

For examples, see People vs. Sumbillo, G.R. No. 105292, April 18, 1997, and Filoteo, Jr. us. Sandiganbayan, G.R. No. 79543, October 16, 1996. See People vs. Sol, GR. No. 118504, May 7, 1997. In People vs. Agunias, supra, the Court motu proprio reviewed the additional issue of whether treachery may be appreciated as a qualifying circumstance, despite the failure of the appellant to raise it as an error in his conviction by the trial court for murder even though no qualifying circumstance was alleged in the information.

—-----------

**5. The Court's Ruling **

This part begins with a single sentence stating how the Court disposes of the case under review. Examples are: "We affirm the judgment of the trial court”; “The appeal is not meritorious”; or “The appeal is bereft of merit.”

 Where the assailed decision is modified, a lengthier and more explanatory paragraph is set forth. Thus: 

"We affirm the findings of the trial court as regards the guilt of the appellants. However, the crime committed was not multiple murder with double frustrated murder, but four (4) separate crimes of murder committed against each of the four victims – Clarence Suitos, Macario dela Pena, Anicia Sales, and Luzviminda Pudol – and two (2) separate crimes of attempted (instead of frustrated) murder committed against Spouses Claro and Miceala Suitos.”

"We affirm the conviction of [A]ppellants Quinao and Sidro. Appellant Ortiz is acquitted for failure of the prosecution to prove beyond reasonable doubt his participation in the conspiracy to murder Cecilio Magpantay.”

Such a brief resolutory statement is followed by a full discussion and resolution of the specific errors or issues raised by the appellant as well as other issues identified by the Court as essential to a just disposition of the case. Where there are several errors assigned, each is separately addressed. Thus, the preliminary indication: "First Issue:", "Second Issue:” and so forth. 

In some cases, an issue raised may need a detailed discussion of several subtopics. For clarity, subtopics are also individually taken up. Their titles are italicized, underlined, and left-justified.

In discussing and resolving the issues, I use the present tense. In fact, in the entirety of the ponencias - except in the statement of facts and the rulings of the lower courts - I normally use the present tense. In this manner, the drama of each case becomes more vivid and easier to understand, as if the parties or their counsel are actually and presently arguing, with the Court issuing the corresponding ruling or resolution thereon.

**6. The Disposition or Dispositive Portion **

The dispositive portion is the essential part of the decision. It states whether the appeal is granted, denied or modified; whether the appealed decision is affirmed, reversed or modified; whether the accused is convicted or acquitted. Sometimes the trial court may be correct in finding the guilt of the accused but may err in imposing the proper penalty. A common error is the lower court's occasional equation of life imprisonment with reclusion perpetua. These are different. So, too, the proper designation of the penalty — like prision mayor or reclusion temporal — is stated because each carries specific accessory penalties. Finally, to guide penal officers in case of acquittal, the clause "x x x his [the appellant's] RELEASE from confinement is immediately ORDERED, unless he is being detained for some other legal cause,"or words to the same effect, are added bearing in mind that a judgment of acquittal is unappealable and immediately executory.

B. NONCRIMINAL CASES

 

While essentially, my style in writing decisions involving criminal cases is similar to that in petitions for review from decisions of lower courts, administrative cases involving personnel in the judiciary, petitions for certiorari and other original actions before this Court, there are some marked differences. The main parts and topics are replicated, but some explanations are needed. 

- **Introduction or Prologue **

By way of introduction or prologue, the main factual or legal issue or the relevant doctrinal rule is stated. In some cases, particularly those concerning public interest or those involving complicated commercial, scientific, technical or otherwise rare subject matters, a declaration of principle or a brief backgrounder on the subject serves as the introduction. Examples of these are found in Tañada vs. Angara in which the issue on the constitutionality of the country's adherence to the WTO Agreement was taken up, in Subic Bay Metropolitan Authority vs. Commission on Elections in which the exercise of local initiative was at issue, and in Fortune Motors (Phils.) Corp. vs. Court of Appeals in which a creative financial/commercial packaging scheme was involved.

—-------------

G.R. No. 118295, May 2, 1997, which had the following opening paragraph: "The emergence on January 1, 1995 of the World Trade Organization, abetted by the membership thereto of the vast majority of countries, has revolutionized international business and economic relations amongst states. It has irreversibly propelled the world towards trade liberalization and economic globalization. Liberalization, globalization, deregulation and privatization, the third millennium buzz words, are ushering in a new borderless world of business by sweeping away as mere historical relics the heretofore traditional modes of promoting and protecting national economies like tariffs, export subsidies, import quotas, quantitative restrictions, tax exemptions and currency controls. Finding market niches and becoming the best in specific industries in a market-driven and export-oriented global scenario are replacing age-old 'beggar-thy-neighbor' policies that unilaterally protect weak and inefficient domestic producers of goods and services. In the words of Peter Drucker, the well-known management guru, Increased participation in the world economy has become the key to domestic economic growth and prosperity.'" 

262 SCRA 492, September 26, 1996, which had this introduction: "The 1987 Constitution is unique in many ways. For one thing, it institutionalized people's power in law-making. Learning from the bitter lesson of completely surrendering to Congress the sole authority to make, amend or repeal laws, the present Constitution concurrently vested such prerogatives in the electorate by expressly recognizing their residual and sovereign authority to ordain legislation directly through the concepts and processes of initiative and referendum."

G.R. No. 112191, February 7, 1997, which had the following introduction:

"To fund their acquisition of new vehicles (which are later retailed or resold to the general public), car dealers normally enter into wholesale automotive  financing schemes whereby vehicles are delivered by the manufacturer or assembler on the strength of trust receipts or drafts executed by the car dealers, which are backed up by sureties. These trust receipts or drafts are then assigned and/or discounted by the manufacturer to/with financing companies, which assume payment of the vehicles but with the corresponding right to collect such payment from the car dealers and/or the sureties. In this manner, car dealers are able to secure delivery of their stock-in-trade without having to pay cash therefor; manufacturers get paid without any receivables/collection problems ; and financing companies earn their margins with the assurance of payment not only from the dealers but also from the sureties. When the vehicles are eventually resold, the car dealers are supposed to pay the financing companies - and the business goes merrily on. However, in the event the car dealer defaults in paying the financing company, may the surety escape liability on the legal ground that the obligations were incurred subsequent to the execution of the surety contract?”

**2. Statement of the Case **

The nature of the petition is indicated here. Generally, they are either petitions for review under Rule 45 or certiorari under Rule 65 of the Rules of Court or other original actions within the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court. Where the public respondent is the Court of Appeals, the National Labor Relations Commission or the Sandiganbayan, the particular division which decided the case elevated for review is mentioned and the members thereof named, with the ponente specified. This is for the same purpose as mentioning the trial judge and lawyers concerned in a criminal case.  

Quoting the dispositive portion of the appealed decision also serves the same purpose as that in criminal cases. In instances where the appealed decision is a reversal or a substantial modification of the trial court’s, the disposition of the latter is also quoted in full.

Where the petitioner filed a motion for reconsideration in the lower tribunal, this is also mentioned, including a reference to the order or resolution denying the motion. This is important in certiorari proceedings where, as a rule, the filing of a motion for reconsideration is a condition precedent to a petition before this Court.

**3. The Facts **

Unlike in appealed criminal cases, the opposing sets of facts are, generally, no longer presented here. Issues for resolution usually involve questions of law, grave abuse of discretion or want of jurisdiction; hence, the facts of the case are often not disputed by the parties. With a few exceptions, factual issues are not entertained in noncriminal cases. Consequently, the narration of facts by the appellate body, when exhaustive and clear, is reproduced; otherwise, the material factual antecedents are reconstructed by the Court. 

As in criminal cases, the ratio decidendi of lower court or body whose decision is under review is laid out for the same reasons. It aids the reader in understanding why the lower court ruled in a certain way and why this Court either finds no reason to reverse it or concludes otherwise. 

**4. Issue/s** 

The issues raised by the petitioner are often also quoted in full, unless they are verbose, in which case they are restated in plain and more comprehensible language. A lone and simple issue to be resolved, as most often in administrative cases is directly stated . A summary of the respective contentions of the parties and an evaluation of the same in the Court's ruling follow. 

Unlike in criminal cases, the Court is limited to a review only of the specific legal issues raised in the petition which were likewise questioned in the lower courts.  The few exceptions are errors of jurisdiction, or a question not raised that is necessary in arriving at a just decision on the case, or an unassigned error that is closely related to the error properly assigned or upon which the determination of the question properly raised is dependent. An appeal in noncriminal proceedings does not throw the whole case open to scrutiny by this Court. 

**5. The Court's Ruling **

As in decisions on criminal actions, this part begins with a prefatory statement on how the Court disposes of the case - either in favor of the petitioner or merely affirming or modifying the decision of the public respondent. Examples are: "The petition is meritorious" or "The petition is bereft of merit" or "We find for the petitioner" or "The petition is partly meritorious." 

Then each issue is also distinctively discussed and resolved. The position of each side is often summarized and the Court’s ruling follows. Where procedural questions are raised in addition to substantive ones, the former are preliminarily resolved. Subtopics under a main issue may also be discussed separately for clarity.

Where the case involves public interest or novel subject, an epilogue is added prior to the dispositive part of the decision. This summarizes the opinion of the Court on the principal subject of discourse in the decision. It may also lay out a long-term philosophy of law or guiding principle.

—------------

In my Separate Opinion in PIRMA vs. Comelec, G.R. No. 129754, September 23, 1997, I summed my view on the people's right to initiative in this wise:

"I believe in democracy - in our people's natural right to determine our own destiny. "I believe in the process of initiative as a democratic method of enabling our people to express their will and and chart their history. Initiative is an alternative to bloody revolution, internal chaos and civil strife. It is an inherent right of the people — as basic as the right to elect, the determination and the right to individual liberties. I believe that Filipinos have the ability and the capacity to rise above themselves, to use this right of initiative wisely and maturely, and to choose what is best for themselves and their posterity.

"In the end, the mission of the judiciary is to discover truth and to make it prevail. This mission is undertaken not only to resolve the vagaries of present events but also to build the pathways of tomorrow. The sum total of the entire process of adversarial litigation is the verity of facts and the application of law thereto. By the majority copout in this mission of discovery, our country and our people have been deprived not only of a basic constitutional right, as earlier noted, but also of the judicial opportunity to verify the truth."

—--------------

**6. Dispositive Portion**

Finally, I lay down the fallo of the case, which is the real judgment in a litigation. It states whether the petition is granted or denied, and/or whether the appealed decision is affirmed, modified or set aside. Where the appealed judgment is nullified or set aside, the new judgment rendered by the Court is set down herein. In administrative cases supported by substantial evidence, the fallo indicates the liability of the private respondent and also the penalty imposed (whether dismissal from the service with its accessory penalties, suspension, reprimand, fine, etc.) Where the penalty is lower than dismissal, the warning clause "the commission of the same or similar acts in the future shall be dealt with more severely by this Court” or words of similar import. 

C. SEPARATE OPINIONS 

The format of my separate opinions differs from that in my ponencias. In my separate (as well as concurring and dissenting) opinions, I state directly either the holding of the Court majority or the issues of the case. Then I briefly express my view thereon (whether agreeing or not) before proceeding to discuss fully the reasons behind such stance. I no longer state the nature of the case and its factual antecedents since these are already laid down in the main decision. Generally, the only topical titles in my opinions are the different issues presented for resolution. In some instances, particularly where a novel subject involving the public interest is at issue, I also add an epilogue. Finally, I normally cast my vote on whether to grant or to deny the petition or the appeal; or to affirm, reverse or modify the lower tribunal's judgment.

**Epilogue **

My first two years in the Court have been very challenging and engaging. I had to do my share in reducing the case backlog of the Court. I worked day and night, even on weekends. I have never been absent or late from any Court session. In fact, I cannot remember ever having preoccupied in all my life. But I am not complaining. I must quickly add that I enjoyed and will always cherish all those full days and long nights. And I look forward with more relish and intensity to the remaining years of my term. 

When I joined the Court, I sold completely and absolutely my interests in my law firm, resigned from my business involvements and cut off memberships in professional, civic, religious, charitable and other non-judicial associations, with the exception of three. I also severely limited my presence in social functions, attending only when there were special reasons like family get-togethers, weddings or anniversaries of very close friends, sports competitions (golf and tennis) with members of the judiciary or close relatives and friends with whom I was already playing prior to my appointment or who, in any event, have no known litigations before the Court. 

Some of my relatives and friends must be disappointed with me - a burden I must bear - because I refuse to receive visitors and telephone calls, and I shun any conversation regarding my work. In fact, I have given written instructions to the Supreme Court security force not to allow any visitor in my chamber without my prior express permission. And when people write to me about the Court matters, even those marked "personal," I always include them in the Court's agenda for proper notation. I feel there is nothing "personal" about matters pending before the Court, and fairness demands that every item involving litigations must be brought to the Court's or, at least, the Chief Justice's attention and, when appropriate, also to the opposing side. Whenever the opportunity is present - particularly during the few speaking engagements I accept — I make it a point to tell my audience to do me the favor of not writing or calling me up or discussing with me any matter pending before the Court.

I cannot close this anniversary piece without thanking our Lord Jesus Christ for the graces and blessings He has mercifully showered me. In trying to help render justice in our sinful world, my prayer is that I may be granted wisdom, discernment and courage to do His will. "Your will be done, oh Lord, not mine." 

—--------

If the 6000 pages of ponencias, opinions and extended resolutions I penned for the Court are divided by the two years or 731 days I have served, that would mean I produced an average of 8.2 pages everyday including Saturdays, Sundays, and holidays. In addition, I had to report on an average of about 35 items assigned to me during Division sessions of the Court on Mondays and Wednesdays and the about 12 items during en banc meetings every Tuesday, aside from reading and studying the proposed ponencias and recommendations of the 14 other members of the Court.

Under Art. VIII, Sec. 11 of the Constitution, members of the Court shall serve, during good behavior, or until the age of seventy (70) years. 

After my first two years, I will have a lessened caseload, not only because of my foregoing dispositions but also because I would have the privilege of "unloading" old cases to incoming appointees to the Court. As a rule, these old cases - meaning, more than two years from submission of the last pleading — may no longer be penned by more than two-year incumbents, per the Constitution. Perhaps, now I can look forward to "smelling the flowers" on workless Sundays. 

Prior to my appointment, I was active in more than 15 organizations from all of which I had to resign, except in three: (a) Pontifical Council for the Laity, (b) Rotary Club of Manila, and (c) Bantayog ng mga Bayani Foundation. There are precedents allowing incumbent justices to be members of these organizations like Feria, *J. *in PCL; Malcolm, Paras and Padilla, *JJ.* in RCM; and Yap, *C.J. *and Sarmiento, *J.* in Bantayog. In addition, I serve as a Special Minister of Holy Communion when I attend masses in my parish, where there is likewise a precedent - Vitug, J. serves as SMHC in his parish.

In moments of solitude and personal prayer, I contemplate my nothingness, my unworthiness before my Lord and Savior, and I always break out with a thanksgiving, reminiscent of St. Paul’s First Letter to Timothy (1 Tim 1:12-17).

## Summary

Chapter 1 of 'Justice and Faith' is CJP's October 10, 1997 second-anniversary self-account of how he, a non-career jurist whose Supreme Court appointment was his first full-time government job, evolved his decision-writing style from zero -- having taken his oath on October 10, 1995 'without having authored a single judicial decision.' He reckons the output: 230 full-blown signed ponencias, 11 separate opinions, ~140 extended resolutions, ~675 minute resolutions, bound into '11 volumes of about 500 pages each or a total of 5,500 pages' -- 8.2 pages per day for 731 days including weekends. His first Dissent (Imelda Marcos v. Comelec, 15 days after oath) and first ponencia (Ty v. Trampe, 52 days after, nullifying an unreasonable real-estate-tax increase) anchor the chronology; Tañada v. Angara (WTO, 72 pages) is the longest ponencia. The thesis: 'simple English comprehensible as much as possible to a high-school graduate' so the Constitution is read 'in the sense understood by the ordinary men and women who place their lives on the line in its defense' (Marcos Dissent). 'More than lingual elegance and even legal pugnacity, I strive for simplicity, clarity and precision in nuances and shades of meanings.' Part I lays out a six-part ponencia anatomy (introduction → statement of the case → statement of facts → issues → Court ruling → disposition, with optional epilogue), distinguishes criminal from noncriminal cases, codifies the present-tense convention ('the drama of each case becomes more vivid... as if the parties or their counsel are actually and presently arguing'), and demands disciplined penalty designation and an acquittal-clause RELEASE language. The Epilogue records the monastic discipline of chambered work -- completely sold his law-firm interests, resigned all business, cut civic memberships with three exceptions (Pontifical Council for the Laity, Rotary Club of Manila, Bantayog ng mga Bayani Foundation), bars visitors and personal phone calls, and includes letters marked 'personal' in the Court agenda because 'there is nothing personal about matters pending before the Court.' He closes with a Lord's-Prayer echo: 'Your will be done, oh Lord, not mine.'
