# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Ch. 5: Excellence and Ethics in Appellate Practice

Chapter 5

Excellence and Ethics

in Appellate Practice

It is said that justices are better read than heard. Upon their enrolment in the magistracy, they are expected to cut all social, personal and professional ties and become virtual recluses in their sensitive work of deciding litigations. This ode of honor requires them not only to be pure, but also to appear pure before their critical public. It is perhaps for these reasons that Supreme Court justices are sometimes referred to as "the gods of Padre Faura.”

Indeed, as most of you know, I have tried my best to avoid unnecessary public and even private audiences have refrained from entertaining visitors in my chambers and avoided telephone calls and conversations regarding ending matters in the courts, especially the Supreme Court.

Every year, I receive about thirty speaking invitations, but because of the foregoing reasons, I have accepted only a few. Tonight is one of those exceptions. I regard the Philippine Trial Lawyers Association as a special forum for reaching out to the legal profession with minimal risk of undue public criticism. After all, I know that you understand and appreciate the necessity of preserving the independence and the integrity of our black-robed men and women.

Furthermore, I could not turn down the invitation of your distinguished president, former Assemblyman and former Deputy Education Minister Salvador B. Britanico. Buddy and I have known each other since our student leadership days at Far Eastern University.

In your general assembly in this elegant ballroom of the Traders Hotel, I would like to offer you some insights or tips, to use a colloquial term, on how to be more effective appellate court practitioners. I realize that you are the country's most experienced litigation lawyers, who therefore know how to win their cases.

Let me, however, give you an insider's window on how to win not only your cases, but also - and perhaps even more important - how to gain the esteem and respect of the Supreme Court. After all, justice - not necessarily victory - in all litigations is the ultimate aim of our profession. Tonight, therefore, let me discuss two sure ways to gain the esteem and even the admiration of our Supreme Court, in the process, win your cases and render justice to all.

A Reputation

for Excellence

First, cultivate a reputation for excellence. This means that you must know by heart the many special rules observed in and by the Supreme Court. As you all realize, the preparation of pleadings to be filed in the Supreme Courts is much more complicated than that in the lower courts. Many petitions are denied due course at sight, simply because the lawyers have overlooked certain elementary procedural requirements, like the failure to attach an affidavit of service, a no-forum-shopping certification, or a clear duplicate original or certified true copy of the judgment or order contested. In my book Battles in the Supreme Court published in 1998, I listed at least 21 procedural errors, any one of which would be sufficient to justify the outright dismissal of a case.

Those aspiring to be appellate law practitioners should avoid those errors. Otherwise, their petitions would have very slim chances of being granted due course. At this point, I would like you to know that the names of the lawyers of the parties are always listed in the agenda of the court. Thus, when petitions are dismissed because of lapses in elementary procedure, the lawyers are known immediately, and questions about their competence and thoroughness immediately crop up. And when petitions prepared by the same lawyers are repeatedly denied because of simple e technical lapses, they acquire a reputation for sloppiness. Justices invariably comment that, for the sake of their clients, these advocates should stop appearing in the Supreme Court.

Ire of the Court

and of Their Clients

Many times also, some lawyers - either due to negligence or laziness or both - fail to comply with simple resolutions to file a comment, a reply or other pleadings. When the Court threatens them with disciplinary actions, these lawyers often pass the blame on their hapless associates or meek secretaries who have allegedly neglected to remind them of the deadlines for filing those pleadings. I would like you to know that we frown on this hackneyed buck-passing            technique and almost always admonish or fine counsels who habitually do so. Worse, when it is the petitioner's counsel who fails to comply with these simple resolutions, the Court not only disciplines the lawyer, but it also dismisses the petition altogether. As you all know, once a petition is thrown out due to procedural or other lapses, it is quite difficult to have it reinstated. Invariably, the lawyer gets the ire not only of the Court, but also of their clients!

Then, there is the even more pressing need for excellence in presenting the substantive aspects of your case. Except for a few instances like criminal cases, in which the lower courts have imposed the death penalty or reclusion perpetua, the acceptance of an appeal is purely discretionary on the part of the Supreme Court. The High Tribunal is even more discriminating in giving due course to original actions for certiorari and similar petitions that attribute lack of jurisdiction or grave abuse of discretion to other offices and officials of our country.

Poorly prepared pleadings and difficult-to-understand language inevitably spell denial for many petitions. Since the grant of due course in the High Court is discretionary, it behooves lawyers to write pleadings that are clear, correct, complete and concise. It is not enough to allege ultimate facts; it is equally important to back up your allegations with citations of authorities showing that the lower court has indeed committed reversible errors that should be corrected on appeal.

Overworked Justices

Please remember that justices of the Supreme Court are quite overworked. They have to attend to some 6,000 pending cases, and additionally, to shepherd about 5,000 new cases every year. These figures mean that each justice has an average case load of about 400 pending cases plus 25 new ones per month. You can understand why magistrates dismiss at sight unreadable, illogical, ungrammatical and legally shaky pleadings.

In my more than seven-year experience as a sitting justice, I believe that about 60 percent of all new petitions are denied at sight, either because of procedural lapses, or because of the failure of the petitioner to demonstrate clearly any reversible error or grave abuse of discretion on the par of the lower court. A further 20 percent are thrown out after the comment and the reply are filed. In other words, less than 20% of the 5,000 new filings are given due course and mature into full-blown, written ponencias.

Remember that appellate advocates are measured on the basis of how they can invoke a favorable rule of law in one case and provide a substantially meritorious exception in another. Those who are able to decipher, analyze and synthesize nuances of the law eventually get the nod of the Court.

If you are new in appellate practice, it is worthwhile to read slowly and deliberately how decisions are written, how justices analyze legal provisions and applicable jurisprudence, and how carefully distinctions are laid out in given factual milieus. As an illustrious US justice once said, cts; general propositions do not decide specific problems; each case has its own peculiarities that must be ruled upon based on the unique factual circumstances obtained. A great lawyer is able to craft a theory that addresses the issues without offending settled doctrines and yet is able to propose a meritorious exception to the prevailing jurisprudence on the matter. It is also suggested that you read petitions, comments and memoranda of respected appellate court practitioners. Study how excellently they present their cases, and why they succeed in persuading the Court to adopt their presentation. They do not merely repeat doctrines or blindly rely on copied quotes; they explain in elegant prose and unbendable logic why their cases and causes deserve to be upheld.

Pleading with

Distinction

Indeed, I have not always agreed with the briefs, memoranda and other pleadings submitted by distinguished advocates like Estelito Mendoza, Mario Bautista, Agustin Benitez, Eduardo de los Angeles, Simeon Marcelo, Theodore Te, Rene Saguisag, Pacifico Agabin, Antonio Abad Jr., Lorna Kapunan, Katrina Legarda, Roberto Abad and, of course, your president Buddy Britanico. But I have always found great pleasure in reading them, especially during weekends when I cram to write my ponencias.

To encourage lawyers to prepare their pleadings with distinction, Isomètimes quote them in my ponencias. Always, I indicate their names in an appropriate footnote. As some of you know, since I joined the Court more than seven years ago, I have been working seven days a week including Saturdays, Sundays and holidays. The dreariness of routine work is occasionally broken by the spirit-uplifting and free flowing prose of these practitioners. Their pleadings are structured, well-researched, logic-driven and yet simply written. They avoid highfalutin and bombastic language. Moreover, they are respectful in tone even if emphatic in their arguments.

More than all these, I especially urge you to reserve your best performance for occasional oral arguments called by the Court. Study minutely the facts of your case, stick by your theory, and prepare to answer probing questions on every little detail. I assure you the justices do their homework. They read the pleadings, study the law and applicable jurisprudence, and are always ready to pounce on your case theory. Sometimes, they ask questions because they do not know the answers, but more often, they ask questions to test the lawyers' grasp of their arguments. Many years ago, there was a solicitor general who appeared during oral arguments with less than ideal preparation. Thus, he stumbled from question to question. He would change his theory of the case with each hostile interrogation. Worse, he would contradict justices who were trying to help him, but whom he suspected were against him. Up to this day, he is a favorite topic of private jokes amongst the magistrates.

High Ethical

Conduct

My second suggestion on how to win the respect of our justices is for you to develop a reputation for high ethical conduct. Ethics embraces, among others, fairness, candor and integrity in your professional and personal lives.

Fairness means not merely fighting for even-handed treatment of one's cause and case. It extends to a refusal to take undue advantage of the other side, of being honorable in battle and of being scrupulous in the choice of legal weapons.

Immediately after passing the bar examinations in 1960, I became an associate in the law office of our then law dean, Dr. Jovito R. Salonga. One early morning, Dean Salonga called me up at home to say that he was indisposed and could I substitute for him in that morning's trial at the then our   Court of First Instance of Pasay. Even if I knew very little about that inheritance case he was directly handling, I stood up gallantly when the case was called that morning and mumbled, "Ready for the defense, Your Honor."

Judge Angel Mojica, who was presiding, looked askance at me and asked suspiciously, "Where is Atty. Salonga? And young man, are you ready to continue the cross-examination of the plaintiff?"

"He called me up early this morning to say that he was indisposed, Your Honor. He told me that I should not ask for a postponement because there is a standing agreement not to delay the case," I nervously replied.

"Your Honor," butted in Atty. Jose W. Diokno, the counsel for the plaintiff, “It is true that we agreed not to defer today's hearing. But in fairness to our young compañero, and knowing that Atty. Salonga would not tell a lie about his health, I myself am now moving to postpone the hearing.

Anyway, this case is also scheduled to be heard two more times during the next two weeks." Addressing me, Judge Mojica boomed, "What do you say to that motion, counsel"?

"No objection, Your Honor," I quickly answered.

This may have been a little incident in the checkered life of the late illustrious Atty. Jose W. Diokno, but it was a major one of mine. It showed me how a great advocate could demonstrate fairness to a young inexperienced lawyer, who hardly knew the pending case; and who, at that point, would probably not have been able to cross-examine the plaintiff adequately. Atty. Diokno earned my immediate admiration and awe, not only for his vaunted competence, but also for his genial sportsmanship in refusing to take undue advantage of a young, nervous lawyer. I am certain that Judge Mojica, who is now in the Great Beyond, was impressed, too.

Candor and

Integrity

Candor refers to straightforwardness or forthrightness in admitting a weakness or even an error. Some lawyers are so proud they would not admit a mistake. Matitigas ang mga ulo.

There are also lawyers who insist on equating the ratio decidendi of a decision with a mere obiter dictum. Credibility goes up when a lawyer frankly admits that a statement in a decision is a mere obiter and may not be binding. And respect rises even more when the learned counsel explains why the obiter makes sense and should anyway be adopted as a doctrine.

But nothing erodes a lawyer's credibility more than the deliberate misquoting of a decision or, worse, the omission of certain words or sentences that qualify or restrict the sense of a ruling. Remember that the eagle-eyed magistrates can easily detect misquotations, out-of-context statements and misleading arguments.

A candid lawyer is a humble person - not only one who is not afraid to admit an error or a shortcoming, but also one who rises from a mistake and more than makes up for it the  next time around.

Finally, by integrity I refer to honesty and truthfulness. A person of integrity acts in accordance with what is true and honest regardless of personal consequences. To act with integrity is to act with moral courage. This involves not only theoretical bravery in battle but, a priori, purity of spirit and clarity of conscience. For only one who is pure of heart and d u e clear of mind can act with integrity.

Assistance in

Reforming the Judiciary

The Supreme Court, as you know, has embarked on a comprehensive reform program to address the many complaints of corruption, incompetence and delay in the delivery of justice. Many times these problems are brought about, or at least aggravated, by members of the bar.

I now take this occasion to call for your cooperation and assistance in eradicating these ills. As active practicing lawyers, you know who are the corrupt and the incompetent among the magistrates. You know who do not deserve to wear their majestic black robes.

Lawyers of integrity will never bribe judges. Remember, corruption will take place only if it is abetted or tolerated by the bar. Integrity demands not only moral courage in denouncing the corrupt and the incompetent but also the initiative in defending the decent and the competent from malicious, unreasonable and scurrilous attacks.

Just as there are some dishonest judges, there are also some unscrupulous lawyers who want to win at all costs. Worse, in an effort to earn more, there are lawyers who ask money from clients, allegedly to line the pockets of magistrates. Whether they actually bribe magistrates or merely increase their earnings unethically, such practice is reprehensible and should be condemned by all lawyers of integrity.

I also denounce most vehemently some lawyers' habit of terrorizing decent and competent judges with threats of administrative complaints. Thus, the promotion of deserving magistrates are sometimes stymied or delayed. The mere pendency of these baseless charges is enough to hinder the Judicial and Bar Council from processing their otherwise deserving promotions.

To summarize, if my presence tonight will have any lasting significance and if you are lending me twenty minutes of your precious attention and time will have any worth, please remember this. To win the hearts and the esteem of the Supreme Court, there are two "E" words to live by: excellence and ethics. If you develop these two values to perfection, I assure you of success, not just in winning cas in the Supreme Court, but also in gaining the respect and the admiration of the justices.

Maraming salamat po.

## Summary

Chapter 5, 'Excellence and Ethics in Appellate Practice,' sets out the standards expected of appellate justices and advocates. CJP observes that justices — 'the gods of Padre Faura' — must be pure and appear pure, recounts his own practice of avoiding audiences, visitors, and calls about pending matters, and urges both bench and bar to combine excellence with unimpeachable ethics.
