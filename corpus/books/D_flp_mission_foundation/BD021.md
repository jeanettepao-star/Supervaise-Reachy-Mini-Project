# Justice and Faith -- Ch. 4: Advice for Aspiring Attorneys

## Advice for Aspiring Attorneys

I thank my good friend Judge Clemente M. Soriano for his kind introduction, especially that portion mentioning me as an FEU alumnus. I hold Judge Soriano in very high esteem, because he was the first law student to be elected president of the FEU Central Student Organization or FEUCSO, a post I coveted and attained because of the driving inspiration he gave me as student leader.

Indeed, I am proud to be a graduate of FEU, the best law school in the Philippines! Hindi yata kayo naniniwala! Actually, that statement was challenged by a cabinet member who is a friend. He claimed that his alma mater, which is located somewhere in Diliman, Quezon City, was the best. He said, “Our law school is the best because from our graduates come the most number of Philippine presidents, cabinet members, senators, congressmen, justices, judges and other public officials; whereas the FEU Institute of Law has yet to produce a Supreme Court justice or a secretary of justice, not to say a President or a Vice President of the Philippines.” For a while, I was taken aback - but only temporarily - as I burst forth, “That is why our country is plagued with poverty, corruption and injustice. It is because a majority of our key officials are products of your law school. Just wait when the FEU graduates take over — we shall have the Philippines we are all dreaming of." That's the difference between reality and promise. They are the reality; we are the promise. 

But levity aside, I thank Justice Oscar R. Victoriano, our venerable dean, for asking me to be your commencement speaker today. His invitation truly gives me the opportunity to reminisce thirtyfive years ago, when I too graduated from the green and gold portals of FEU — full of hope and excitement at the challenges of the noble profession of law that beckoned.

My immediate concern then, as is probably yours now, was to pass the bar examination. My own dean at the time, Dr. Jovito R. Salonga, gently reminded me that he would be greatly disappointed if I did not land among the top ten. Although the bar examination is not the real measure of a lawyer, still it gives bar topnotchers — as it gave then - some advantages. Bar placers carry this record all their lives, even in their old age when they become great lawyers, jurists or legislators. 

And so, like you, I placed immediate priority on my preparation for the examinations. I bought some multi-lined Grade I pad paper - believe it or not - and I relearned to write legibly and clearly. I was told — as I am sure you are told now — that the bar examiners are probably grumpy, poor-sighted lawyers who do not have the patience to decipher the handwriting of physicians. Because they themselves are used to reading grammatically correct, neatly prepared and laser-printed briefs and pleadings, they have no tolerance for dangling prepositions, wrong tenses, and misplaced idioms. So, I also had to review basic English with my English professor. 

During the bar review proper, I observed a rigid schedule of six hours of sleep and eighteen hours of study. I set aside all other concerns. I listened to the bar reviewers with awe and reverence and took nothing for granted. The more I studied, I learned how much I did not know. the more It was kind of frustrating, but when I discussed cases and problems with my fellow reviewees, I found that my mind could recall what I thought I could not. And gradually that gave me confidence. The human mind is dependable. If you feed it with data attentively enough, it could synthesize and recall this information when the need arises. 

During the last few weeks of review, I became increasingly nervous. I was hospitalized on the first day of the examination. From the FEU hospital, I walked weak-kneed to the nearby University East where the exams were being held. I thought I did not do well on the first day, but I persevered and prayed hard. 

When I was reviewing for the bar exams, I was lucky to be a recipient of the tutorial system. Under this system, the top ten who passed the last three bar examinations were asked to tutor — in Tagalog, para "tutukan" - the current candidates who , in the opinion of the dean and the faculty, had more than an even chance of landing in the "magic 20." This was one reason why I just had to go on, in spite of my illness the first day. My tutor prodded me to go on.

As a reward, all the bar topnotchers of FEU in the late fifties and early sixties were invited by our then Dean Jovito R. Salonga, who himself was Number One in the bar, to join his prestigious law firm. After joining him a few years, I formed my own law firm in partnership with three other FEU bar topnotchers who were also connected with the Salonga law firm, namely, Agustin O. Benitez, No. 1 in 1959; Custodio O. Parlade, No. 4 in the same year, 1959; and Leopoldo L. Africa, No. 1 in 1960 which was also the year I took the examination and managed to be a bar placer myself. From 1963 to the present, we have practiced together in the Panganiban, Benitez, Parlade, Africa and Barinaga Law Offices. Incidentally, Barinaga stands for Sylvia Katherine LuczonBarinaga, the FEU graduate who placed fifth in 1968, and who joined our partnership after her own stint of three years with Senator Salonga. And to do unto you what has been done unto us, may I invite as many of you who will land in the top twenty in this bar examination to join our law firm of FEU bar topnotchers! 

So much for the bar examination. Now let us look at the longer road ahead of you and assess the long-term prospects of a legal career. 

In a movie shown a few years ago, there was a courtroom drama involving two women, both of whom were claiming to be the mother of the same infant. Now, that is unusual. When the father of a child cannot be determined, that is usual. But when two women claim to be the mother of the same child, that is indeed unusual. It is reminiscent of the days of the wise King Solomon. 

When the case was called for hearing, the judge asked the two claimants to stand up. Nobody responded, not even the lawyers. And so, in utter irritation, His Honor banged his gavel and boomed: "Tinatawagan ko and mga partido. Tumayo lamang at lumapit dito sa harap."

 Slowly, a very old man with white hair and a frail body limped towards the front. The Judge was more irritated. "Hindi po kayo, Lolo. Ang tinataw ay iyong dalawang babae na nagdemandahanag ko dito. lyon pong mga partido." But the old man continued to approach the judge, and when he was near enough he said, "Patay na po ang dalawang babae. Ngunit partido rin po ako. Ako po iyong baby na pinag-awayan nila." 

Yes, my dear graduates, this little story highlights the frustration of our people in our slow justice delivery system. Lawyers and parties are able to use, misuse and abuse the Rules of Court to defeat the very objective of our legal system which is fair, speedy and adequate justice for all, especially the poor and the marginalized.

In a paper presented at the University of the Philippines assessing the judiciary during his incumbency, then Chief Justice Marcelo B. Fernan wrote, "Court congestion and litigation delay are the deterrents to the speedy and inexpensive delivery of justice."

In a similar self-assessment of the judiciary delivered on March 10, 1992 also in UP, incumbent Chief Justice Andres R. Narvasa said that while the Supreme Court had been able to reduce its backlog, "the picture is not quite as encouraging as far as the rest of the court system is concerned. The total filings of 338,517 in all the lower courts more or less equaled the number of case dispositions."

The Workshop on the Administration of Justice of the Multi-Sectoral Conference - of which I had the honor of being chairman — was held at the De la Salle University on October 17, 1992, to propose a program of government for the next one thousand days of the Ramos administration. The participants agreed with the above-mentioned assessment of both Chief Justices, as it noted that there was a growing backlog of cases and that, unless remedial measures were taken, justice would be delayed even more as time went by. 

But complaints are against not only excessive delays in court proceedings. They often are against the very quality of decisions and, worse, they are against corruption and bribery. When some clients come to our office, many times the critical questions they ask is not "Is my case meritorious?" The question they ask is "Do you know the judge?"

Indeed, last year the now defunct National Unification Commission conducted a nationwide survey of what people perceived to be the problems of government, and they came up with INJUSTICE, along with POVERTY, CRIMINALITY and CORRUPTION.

Many people, especially foreigners, were amazed at the public outrage over the recent hanging of Flor Contemplacion by the Singapore government. That this outrage might result in economic dislocation for our country and people was totally set aside. Indeed, our people can bear poverty and hunger, but not injustice and indignity. A poor Filipino may silently suffer occasional hunger, but he will not tolerate abuses on his person for long.

As citizens of this country, we must understand the sociological background of this outrage. A national survey conducted by the Social Weather Station on March 19 to April 21, 1994 —before reports of this controversy hit the media - showed that fifteen percent of all households had a member who was temporarily abroad. In Metro Manila, the statistics were even more staggering. One out of every three households had someone temporarily abroad. Surely, these resident family members must have heard of the abuses, indignities and crimes committed against their kin. The media reports on the plight of a seemingly helpless Filipino domestic helper or maid must have touched a raw nerve in our people. What happened to Flor Contemplacion symbolized the rage against injustice and crime that their own relatives abroad suffered while trying to earn a living and provide bread for them. How true indeed! Against a perceived injustice, our people cannot but rage! 

And this is the wound that you as aspiring attorneys are called upon to care for and to heal. For your main task — as it is that of all lawyers — is to provide justice for all, whether rich or poor, and everywhere, whether in the Philippines or elsewhere. 

And therefore today, on your graduation, let me give you, as aspiring attorneys, seven-point advice, so that you can have a good start in the profession of law. First, improve your preparation as lawyers. At this point, you have all graduated from the law school, but it bears going back to Chief Justice Fernan's lecture again, in which he lamented, "There is, however, a noticeable deterioration in the quality of graduates seeking admission to the law profession as reflected in the appalling results of the bar examinations during the past years." I say, let the FEU graduate be part of the solution, not of the problem. Let us prepare well for the bar examinations, place among the top twenty and, more importantly, raise our passing average above the national passing median. I repeat, FEU graduates have done it in the past. FEU graduates can do it again at present. If you need assistance, like the institution of the tutorial system, I am sure the FEU Law Alumni Association and our law firm will be more than willing to help. 

Second, study and utilize, whenever possible, the alternative modes of settling legal problems like arbitration and mediation. These methods will expeditiously settle disputes, especially commercial issues, without clogging court dockets. In most international contracts, there are always provisions requiring settlement of disputes by arbitration, usually in accordance with the rules of the International Chamber of Commerce. If you intend to specialize in the lucrative area of conflict of laws, particularly in international business laws, you need to study intensely these alternative modes of dispute resolutions or ADRs. Only two weeks ago, the International Chamber of Commerce held a well-attended seminar on this subject, with three lawyers as resource lecturers: Ms. Louise Barrington, a Canadian-British lawyer; Mr. Stephen Bond, a Paris-based American compañero; and yours truly. 

Third, cooperate with judges in using pre-trial methods to abbreviate proceedings, admitting what obviously are facts and refraining from using very technical objections with the sole purpose proceedings and delaying adjudications. of prolonging Many lawyers adopt the attitude of admitting nothin g proof even for what are obvious and and asking clear. They unduly tax the time and the patience of the court and the parties. In the long term, let us remember that the respect of judges and even of the opposing counsel for gentlemen-lawyers will more than compensate for any "wise guy" antics that earn, at best, temporary upmanship. 

Fourth, use your legal knowledge to promote justice, not to delay or frustrate it. Let the FEU graduate be known as an upright, moral and righteous lawyer who does not use legal technicalities to delay proceedings, but who uses procedures to see that truth and justice prevail. This reputation for truth, fairness and prudence will be more useful and admirable than a stigma of winning through the abuse of technical rules and prevailing at any cost.

The Supreme Court itself has found occasion to distinguish between what is merely legal from what is just, and I quote from the ponencia of retired Mr. Justice Isagani R. Cruz: 

"The Supreme Court is not only the highest arbiter of legal questions but also the conscience of the government. The citizen comes to us in quest of law but we must also give him justice. The two are not always the same. There are times when we cannot grant the latter because the issue has been settled and a decision is no longer possible according to the law. But there are also times when, although the issue has disappeared, as in this case, it nevertheless cries out to be resolved. Justice demands that we act then, not only for the vindication of the outraged right, though gone, but also for the guidance of and as a restraint upon the future." 

Along the same distinction, the Epistle of St. Paul to the Philippians (Phil. 3: 8-9), the second reading during last Sunday's Mass, exhorts: 

"For (Christ's) sake, I have forfeited everything; I have accounted everything else as rubbish so that Christ may be my wealth and I may be in Him, not having any justice of my own based on observance of the law. The justice I possess is that which comes through faith in Christ. It has its origin in God and is based on faith."

Simply stated, justice is served when we interpret law, "not by the letter that killeth but by the spirit that giveth life." As Justice Victoriano exhorted you earlier," the letter of the law must be tempered with reason." 

Fifth, volunteer your free legal services for the poor and the underprivileged. While we want to succeed in our profession because we ourselves want to be delivered from poverty and injustice, we should not forget the deprived and marginalized who cannot fend for and defend themselves. Join the free legal assistance group of the Integrated Bar of the Philippines or other voluntary law associations. Be involved as a watcher or legal aide for citizen election watchdogs, like the Parish Pastoral Council for Responsible Voting or the National Movement for Free Elections. Aside from helping the poor, you will also be able to hone your legal skills. Indeed, helping the poor and the underprivileged has its own rewards which money cannot buy. When I was a new lawyer, one of the first cases I handled was a rape case against a detained accused. After I got him acquitted, he was, of course, released from prison. He was absolutely penniless - I had to pay for his bus fare home. But the inner joy and fulfillment I felt as a young lawyer was even more intense than when our law firm won our Supreme Court battle for the reopening of Banco Filipino. 

Sixth, develop and stand by a philosophy of law and some core values like truth, integrity, excellence and courage. Many times we hear of "Dura lex sed lex." The law may be harsh, but it is the law; therefore it must be followed. Then we also read about "Vox populi, vox dei." The voice of the people is the voice of God. Some say, "What is black is black; what is white is white." But we also read the advice of a revered Justice: "Constitutional provisions are not cases of black and white, but are varying shades of gray which are to be painted frame-by-frame." These statements and maxims really represent various schools of legal philosophy. And they answer the question "What is really the purpose and end of law?" While ideally, a decision is arrived at after applying the legal provisions on a given set of facts — hence, the formula Law x Facts = Decision - in reality, a judgment is reached on the basis of the formula S x P = D or stimuli times the personality of the judge equals decision. Hence, knowing the personality, particularly the legal philosophy of the judge (or the lack of it), will help you predict his decision. Most great lawyers and jurists, like Oliver Wen dell Hol mes , Roscoe Pou nd and Rob erto Concepcion, are known by their philosophy of law through their oft-quoted one-liners or, better still, through their ponencias and books. 

Seventh, surrender yourself to God. At one time or another, we make mistakes; we get frustrated; we are injured, afflicted, persecuted, broken, abandoned and crushed. And so we become totally unhappy, angry and reclusive. At some other time, we feel victorious, triumphant, satisfied - so contented that we become self-righteous and self-adoring. Then we feel alienated and are eventually despised by others. Rising from the pits of poverty and deprivation, I had my own share of worldly wealth, fame, and public acclaim. But in spite of these, questions kept hounding me: Who am I, really? Why was I born? Where am I headed for? It was only when I surrendered to Jesus Christ all that I was and all that I intended to be that I found true happiness and peace. Victories in this temporal life on earth are nothing compared to the conquest of eternal life. I found joy, peace and fulfillment only when I fully realized that, while I was a resident of earth, I was really a citizen of heaven. 

I leave with you today the foregoing seven pieces of advice. Each of them could be the subject of a full speech, or even of a book. And that is the challenge I give you. Reflect, expand and develop each of these topics. And someday we may meet again and I would be most happy if you tell me that you have heeded these pieces of advice and have found fulfillment in their use. Then I will have done my job today.

In closing, my friends, dear graduates, I reiterate my most important message which is a synthesis or summary of all the above. We all want to be great lawyers. We all want to be defenders of the rule of law. That is a legitimate ambition. In the end, however, our real happiness does not lie in having, keeping and enjoying for ourselves what we want. Our fulfillment and joy depends upon our giving ourselves and of our talent; and in sharing our possessions with the poor, the weak and the afflicted, with those who cannot share with us or give us back anything in return. 

Finally and eventually, on that ultimate day in our lives when we meet our Creator and Supreme Judge, He will not ask us whether we were bar topnotchers or great lawyers; He will not ask us how many millions we earned; He will not ask us many diplomas and honorific titles we collected or how how high our position was in our government. 

Our Lord and Judge Jesus Christ, according to the Scriptures in the book of Matthew, Chapter 25, will ask us just one question; "Did you feed the hungry, clothe the naked, shelter the homeless, heal the sick and comfort the prisoners? In other words, the ultimate question we must answer to our God is: "Did you use your possessions and profession only for yourself, or did you use them to defend and protect the weak and the poor?" 

On that question, I rest my message to you this afternoon, and your answer depends on your final destiny. I thank you.

## Summary

Chapter 4 of 'Justice and Faith' is CJP's FEU Institute of Law commencement address (c. 1995), introduced by his former FEUCSO predecessor Judge Clemente M. Soriano and hosted by Dean Justice Oscar R. Victoriano. CJP returns to 'the green and gold portals' thirty-five years after his own 1960 graduation, recounting his Salonga-tutored bar-prep, hospitalization on day one of the 1960 exams, and the FEU-bar-topnotcher pedigree that later produced his Panganiban-Benitez-Parlade-Africa-Barinaga law firm. The body of the speech diagnoses the Philippine justice system's deepest grievance -- clogged dockets and lawyerly delay tactics -- quoting Chief Justices Marcelo B. Fernan and Andres R. Narvasa's UP self-assessments and the Multi-Sectoral Conference on the Administration of Justice CJP chaired at De La Salle on October 17, 1992; the 'anak na pinag-awayan' courtroom joke supplies the popular face of that delay, while the Flor Contemplacion outrage (decoded against a SWS finding that 1 in 3 Metro Manila households had a member abroad) supplies the moral. CJP then gives the graduates a seven-point advice: improve bar preparation; embrace ADRs (arbitration and mediation, including the ICC's international rules); cooperate with judges through pre-trial admissions; use law to promote justice not to delay it (anchored in retired Justice Isagani R. Cruz's 'conscience of the government' ponencia); volunteer free legal services for the poor (IBP, PPCRV, NAMFREL); develop a personal philosophy of law (S × P = D realism, with Holmes/Pound/Concepcion as exemplars); and finally, surrender to God. He closes by collapsing the six secular advices into the seventh through Philippians 3:8-9 ('not by the letter that killeth but by the spirit that giveth life') and Matthew 25 -- the ultimate question we will be asked at judgment is whether we used possession and profession to defend the weak and the poor, not how many bar honors, millions or titles we collected.
