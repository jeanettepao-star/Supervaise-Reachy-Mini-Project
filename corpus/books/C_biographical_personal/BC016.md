# Justice and Faith -- Ch. 9: Jesus, Shepherd and Leader of All Time

## Jesus, Shepherd and Leader of All Time

The gospel reading last Sunday was quite short. Jesus talked about His sheep hearing His voice, knowing him and following him. Because of his, he gives them the best gift of all: eternal life. This exhortation from John 10, verses 27 to 30, is a continuation of Jesus’ famous discourse on the Good Shepherd. My reflection tonight should therefore necessarily relate to these passages on the Good Shepherd. For Jesus is our Shepherd, our Lord and Leader of all time and for all time.

These readings were intended for last Sunday, one day before the election of our new government or for our Church. There is really only one standard of leadership, as there is one leader truly worthy of emulation. And that is Jesus Christ. As we welcome our newly elected officials - senators, congressmen, governors, mayors, etc. - it is good to remind them of the standard leadership we expect of them, which are the standards of Christ himself. Jesus, our Good Shepherd, is the perfect leader whose leadership q u a l i t i e s every other leader should aspire to approximate as closely as possible. In a prayer meeting last year before (where the North Sector was not included), I had the occasion to deliver a reflection on this subject in connection with the celebration of the Feast of Christ the King. 

Brothers and sisters in Christ, let me start by saying that Jesus is an unusual leader. His throne is a wooden cross, His crown a bouquet of thorns, His scepter a lowly reed, and His ring a rusty nail driven through His palm. His constituents betrayed Him, chanting, "Crucify Him, Crucify Him." And yet He is the only King who resurrected from the dead and by such resurrection conquered sin, not just in His own time but in all eternity. Having said that, let us reflect this evening on what kind of Jesus was a leader and is.

**Leader with a Clear Vision and Mission **

First, Jesus is a leader with a vision and mission. He made it absolutely clear who He was, what His objectives were and how He intended to accomplish them. Jesus incessantly articulated His vision and ideas. To His disciples He spoke loftily, assertively and authoritatively. His mighty sermon on the mount is the greatest teaching of all time and for all time. He leaned heavily in favor of the poor, the sorrowing, the simple-hearted and the peacemakers. He opposed the hypocrite, the arrogant, the proud and the selfrighteous. He forgave the repentant and the simplehearted.

To those who were not His disciples, to the Jew ship and the Gentiles, to His opponents, he taught through parables. The parables were intended "to enable the ayer ctor listener to discover something for himself." They awakened faith and made the listener discover the truth without being told directly. That is why most of His parables ended with questions. "Which of these three, in your opinion, was neighbor to the man who fell in with the robbers?" was the by question He asked in the parable of the good Samaritan (Lk ins, 10:36); "Which of them was more grateful to him" nail in the parable of the forgiven debtors (Lk 7:42); or "What fate do you suppose the owner of the vineyard yet has in store for them?" in the parable of the tenants (Lk 20:16). 

Indeed, Jesus knew who He was, what He was supposed to do and how He was to achieve His mission. He knew He would suffer and die, but also knew He would be victorious! As a great teacher, . He proclaimed His salvific mission in different ways to different listeners.

**A Leader of Courage and Conviction **

Second, Jesus was a leader of enormous courage and unbreakable conviction. When He walked the earth 2,000 years ago, there was no freedom of speech, and no freedom to dissent. Group conformity — particularly with the religious leaders of the time — was the only measure of truth and virtue. But Jesus did not conform. Albert Nolan, in his book Jesus Before Christianity, describes Him as "a man who has the courage of his convictions. No tradition was too sacred to be questioned. No authority was too great to be contradicted . . .  No assumption was too fundamental to be changed."

He mixed with sinners, tax collectors, a Samaritan woman, a prostitute and an adulteress. He soon acquired a "badö" reputation of being a" glutton and a drunkard." No matter. He did not seek anyone's approval but avowedly denounced sin, repeatedly called for repentance and boldly proclaimed the kingdom of God, a kingdom which is the opposite of that preached by the Jews and the Romans; a kingdom where the weak is strong and the strong is just and the just is compassionate. 

He did not mince words in identifying His opponents and in denouncing their sins. He exclaimed (Mt. 23:25-27): 

Woe to you scribes and Pharisees, you frauds! You cleanse the outside of cup and dish and leave the inside filled with loot and lust! Blind Pharisee! First, cleanse the outside of the cup so that its outside may be clean. Woe to you scribes and Pharisees, you frauds! You are like whitewashed tombs, beautiful to look at on the outside but inside full of filth and dead men's bones. 

**An Exemplary Servant-Leader**

Third, Jesus was a servant-leader who led by example. In the gospel according to Mark, Jesus told His disciples: 

You know how among the Gentiles those who seem to exercise authority to lord it over them; their great ones make their importance felt. It cannot be like that with you. Anyone among you who aspires to greatness must serve the rest; whoever wants to rank first among you must serve the needs of all. The Son of Man has not come to be served but to serve - to give His life in ransom for the many. (Mk. 10:42-45)

True it is indeed, among the Gentiles, among the non-disciples: The leaders lord it over their followers. In our country, it is no different. Although our leaders are elected by the people and call themselves public servants, the sad fact is that they arrogate unto themselves power and authority over de by the life and death of our people. Hence, they allocate s told unto themselves huge discretionary funds, so they could dispense patronage and favors. So people will personally owe them the building of roads, the repair of public markets or the construction of water systems. Our leaders make themselves the center of authority such that no business can be opened without their blessings; no employment can be secured without their recommendation; and no wedding or baptism will be complete without a politician as a sponsor. Indeed, our leaders make their importance felt — not by reason of their wisdom or their service - but by the authority and the power they clothe themselves with. 

Quite the opposite, Jesus did not come to be served but to serve. He gave of Himself freely and fully, not wanting or needing anything for Himself. And more than that, He led by example. To show that He was ready to perform the humblest of service, He washed the feet of His apostles telling them,, "You address me as 'Teacher' and 'Lord' and fittingly enough for that is what I am. But if I, who am Teacher and Lord, have washed your feet, then you, too, must wash each other's feet. What I did was to give you an example: as I have done for you, so you must do." (Jn 13:14-16) 

**A Leader of Prayer and Faith **

Fourth, Jesus was a leader of intense prayer and unbending faith. The Bible is replete with stories of Jesus praying to His Father. Just before Jesus walked on the water, the evangelist Matthew related that He went up on the mountain by Himself to pray, remaining there alone as evening drew on." (Mt 14:23-24). In the garden of Gethsemane, Jesus prayed so intensely that His sweat became like drops of blood falling to the ground (Lk 22:44). And while He was nailed to the cross, He prayed aloud several times: "Father, forgive them for they know not what they do" and "Father, into Your hands I commend my spirit." 

And to emphasize the great importance of pra He Himself taught His disciples the best prayer, of all, the Lord's Prayer.

**A Leader of Justice and Love**

Finally and most importantly, Jesus was a leader of justice and love. Romans, Chapter 14, Verse 17 says that "(t)he kingdom of God is not a matter of eating or drinking, but of justice, p e a c e d and the joy that is given by the Holy Spirit." Justice, f. peace and joy. These are hallmarks of Christ. 

When the bible speaks of justice, it really refers to righteousness — being "right" with God. We are right with God when we obey His commandments and follow His ways. We can be right with God when we heed Jesus who said: "He who will not take up his cross and follow me is not worthy of me." Jesus emphasized the importance of righteousness by concluding His sermon on the mount with these words: "Seek ye first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added r unto you." (Mt. 6:33) 

The Scriptures describe Jesus as the ultimate judge of all. He will come in glory with His royal angels and will pass judgment on the basis of whether f we His creatures gave food to the hungry, drink to the thirsty, clothing to the naked, and comfort to the S imprisoned (Cf Mt. 26:35-40). In announcing the imminent arrival of Jesus into the world, the prophet Isaiah declared: 

Here is my servant whom I uphold, 

my chosen one in whom I am pleased, 

Upon whom I have put my spirit; 

he shall bring forth justice to the nations. 

Isa. 42:1;6 

But Jesus is not only just. More than anything, He is compassionate and loving. In many parts of the gospel, Jesus showed His compassion for the crowds who followed Him. Though He was at times tired and weary, still He taught them, fed them and healed them. He never turned them down. On the contrary, He showed overwhelming love for the weak, the sick, the poor, the marginalized, the outcast and the sinner.

A good illustration of the supremacy of His love is shown in the story of the adulteress (Jn. 8:1-11). Jesus was confronted by the scribes (or canon lawyers) with a woman caught in flagrante with adultery. "Teacher," the scribes mischievously said to Him, "this woman has been caught in the act of adultery. In the law, Moses ordered such women to be stoned. What do you have to say about the case?" 

You know, brothers and sisters, this was a difficult situation, for really the law, as known by the Jews, imposed the penalty of death by stoning every woman who committed adultery. If Jesus had not imposed it, He would have violated the law; and if He did, He would have virtually authorized the killing of a human being. In the face of this dilemma, Jesus replied, "Let the man among you who is sinless be the first to cast a stone at her." At this, the men dispersed, for they realized that Jesus knew their many sins. 

Indeed, to be just is to be wise. But justice is not the most valued virtue in the kingdom of God. It is love. As St. Paul said (1 Cor. 13): 

If I speak with human and angelic tongues but do not have love, I am a noisy gong, a clanging cymbal... if I have faith great enough to move mountains, but have no love, I gain nothing... If I give everything I have to feed the poor and hand over my body to be burned but have no love, I am and nothing. 

There are in the end three things that last: faith, hope and love, and the greatest of these is love. 

In our modern day and age, it is difficult to imagine a leader who is just and loving at the same time. For justice is seen as aloof and objective, while e r y. love is perceived as intimate and subjective. Indeed, how can a judge impose the law as justice requires and at the same time waive the penalty as love pleads? In other words, can justice be satisfied while at the same time express the t r u e s t and dearest of icult love? The answer is YES. Let me illustrate.

There was this young lady-driver who was brought before a judge for speeding. "Young lady," solemnly intoned His Honor, "this police officer says you were driving at 100 kms. per hour in a 50-kilometer zone. What do you have to say?"

"Your Honor," grieved the young lady, "I admit my mistake. I am truly sorry and I solemnly promise I will not do it again." 

"For speeding, you are hereby sentenced to pay a fine of P500," concluded the judge. 

After adjourning the court session, the judge removed his black robe, went straight to the court cashier, pulled out his wallet and paid the five hundred-peso fine. He paid the penalty out of love because the law-breaker was his daughter. Justice was served — and so was love.

And this is really the story of Jesus: He is justice personified. We, His people, sinned. We violated the law of God. And so the justice of God requires our punishment. Because justice is in the nature of God, He — even if He is the most powerful being in the universe - cannot refuse to impose the sanction of the law. Otherwise, He would be violating His own nature. But Jesus is also love personified. 

So, out of His great love, He came into the world and atoned for our sins. He saved us from the fury of hell. He made it possible for us to experience the never-ending joy of heaven. Jesus is the judge in our little story. He had to impose the penalty so justice could be served. But because He loves us, He bore our guilt and paid the penalty. Such is the love of Jesus. 

Such is the Leader in Jesus. Such is the Shepherd that is Jesus. Such is the Shepherd of my soul. 

Praise to you, Lord Jesus Christ!

## Summary

Chapter 9 of 'Justice and Faith' is CJP's BLD reflection on John 10:27-30 (the Good Shepherd discourse), delivered the night before the 1995 Philippine elections and offered to newly elected officials -- senators, congressmen, governors, mayors -- as the leadership measure. He opens with an unusual-leader catalogue (wooden cross, thorns crown, reed scepter, rusty-nail ring, 'Crucify Him' constituents) before naming the only King who resurrected from the dead, then expounds five leadership qualities of Jesus as the chapter's spine. First, clear vision and mission -- the Sermon on the Mount as 'the greatest teaching of all time,' preference for the poor and peacemakers, parables designed to make listeners 'discover something for themselves' (Lk 10:36, 7:42, 20:16). Second, courage and conviction -- Albert Nolan's 'Jesus Before Christianity' on the courage to question tradition, sinners-and-tax-collectors company, the searing Mt 23:25-27 woes against Pharisees as 'whitewashed tombs.' Third, servant-leadership -- Mk 10:42-45 ('Anyone among you who aspires to greatness must serve the rest') is set against Filipino patronage politics in which leaders 'arrogate unto themselves power...allocate huge discretionary funds...dispense patronage and favors'; the foot-washing (Jn 13:14-16) is the example. Fourth, prayer and faith -- the mountain retreat (Mt 14:23-24), the Gethsemane sweat 'like drops of blood' (Lk 22:44), 'Father, forgive them' and 'into Your hands I commend my spirit' from the cross, the Lord's Prayer as the model. Fifth, justice and love -- Romans 14:17, Mt 26:35-40, Isaiah 42, the adulteress in Jn 8:1-11 ('Let the man among you who is sinless be the first to cast a stone'), and St. Paul's hymn to love (1 Cor 13). He closes with a judge-pays-the-fine analogy -- the speeding lady-driver case in which the judge imposes a P500 fine then removes his robe, walks to the court cashier and pays it himself because the law-breaker is his daughter ('Justice was served -- and so was love') -- and reads the atonement as the cosmic instance of this convergence: Jesus the judge imposed the law of God for sin, then bore the guilt and paid the penalty Himself. 'Such is the Leader in Jesus. Such is the Shepherd that is Jesus. Such is the Shepherd of my soul.'
