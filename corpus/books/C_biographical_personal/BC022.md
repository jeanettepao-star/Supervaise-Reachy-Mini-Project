# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Preface

PREFACE

As my small contribution to transparency, I try to report on my work as a member of the Supreme Court yearly, in book form. The present volume covers the salient events during my eighth year in office, from October 10, 2002 to October 10, 2003.

As its title suggests, this book aims to call attention to the new biosciences and biotechnologies, especially how they affect the judiciary's work of decision-making.

It has often been said that law is a jealous mistress that demands constant attention and continuous study. It requires understanding and appreciation, not only of its own disciplines and rules, but also of every facet of life and learning. Consequently, jurists and lawyers should not be merely walking computers of the Constitution, the codes and jurisprudence; they should also have a working knowledge of all branches of human endeavors, including the arts, economics, accounting, mathematics, finance, health, computers, political science, medicine, history, and the latest sciences and technologies.

In the distant past, the preoccupation was feeding and sheltering the population. Hence, the main concern of the world during the agricultural age was land and food production. As a consequence, law and jurisprudence also concentrated on these two subjects.

On the other hand, during the industrial revolution, the world's attention shifted to factories, machineries and tools that were used in the production of wealth. Thus, the judiciary had to adjust to new norms in commercial transactions, corporate structures, taxation, economic indicators and labor requirements.

The third wave of civilization - the information age revolutionized the world through computerization, miniaturization, digitization, satellite communications, fiber optics and the Internet. All these developments, taken together, integrate knowledge on a worldwide scale. As I said in my book Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity, these massive technologies "changed not only the economic face of the globe, but also everything else that matters - political systems, lifestyles, work habits, sciences, philosophies, and yes, even the normally reclusive and exclusive enclave of decision-making."

And now, while still trying to adjust to the transformational shifts of the information age, the world is once more buffeted by a new phenomenon - the completion of the human genome sequence on April 14, 2003. This is a momentous scientific achievement of far-reaching consequence to all of humankind, including the men and women of the judiciary.

The term "genome" refers to the totality of genetic information in a human being. Because of the completion of the sequencing project, scientists now have a complete list of the relevant parts of the human gene. It is as if all the parts of an airplane have been inventoried, thereby presaging the inevitability of assembling them into a new flying machine or - if such machine has already been assembled - of scientifically repairing a worn-out one. The completion of the project makes it possible for scientists to study, experiment on, modify or alter genetic structures to prolong life" and hasten the pace of evolution.

In our Supreme Court, the awareness of these momentous scientific developments heightened on November 14, 2000, when Dr. Franklin M. Zweig delivered a paper on "Life Technologies and the Rule of Law" as part of the Centenary Lecture Series conducted in 2000-2001 to celebrate the 100th anniversary of the Court.

For the Philippine judiciary as a whole, I believe that the defining event that ushered the dawn of the bio-age was the Conference-Workshop on Bioscience and Biotechnology held on August 26-29, 2003 in Tagaytay City, because it involved not only the Supreme Court but also selected lower court judges nationwide. It was during this event that the backbone of the judicial system - the trial courts - learned of the impact of the new sciences on decision-making.

My personal participation in this seminar is discussed in Chapter 1, "Enter the Bio-Age." This initial part of the book assesses the intricacies of the new sciences and the applicability, the relevancy and the adequacy of current laws and jurisprudence to meet their challenges.

For those interested in a more in-depth discussion of the new sciences and technologies and their impact on jurisprudence, I have included as Appendices to this book - with the permission of the authors - selected papers submitted during the Tagaytay Conference by Justice Leonardo A. Quisumbing, Dr. Pacifico A. Agabin and Fr. Ranhilio C. Aquino. I have also reprinted an Appendix of Dr. Franklin M. Zweig's paper entitled “Life Technologies and the Rule of Law," delivered during the Supreme Court Centenary Lecture Series. These dissertations, I believe, are essential to a well-rounded understanding of the bio-age as it dawns on the Philippine judiciary.

Chapter 2 was my lecture during the First Australasia Judicial Educators Forum. It speaks of paradigm shifts in law brought about by the gigantic strides in other fields of human knowledge, especially the informational and technological revolution, unfolding in the world. Here, I tried to interrelate the information age with the incoming bio-era. I also discussed the many Supreme Court Decisions issued in response to new questions raised by the information age.

The rest of Part I speaks of the relevance of legal education and legal practice in the evolution of knowledge in various fields. It also contains "A Primer on the APJR" to keep the readers informed of developments in this major program of the Davide Court.

Part II, on the other hand, discusses the controversial cases rendered by the Court during this same one-year period (2002-2003), in which I took an active part as ponente or as the writer of a separate, concurring or dissenting opinion. It also includes my Dissenting Opinion in the Resolution of the Court promulgating the new "Rule on Declaration of Absolute Nullity of Void Marriages and Annulment of Voidable Marriages."

May I also report in all humility that I was able to write over 100 full-length decisions and six separate opinions during this one-year period, thus bringing my grand total during all of my eight years in office, as of October 10, 2003, at more than 860 ponencias (full-length, signed decisions) and 74 separate opinions. This count does not include the thousands of unsigned minute resolutions I have also penned during those eight years.

The net effect of this production is that all the cases assigned to me have been decided within the two-year constitutional mandate. In other words, I have no backlog.

On July 1, 2003, I was transferred from the Senate Electoral Tribunal (SET) to the House of Representatives Electoral Tribunal (HRET). Like the SET, the HRET is composed of three senior SC justices (who, aside from me, are Justices Reynato S. Puno and Consuelo Ynares-Santiago); and six House members - Reps. Douglas R. A. Cagas, Zenaida G. Cruz-Ducut, Oscar S. Rodriguez, Salacnib F. Baterina, Enrico R. Echiverri and Joaquin M. Chipeco Jr. Under the Constitution, the Senate Electoral Tribunal and the House of Representatives Electoral Tribunal shall be the sole judges of all contests relating to the election, the returns and the qualifications of members of Congress.

During the one-year period that is the subject of this book, Justice Vicente V. Mendoza retired on April 5, 2003. In his place was appointed Justice Dante O. Tinga. On November 13, 2003, Justice Josue N. Bellosillo will also leave the Court. Under the Constitution, his replacement shall be named within ninety days from his retirement.

I close this Preface with gratitude to my colleagues in the Court. I have learned a lot from their ponencias, opinions and other writings. I believe that our collegial deliberations, both written and verbal, have molded and tested my own philosophy of law, decision-writing and jurisprudential outlook. As I said during my tribute to Justice Vicente V. Mendoza on the occasion of his retirement, "the Supreme Court thrives in a kettle of free ideas where truth, fairness and justice can be distilled only after all sides, including the most outlandish ones, have been pored and percolated." And although justices disagree on ideas, philosophies and other matters of the mind, they rarely carry those differences on a personal plane, much less on an emotional level. Their differences are focused on arguments, not on personalities. I am grateful to my colleagues for maintaining this atmosphere of cordiality amidst the intellectual battles in the Court.

May I also extend my appreciation to some Court officials and my legal staff for their assistance in producing this book.

October 10, 2003.

ARTEMIO V. PANGANIBAN
