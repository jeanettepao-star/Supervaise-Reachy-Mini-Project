# A Matter of Legal Philosophy

Address delivered by retired Chief Justice ARTEMIO V. PANGANIBAN during a Forum sponsored by the Institute of Government and Law Reform of the University of the Philippines College of Law on August 5, 2011 at the UP Malcolm Hall, Diliman, Quezon City.

---

I thank Professor Florin T. Hilbay, director of the Institute of Government and Law Reform of the University of the Philippines for sponsoring this Forum to dissect academically the June 28, 2011 decision of the Supreme Court in Wilson Gamboa vs. Margarito Teves, G. R. No. 176579 and for inviting me to deliver this main or keynote address.

Inasmuch as this case involves PLDT, let me at the outset formally disclose that I have been sitting as an independent member of the board of advisers of PLDT since mid-2009. That was well after I had retired from the Supreme Court in 2006. In that independent capacity, I do not represent PLDT's management or any stockholder. I cannot propose or second motions in the board of directors. Neither can I vote.

RATIO OF THE DECISION

The decision, written by Justice Antonio T. Carpio and concurred in by nine other magistrates, disposed as follows: we PARTLY GRANT the petition and rule that the term capital in Section 11, Article XII of the 1987 Constitution refers only to shares of stock entitled to vote in the election of directors, and thus in the present case only to common shares, and not to the total outstanding capital stock (common and non-voting preferred shares). Respondent Chairperson of the Securities and Exchange Commission is DIRECTED to apply this definition of the term capital in determining the extent of allowable foreign ownership in respondent Philippine Long Distance Telephone Company, and if there is a violation of Section 11, Article XII of the Constitution, to impose the appropriate sanctions under the law.

JURISDICTION AND DUE PROCESS

I have several concerns about this decision. First, on due process: the decision directed the SEC to investigate PLDT and sanction it in case it violated the Constitution. But neither the SEC nor PLDT was a respondent in the case as a corporate entity. More importantly, prior to this decision, the government, notably the Department of Justice and the Securities and Exchange Commission, have since 1935 uniformly held that the entire capital stock of a corporation, including voting and non-voting shares, shall be used in determining the 40 percent allocation to non-Filipinos. On this basis, the government has since then enticed foreigners to invest here, most notably during road shows sponsored by the government abroad. Relying on this representation, many foreigners invested in Philippine companies, including public utilities.

The most basic concept of due process is traced to Themistocles who cried, Strike, but hear me first! One may even agree with the Supreme Court's restriction on foreign stockholdings, but no one, not even the highest court of the land, may impose it without first hearing the persons who would be most adversely affected, the foreign investors.

LIBERTY AND PROSPERITY

I believe that in construing the economic provisions of the Constitution, courts should, absent grave abuse of discretion, defer to the government, especially the Executive Department. Here, judicial restraint is the better course. In litigations involving civil liberties, the scales of justice should weigh heavily against the government and in favor of the people, particularly the poor and marginalized. I have always espoused this philosophy of liberty and prosperity.

With due respect, I submit that the judiciary does not have the mandate, not to say the expertise, to decide on matters relating to the economy and prosperity, and must, as much as possible, defer to the officials elected by the people to look after these issues. After all, if these elective officials fail to deliver on their mandate, they can be held accountable during periodic elections.

Most objectionable is the threat of sanction that the Court ordered the SEC chair to impose retroactively. How can we punish investors who relied in good faith on government's representations over the last 75 years? That is most cruel and unfair.

However, if the decision cannot be reversed, I respectfully submit that instead of being sanctioned, investors should be given a reasonable period of time, a few years, to comply with the new ruling. Finally, I believe Congress should consider passing a new law which will govern an orderly and fair divestment process that will not unduly derail the country's economic progress.

Maraming salamat po. I am now ready for your questions.

## Summary

Delivered August 5, 2011 as the keynote address at a UP forum on Gamboa v. Teves (GR No. 176579, June 28, 2011) -- organized by Professor Florin T. Hilbay of the UP Institute of Government and Law Reform -- this address by CJP opens with a formal disclosure: 'I have been sitting as an independent member of the board of advisers of PLDT since mid-2009... I do not represent PLDT's management or any stockholder. I cannot propose or second motions. Neither can I vote.' He summarizes the Gamboa ratio (Justice Carpio ponente, nine concurring justices): 'capital' in Section 11 Article XII refers only to shares entitled to vote in director elections (common shares), not total outstanding capital stock including non-voting preferred shares; the SEC is directed to investigate PLDT and impose sanctions. CJP raises three concerns: (1) Due process -- PLDT was not a respondent and was not heard; the Themistocles principle ('Strike, but hear me first!') applies regardless of whether the SC's interpretation is correct; (2) Constitutionalism -- 'in construing the economic provisions of the Constitution, courts should, absent grave abuse of discretion, defer to the government... the judiciary does not have the mandate, not to say the expertise, to decide on matters relating to the economy'; and (3) Retroactivity -- since 1935 (75 years), the DOJ and SEC 'uniformly held that the entire capital stock' is used to compute the 40% allocation, and the government used this interpretation in foreign road shows to attract investors; 'how can we punish investors who relied in good faith on government's representations over the last 75 years? That is most cruel and unfair.' His proposed remedies: give investors a few years to comply if the decision cannot be reversed; and Congress should pass a new law governing an orderly and fair divestment process.
