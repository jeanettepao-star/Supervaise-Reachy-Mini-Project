# Ang Pagiging Hiwalay ng Simbahan at Pamahalaan

Lecture delivered on January 29, 2008 by retired Chief Justice Artemio V. Panganiban at the Malolos Constitution Lecture Series sponsored by the Bulacan State University, Malolos, Bulacan.

---

Magandang umaga po sa inyong lahat. Lubos po akong nagagalak at nakabalik muli ako rito sa Bulacan State University. Itinuturing ko na pung isang tahanan at paaralan ang pamantasang ito. Mula po nuong minarapat ninyong bigyan ako ng karangalang maging isang Doctor of Laws, honoris causa, itinuring ko na po ang aking sarili na isa na ring alumnus ng BSU.

TALAYANG DAPAT IPAGDIWANG

Talagang dapat ipagdiwang ang Malolos Constitution, hindi lamang dahil sa ito ay isang simbolo ng ating kalayaan sa lahat ng mga banyaga, kungdi upang sariwaiin din sa alaala na nuon pa man, mahigit na isang daan taon na ang nakararaan, ang ating bayang Pilipinas ay isa ng constitutional democracy.

We feel proud that our Malolos Constitution has all the earmarks of a good fundamental law. It contains the three basic elements of a model constitution: a Constitution of liberty, a Constitution of government, and a Constitution of sovereignty, all at the same time.

LAYING THE PREMISE

In the recent past, we have read in the newspapers or heard over the radio and television, the men and women of the cloth commenting on how our government has been characterized by rampant corruption, violence, extra-judicial killings, and disappearances. On the other hand, high officials of government have been highly visible in big prayer rallies. Do these activities on either end violate the separation of the Church from the State?

EARLY BEGINNINGS

The Spanish Constitution of 1876 ordained Catholicism as the state religion. Under that set-up, there was no dividing line between the Catholic Church, as a religious denomination, and the State as a secular institution. Civil authorities exercised religious functions and the friars exercised civil powers. The Americans altered that cozy relationship. The Treaty of Paris of December 10, 1898 guaranteed religious freedom to the Philippines.

RELIGIOUS FREEDOM UNDER THE MALOLOS CONSTITUTION

On January 22, 1899, the Filipinos guaranteed themselves religious freedom when they adopted the Malolos Constitution. Its Preamble recognized the presence of a Power higher and greater than the State. Article 5 stated: The State recognizes the freedom and equality of all religions, as well as the separation of the Church and the State.

THE NON-ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE

Cases involving the non-establishment clause have invariably sought to protect the people's choice of religion within the bounds of their conscience and free from the dictates of the State. The purposes are two-fold: one, to encourage voluntarism and two, to insulate the political process from interfaith dissension. Thus, the non-establishment clause demands that government be neutral with regard to religious matters.

THE FREE EXERCISE CLAUSE

The Free Exercise Clause of the Constitution takes its bearing from the notion that freedom of belief, including religious belief, is as limitless as one's imagination and thought. However, the exercise of religious belief is an altogether different matter. The Philippine Supreme Court is not predisposed to invalidate a law that seems offensive to religious freedom; instead it tries to carve out an exception to accommodate religious exercise when it is justified.

SUMMATION

Sa loob po ng nakaraang oras, tinangka ko pong sagutin ang masalimuot na mga tanong sa ating talakayin ngayon. Masasabi nating mula nuong ipasinaya ang Malolos Constitution hanggang ngayon, nagaruon tayo sa Pilipinas ng isang pananaw sa isyu ng pagiging magkahiwalay ng Simbahan at Estado na iba sa pananaw sa Amerika.

Dito po sa bayan natin, nagdarasal tayo bago magsimula ng pagpupulong. Bago simulan ang sesyon ng Kabinete at ng Kongreso ay nagdarasal muna ng invocation. Pati po ang hudicatura, mula sa Kataas Taasang Hukuman hanggang sa mga Municipal Courts, nagsisimula po ang mga hearing sa pagdarasal ng Centennial Prayer for the Courts.

To sum up, the Philippines is theist, not atheist, not even agnostic. In fact, it is monotheist; it worships one God. That is why, its concepts and interpretations of the separation of the Church and the State, though originating from the United States, have been evolved over the last one hundred years in a uniquely Filipino manner, as the Malolos Constitution itself is uniquely Filipino.

Maraming salamat po.

## Summary

Delivered January 29, 2008 at Bulacan State University -- where CJP holds an honorary Doctor of Laws and considers himself a BSU alumnus ('itinuring ko na po ang aking sarili na isa na ring alumnus ng BSU') -- on the occasion of the Malolos Constitution celebration, this bilingual address (Filipino opening and close; English main body) argues that the Malolos Constitution deserves celebration not only as a symbol of independence but because it proves that the Philippines was already a constitutional democracy over 100 years ago. The speech then tackles whether priests commenting on corruption/EJKs and officials attending prayer rallies violate church-state separation. The historical context: the 1876 Spanish Constitution made Catholicism the state religion (friars exercised civil powers; civil authorities performed religious functions); the 1898 Treaty of Paris guaranteed religious freedom; on January 22, 1899, the Malolos Constitution's Article 5 declared 'The State recognizes the freedom and equality of all religions, as well as the separation of the Church and the State.' CJP analyzes the non-establishment clause (two purposes: encourage voluntarism; insulate political process from interfaith dissension; government must be neutral on religion) and the free exercise clause (freedom of belief is limitless; exercise can be regulated; Philippine SC carves out exceptions to accommodate religious exercise). His conclusion: the Philippines has evolved a 'uniquely Filipino' concept of church-state separation over 100 years -- different from the strict US atheistic model; 'the Philippines is theist, not atheist, not even agnostic. In fact, it is monotheist; it worships one God. That is why, its concepts and interpretations of the separation of the Church and the State... have been evolved over the last one hundred years in a uniquely Filipino manner, as the Malolos Constitution itself is uniquely Filipino.' Evidence: Cabinet sessions, Congressional sessions, and court hearings all open with invocations.
