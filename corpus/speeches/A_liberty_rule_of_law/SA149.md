# Access to Justice

Lecture delivered by Chief Justice Artemio V. Panganiban during the inauguration of the Dr. Jovito R. Salonga Center for Law and Development on September 16, 2006, at the Luce Auditorium, Silliman University, Dumaguete City.

---

I feel privileged to be here today in this great institution of learning to deliver the first inaugural lecture for the Dr. Jovito R. Salonga Center for Law and Development. I must congratulate the College of Law of Silliman University for conceiving of and giving life to this Center which, with Senator Salonga's inspiration, will surely be in the forefront of this University's vision and mission of learning and serving. By deciding to pioneer in transformative law — which means harnessing the law to transform society and to improve the lives of the people — the Center institutionalizes Dr. Salonga's deep-seated compassion for the poor and the downtrodden.

UNDP STUDY ON ACCESS TO JUSTICE

According to the United Nations Development Programme, access to justice is the ability of people to seek and obtain a remedy through formal or informal institutions of justice, and in conformity with human rights standards.

In the Philippines, a survey of private legal practitioners was conducted in 2004 to monitor access to justice by the disadvantaged. It reported some disturbing findings. Lawyering for the poor had an urban bias. Courts were also located mostly in urban centers. For the rural poor, the physical inaccessibility of the courts posed difficulties in access to justice. Loss of income appeared to be the strongest deterrent to lawyering for the poor, especially among young and new lawyers.

ACCESS TO JUSTICE AND POVERTY REDUCTION

The UNDP observes that access to justice is essential to poverty eradication and human development. Groups such as the poor and disadvantaged who suffer from discrimination also often fall victim to criminal and illegal acts. Crime and illegality are likely to have a greater impact on poor and disadvantaged people's lives, as it is harder for them to seek redress.

ACCESS TO JUSTICE PROJECTS

To implement the pro-poor constitutional provisions, the Supreme Court has opened the justice system to indigents through various measures. It has exempted poor litigants from the payment of docket and other fees; protected and enforced the constitutional rights of the accused; and granted free legal counsel to poor litigants.

To increase the accessibility of the judicial system, especially to the poor and the disadvantaged, the Supreme Court launched its Justice on Wheels program on December 21, 2004. The mobile courts are designed to bring the courts to places where they would otherwise be inaccessible.

The Supreme Court also conducts pro-poor, pro-labor and pro-human rights decisions. In Ang Bagong Bayani-OFW Labor Party v. Commission on Elections, it held that the Filipino-style party-list system is reserved for the poor and the marginalized.

LIBERTY AND PROSPERITY: TWIN BEACONS FOR THE POOR

In addition, I have crafted a judicial philosophy founded on the firm belief that justice and progress must be directed primarily to assist the poor, the marginalized and the disadvantaged. The ACID reforms and the four Ins I vowed to pursue for the judiciary must lead to the attainment of two loftier end goals: safeguarding the liberty and nurturing the prosperity of our people.

I urge you to contribute fully and meaningfully to discussions, bearing in mind that the results of this conference will lay the groundwork for the forthcoming Global Conference on Liberty and Prosperity scheduled on October 18-20, 2006. During that global gathering, we hope to spread the gospel of Liberty and Prosperity beyond our shores and unto the world.

Maraming salamat po.

## Notable Anecdotes

### Anecdote 1

Transformative law: the Silliman Salonga Center is described as pioneering 'transformative law -- which means harnessing the law to transform society and to improve the lives of the people' -- CJP's most complete definition of the social-engineering role of law as an academic discipline.

### Anecdote 2

Income-loss as the strongest deterrent: the 2004 Philippine UNDP survey finding that loss of income (not lack of training, not distance) is 'the strongest deterrent to lawyering for the poor, especially among young and new lawyers' -- the most empirically specific access-to-justice diagnosis.
