# Transforming the Judiciary

Address delivered by Retired Chief Justice Artemio V. Panganiban before a meeting of the US-Asean Business Council held on March 30, 2011 at the SGV Lounge, SGV Building 6760 Ayala Avenue, Makati City.

---

At the outset, I say welcome to the US-Asean Business Council, especially its President Alexander C. Feldman, for flying all the way from Washington DC to assess the business climate in our country under our new President Benigno Aquino III. I thank the venerable founder of the SGV Group, my good friend Washington Sycip, current SGV Chairman Cirilo P. Noel and Asian Institute of Management President Edilberto De Jesus for inviting me to address your forum this afternoon.

THREE MAIN COMPLAINTS AGAINST THE JUDICIARY

Let me preface my short remarks by relating three incidents in the fictional life of Judge Cerapio I. Delayo, who for easy recollection, we will just address by his initials, CID.

One early morning while our judge was having breakfast at home, a lawyer with a multi-billion case visited him. Why are you here? Do you not know it is unethical to talk with a judge about a pending case outside the courtroom? the magistrate asked. No, Your Honor, I did not come to speak with you about my case. But if you will look outside your window, you will see a sparkling new Mercedes Benz S560 parked on your driveway. That is the worse; you have come to bribe me! No again, Your Honor. I am not giving you the car. I am selling it to you. And how much is the car? His Honor asked. Fifty dollars, the smart attorney answered. In that case, Judge CID solemnly said, I will buy two!

These three apocryphal incidents in the life of fictitious Judge CID may be funny, but they illustrate the three most common complaints against our judiciary: corruption, incompetence and delay, or CID, in the delivery of justice.

THREE PROPOSALS TO SOLVE THE COMPLAINTS

To address these problems, three proposals have been put forward: first, overhaul the constitutional system; second, pass new laws to increase judicial compensation and penalize corrupt practices; and third, strengthen the ethical backbone of our judges.

With due respect, I disagree that constitutional change is the answer. I believe that the presidential system, which we borrowed from the United States and improved upon based on lessons learned from the Marcos years, is not the source of our problems. The US Constitution does not provide for an independent Commission on Election, yet elections in America are generally peaceful and orderly. Constitutions are not made so people would adjust to them. Rather, it is the other way around.

I also believe the second proposal, to pass new laws to increase judicial salaries and combat corruption, is also not the priority. During my incumbency in the Supreme Court, the legislature already doubled judicial compensations. We have enough laws to punish crimes.

THE RIGHT SOLUTION TO THE COMPLAINTS

If overhauling the Charter and the enactment of new laws are the wrong solutions to excessive corruption and grinding poverty, what is the right one? It is changing the officials who subvert our institutions and make a mockery of our laws. More accurately, what we need is not just a change of leaders, but a change in our leaders, in the men and women who run our institutions and systems. A change from the inside out, a change of hearts, a change of values, a change in ethical backbone.

What we need are courageous, competent, ethical and patriotic leaders who will make our institutions and systems work, who will enforce our existing laws, who will fulfill our grand visions and programs, who will put our nation's interest above their own.

Modesty aside, during my incumbency in the Supreme Court, we put in place a full-scale program to reform our judicial machinery called the Action Program for Judicial Reform or APJR. The program included the establishment and maintenance of the Philippine Judicial Academy to provide continuing legal education for judges. As Chief Justice, I was able to obtain from the Japanese government a grant of P300 million, which was used to construct the Academy's buildings in Tagaytay City. We also instituted a program of Mandatory Continuing Legal Education under which all lawyers are required to undergo a retraining every three years to renew their licenses to practice. To address the problem of delay, we filled up the vacant judgeships in the country and monitored the flow of cases by a computerization program supported by the World Bank, the US AID, the Canadian International Development Agency and the European Union.

In fact, I am proud to tell you that our APJR was cited by the World Bank as its official model in assisting other judiciaries in the world. Unfortunately, after I retired in 2006, the Court gave little priority to the Action Program for Judicial Reform. Sadly, the APJR was taken off the World Bank's website as a model judicial reform program.

One final note. During my incumbency in the Supreme Court, I espoused a clear judicial philosophy of Liberty and Prosperity. Under this philosophy, the judiciary helps advance the public weal by adopting two standards of judicial review: In litigations involving civil liberties, the scales of justice should weigh heavily against the government and in favor of the people, particularly the poor and the marginalized. In conflicts affecting prosperity, development and the economy, deference must be accorded to the political branches of government, for they know best what is good for the well being of our citizenry.

To close, may I say that the Philippines should be experiencing a renaissance in the near future, not only in the judiciary but also in the entire country. As our favorite investors, coming from our favorite ally and benefactor, the United States of America, I hope you will find renewed trust in our economy and will be the first to place your investments in the new Philippines. Join the battle cry of President Noynoy Aquino, Kung Walang Corrupt, Walang Mahirap. Let us all join the war against corruption and poverty. I thank you.
