# Nurturing Lives, Fulfilling Dreams

Address delivered by retired Chief Justice ARTEMIO V. PANGANIBAN during the commencement exercises at the Taguig City University on May 4, 2016.

---

I thank Mr. Aurelio Paulo R. Bartolome, President of the Taguig City University for inviting me as your guest speaker and to speak on the theme, Nurturing Lives, Fulfilling Dreams.

Dear graduates, let me underscore how blessed all of you are to be beneficiaries of Taguig City's generosity in giving you the opportunity to secure a college education for free. Let us hail Mayor Lani Cayetano and the Taguig City Council for making this Graduation Day possible.

Like many of you, I was born poor. My father barely finished high school while my mother did not even make it through elementary school. Being the eldest of his seven siblings, my father had to take care of his brothers and sisters after their own father died at an early age. However, as a simple rank-and-file government employee, he could hardly afford to send all his dependents to school.

As the youngest in the family, I had to work at a very young age, hawking newspapers, selling cigarettes and shining shoes along the streets of Manila. Many times, I had to sleep on the sidewalks to be ready for the morning delivery of the newspapers I hawked. Envious of my classmates who were still asleep in their homes, tears would well up my eyes. I told myself, Today, I am just a newsboy. But someday, I will be president of the Manila Times, then the largest-circulated daily in our country. Yes, I dreamed big. And much later on in 1991, prior to my stint in the Supreme Court, I became president of the Philippine Daily Inquirer, the largest-circulated daily currently.

My classmates at Mapa High School and I used to go to the University of the Philippines in Quezon City. As an honor student in a class of over 1,200 high school graduates, I was granted a UP scholarship but I still could not enroll in my chosen university because my impoverished father could not afford the then 15-centavo bus ride from our rented apartment in Cataluna Street, Sampaloc, Manila to the UP campus in Quezon City.

Frustrated, I applied for a scholarship at the nearby University of Santo Tomas. The old Dominican priest who interviewed me asked: how many Gods are there? One. How many persons are there in one God? Three. Name them. Believe it or not, I did not know the answer. So, I sheepishly said, Jesus, Mary, Joseph. Yes, I failed the test and was refused a scholarship. Dejected, I went to FEU which was also nearby, where I was granted a scholarship with no questions asked.

At FEU, I met the brilliant Dr. Jovito R. Salonga, our young law dean, who taught me what he learned from UP, Harvard, and Yale. He took a personal liking for me and consistently monitored my progress.

I also met arts and sciences Dean Alejandro R. Roces who, at 37, was later appointed the youngest secretary of education. And I likewise befriended Fr. Michael Nolan, the erudite FEU chaplain, who taught me the rudiments of my Catholic faith.

When it was time to take the bar examinations in 1960, I got sick and was hospitalized. On the first day of the exam, a heavy downpour hit the city. I had to walk in knee-deep flood water to the University of the East where the exams were being held. When the results came out, I still managed to place 6th.

As for my personal career, I got to the Supreme Court without really aspiring for it. One day in June 1992, the then newly-elected president of our country, Fidel V. Ramos, invited me to a one-on-one meeting in his private office in Makati. He offered me to join his Cabinet as Secretary of Justice. However, I respectfully declined. Because of these reservations, he instead offered me a seat in the Supreme Court, an offer I could not refuse because it is the dream of every lawyer. After 10 years of faithful service in the Court, I was named by then President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo as Chief Justice.

What lessons can we learn from this brief summation of my life? There are at least five: First, be prepared for hard work. Second, always strive for excellence. Third, remember to persevere. Fourth, be kind and forgiving. And fifth, always have faith in God.

To conclude, I have journeyed from being a poor newsboy in the backstreets of Sampaloc to being president of the most widely read newspaper in our country; from a Catholic ignoramus to a member of the highest lay council of the Catholic Church in the Vatican; from a frustrated applicant for graduate studies to father of five wonderful children who each achieved my impossible dream of finishing in a pedigreed US university; from an aspiring chemical engineer to a reluctant lawyer and, finally, to the highest magistrate of our country.

As I contemplate my life and move toward its sunset, I know that God has woven my many pains and gains into a magnificent tapestry showing His mystical presence. Truly, there is one constancy in my life: the presence, care and providence of our Lord and Savior, Jesus Christ. To Him, I dedicate all that I have been, all that I am, and all that I will ever be. To God be the glory!

## Summary

Delivered May 4, 2016 as the commencement address at Taguig City University on the theme 'Nurturing Lives, Fulfilling Dreams,' this speech by CJP opens by calling the TCU graduates 'blessed' for receiving free college education courtesy of Mayor Lani Cayetano and the Taguig City Council -- a generosity that directly mirrors the barrier CJP himself faced: an honor student granted a UP scholarship but unable to enroll because his father could not afford the 15-centavo bus fare from Cataluna Street, Sampaloc to Quezon City. CJP recounts his story from child laborer (hawking newspapers, selling cigarettes, shining shoes; sleeping on sidewalks for morning deliveries; promising himself he would be president of the Manila Times, fulfilled as Inquirer President in 1991) through the UST Trinity failure ('Jesus, Mary, Joseph' -- denied scholarship; went to FEU where he was 'granted a scholarship with no questions asked') through FEU's gifts (Salonga taught him UP-Harvard-Yale; Roces later became youngest Edu Secretary; Fr. Nolan taught him the faith) through the 1960 bar exams (sick, hospitalized, waded knee-deep through flood, placed 6th) to President-elect Ramos's June 1992 offer of the Secretary of Justice position (declined) and then the Supreme Court ('an offer I could not refuse because it is the dream of every lawyer') to the Chief Justice appointment by GMA. He distills five lessons: hard work, excellence, perseverance, kindness and forgiveness, faith. He maps four journey arcs: poor newsboy to Inquirer President; Catholic ignoramus to Vatican lay council; frustrated US-graduate applicant to father of five US university graduates; aspiring chemical engineer to Chief Justice. He closes: 'God has woven my many pains and gains into a magnificent tapestry showing His mystical presence... To God be the glory!'
