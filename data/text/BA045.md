# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Ch. 10: Agan v. Piatco — Validity of a BOT Contract
Source:     The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary (book)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

Chapter 10

Agan v. Piatco:

Validity of a BOT Contract

One of the most publicized cases during my eighth year in office was Agan v. Piatco. It occupied the attention of the media and the general public for many months. Various parties, including several members of Congress, challenged five agreements executed by the Philippine government with the Philippine International Air Terminals Co., Inc. (Piatco) for the construction and the operation of a new international airport terminal in the National Capital Region ("Terminal III”).

The Facts

In 1993, six business leaders of the country - John Gokongwei, Andrew Gotianum, Henry Sy Sr., Lucio Tan, George Ty and Alfonso Yuchengco - formed Asia's Emerging Dragon Corporation (AEDC).

On October 5, 1993, AEDC submitted an unsolicited proposal to the government for the construction of the Ninoy Aquino International Airport Terminal III under the buildoperate-and-transfer (BOT) scheme governed by RA 6957.

The proposal was endorsed by the Department of Transportation and Communications (DOTC) to the National Economic Development Authority (NEDA ICC), which in turn approved it on February 13, 1996 through Board Resolution No. 2.

Vis-a-vis AEDC's unsolicited proposal, DOTC caused the publication in two daily newspapers of an invitation for competitive or comparative proposals, pursuant to the BOT Law. To supervise the bidding, a Prequalification and Bidding Committee (PBAC) was formed by the government.

On September 20, 1996, the Paircargo Consortium - composed of People’s Air Cargo and Grounds Services, Inc. (PAGS) and Security Bank Corporation - submitted a competitive proposal to the PBAC.

After resolving some initial reservations and over the objections of AEDC, PBAC accepted the "prequalification" of the consortium. PBAC's action was later approved by the DOTC secretary.

Both AEDC and Paircargo offered (a) to build Terminal III and (b) to pay the government 5 percent share in gross revenues for the first five years of operation, 7.5 percent for the next ten years, and 10 percent for the last ten years. In addition, however, AEDC offered to pay the government a total of P135 million as guaranteed payment for 27 years, while Paircargo offered P17.75 billion - repeat, billion for the same period.

PBAC accepted the Paircargo price proposal and gave AEDC 30 working days or until November 30, 1996 within which to match the bid. AEDC failed to do so. It objected to the alleged undue preference given to the Philippine International Airport Terminals Co., Inc. or Piatco, into which the Paircargo had incorporated itself on February 27, 1997. Because its objections remained unheeded, AEDC filed in the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City a suit to annul the award.

Despite this suit, the government (through DOTC) and Piatco signed on July 12, 1997 the "Concession Agreement for the Build-Operate-and-Transfer Arrangement of the Ninoy Aquino International Airport Passenger Terminal III" (CA). On November 26, 1998, the parties signed an "Amended and Restated Concession Agreement" (ARCA), which modified several provisions of the original CA. Thereafter, the parties executed three supplements to the ARCA. The First Supplement (FS) was signed on August 27, 1999, the Second (SS) on September 4, 2000 and the Third (TS) on June 22, 2001.

On November 29, 2002, President Gloria Macapagal [the Arroyo declared in a speech that she would not "honor Piatco] contracts which the Executive Branch's legal offices have concluded [are] null and void." On March 6, 2003, Piatco informed the Supreme Court that it had commenced arbitration proceedings before the International Chamber of Commerce against the Philippine government, even while the present case was then pending resolution by the Court.

The Issues

Two sets of issues were raised by the parties. The first set was procedural: Did the Supreme Court have original jurisdiction to hear and decide the Petitions? Though labeled as Petitions for Prohibitions, were they in reality in the n of complaints for declaratory relief, which should have been filed in the regional trial courts? Did petitioners have locus standi? Should the Court decide the cases directly and ignore Piatco's resort to arbitration?

The second set of issues was substantive in character: Was the bidding process conducted in a manner that was unduly favorable to Piatco? Was the 1997 Concession Agreement void? Did the challenged contracts require the government to guarantee Piatco's foreign borrowings?

The Court's Decision, written by Justice Reynato S. Puno, brushed aside all the procedural objections and answered all the substantive questions in the affirmative.

On the Procedural Issues

The Puno ponencia ruled that all the petitioners had standing, because their rights and interests had been violated by the implementation of the Piatco contracts. The Petitions contended that, by entering into those contracts, the DOTC "committed grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction which can be remedied only by a writ of prohibition, there being no plain, speedy or adequate remedy in the ordinary course of law." Wrote Justice Puno:

"We hold that petitioners have the requisite standing. In the above-mentioned cases, petitioners have a direct and substantial interest to protect by reason of the implementation of the PIATCO Contracts. They stand to lose their source of livelihood, a property right which is zealously protected by the Constitution. Moreover, subsisting concession agreements between MIAA and petitioners-intervenors in these cases are legitimate interests sufficient to confer on them the requisite standing to file the instant petitions."

On the standing of the members of Congress, the ponencia opined:

"Standing is a peculiar concept in constitutional law because in some cases, suits are not brought by parties who have been personally injured by the operation of a law or any other government act but by concerned citizens, taxpayers or voters who actually sue in the public interest. Although we are not unmindful of the cases of Imus Electric Co. v. Municipality of Imus and Gonzales v. Raquiza wherein this Court held that appropriation must be made only on amounts immediately demandable, public interest demands that we take a more liberal view in determining whether the petitioners suing as legislators, taxpayers and citizens have locus standi to file the instant petition. In Kilosbayan, Inc. v. Guingona, this Court held '[i]n line with the liberal policy of this Court on locus standi, ordinary taxpayers, members of Congress, and even association of planters, and non-profit civic organizations were allowed to initiate and prosecute actions before this Court to question the constitutionality or validity of laws, acts, decisions, rulings, or orders of various government agencies or instrumentalities. Further, 'insofar as taxpayers' suits are concerned... (this Court) is not devoid of discretion as to whether or not it should be entertained.’ As such’...even if, strictly speaking, they [the petitioners] are not covered by the definition, it is still within the wide discretion of the Court to waive the requirement and so remove the impediment to its addressing and resolving the serious constitutional questions raised. In view of the serious legal questions involved and their impact on public interest, we resolve to grant standing to the petitioners."

The ponencia also ruled that the hierarchy-of-courts principle, requiring that cases should be initiated in the trial courts, "may be relaxed when the redress desired cannot be obtained in the appropriate courts, or where exceptional and compelling circumstances justify availment of a remedy within and call for the exercise of this Court's primary jurisdiction."

Both petitioners and respondents agree that "these cases are of transcendental importance, as they involve the construction and operation of the country's premier international airport. Moreover, the crucial issues submitted for resolution are of first impression x x x."

The arbitration step taken by Piatco did not oust the Court from its jurisdiction over the case. Citing Del Monte v. Court of Appeals, the ponencia explained that the arbitration clause in the contracts had bound only the parties thereto. In the present case, it was clear that many of the petitioners - were not privy to the assailed agreements. Hence, they were not bound by the arbitration clause.

The Substantive Questions

The Court held that Piatco did not have the financial capacity required by the BOT Law. Since the minimum project cost was estimated to be US$350 million or US$ P9,183,650,000, Paircargo consortium had to show to the satisfaction of the PBAC that it had the ability, as required by the BOT Law, to provide the minimum equity for the project in the amount of at least P2,755,095,000."

The Consortium, on the other hand, had a total net worth of only "P558,384,871.55 or only 6.08% of the project cost." The Court said that Security Bank, a consortium member, had a total equity "of P3,523,504,377 [which] cannot be taken into the Consortium's networth,’            because the General Banking Act provides that the investment of a bank in the equity of "any one enterprise, x x x shall not exceed fifteen percent (15%) of the net worth of the bank."

"The PBAC should not be allowed to speculate on the future financial ability of the bidder to undertake the project on the basis of the documents submitted. This would open doors to abuse and defeat the very purpose of public bidding."

Validity of the 1997

Concession Agreement

The Court held that the 1997 Concession Agreement was invalid, because "it contains provisions that substantially in depart from the draft Concession Agreement included the Bid Documents." Added the Court:

"An essential element of a publicly bidded contract is that all bidders must be on equal footing. Not simply in terms of application of the procedural rules and regulations imposed by the relevant government agency, but more importantly, on the contract bidded upon. Each bidder must be able to bid on the same thing. The rationale is obvious.If the winning bidder is allowed to later include or modify certain provisions in the contract awarded such that the contract is altered in any material respect, then the essence of fair competition in the public bidding is destroyed. A public bidding would indeed be a farce if after the contract is awarded, the winning provisions which are favorable to it that were not previously made available to the other bidders. Thus:

‘It is inherent in public biddings that there shall be a fair competition among the bidders. The specifications in such biddings provide the common ground or basis for the bidders. The specifications should, accordingly, operate equally or indiscriminately upon all bidders.

"While we concede that a winning bidder is not precluded from modifying or amending certain provisions of the contract bidded upon, such changes must not constitute substantial or material amendments that would alter the basic parameters of the contract and would constitute a denial to the other bidders of the opportunity to bid on the same terms. Hence, the determination of whether or not a modification or amendment of a contract bidded out constitutes a substantial amendment rests on whether the contract, when taken as a whole, would contain substantially different terms and conditions that would have the effect of altering the technical and/o r financial proposals previously submitted by other bidders. The alterations and modifications in the contract executed between the government and winning bidder must be such as to render such executed contract to be an entirely different contract from the one that was bidded upon."

After analyzing and comparing the signed 1997 CA and the draft Concession Agreement, the Court said that the former “clearly gives Piatco more favorable terms than what was available to other bidders at the time the contract was bidded out."

The ponencia also said that under the 1997 Concession Agreement, particularly Section 4.04 thereof, "default by Piatco of its loans used to finance the NAIA IPT Ill project triggers the occurrence of certain events that leads to the assumption by the government of the liability for the loans.” Again, this new provision "grants Piatco a financial advantage or benefit which was not previously made available during the bidding process." Explained the Decision:

"Public bidding is a standard practice for procuring government contracts for public service and for furnishing supplies and other materials. It aims to secure for the government the lowest possible price under the most favorable terms and conditions, to curtail favoritism in the award of government contracts and avoid suspicion of anomalies and it places all bidders on equal footing. Any government action which permits any substantial variance between the conditions under which the bids are invited and the contract executed after the award thereof is a grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction which warrants proper judicial action.

"In view of the above discussion, the fact that the foregoing substantial amendments were made on the 1997 Concession Agreement renders the same null and void for being contrary to public policy. These amendments convert the 1997 Concession Agreement to an entirely different agreement from the contract bidded out or the draft Concession Agreement. It is not difficult to see that the amendments on (1) the types of fees or charges that are subject to MIAA regulation or control and the extent thereof and (2) the assumption by the Government, under certain conditions, of the liabilities of PIATCO directly translates concrete financial advantages to PIATCO that were previously not available during the bidding merely supplements to or implementing provisions of those already existing in the draft Concession Agreement. The amendments discussed above present new terms and conditions which provide financial benefit to PIATCO which may have altered the technical and financial parameters of other bidders had they known that such terms were available."

Direct Government Guarantee

More than merely violating the "level the playing field" policy of government, Section 4.04 in relation to Section 1.06 of the CA places the government "at risk of being liable to Piatco's creditors should the latter be unable to designate effect, whatever option the government chooses to take in the event of Piatco's failure to fulfill its obligations, the government is still at risk of assuming Piatco’s outstanding loans."

This CA provision is prohibited by Section 2(n) of the BOT Law. This violation was not cured by the ARCA, which even "provides for a direct guarantee by the government to pay Piatco's loans not only to its Senior Lenders but to all other entities who provided Piatco funds or services upon Piatco's default in its loan obligation with its Senior Lenders." On this point, the ponencia elucidates:

"This Court has long and consistently adhered to the legal maxim that those that cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly." To declare the Piatco contracts valid despite the clear statutory prohibition against a direct government guarantee would not only make a mockery of what the BOT Law seeks to prevent - which is to expose the government to the risk of incurring a monetary obligation resulting from a contract of loan between the project proponent and its lenders and to which the government is not a party to - but would also render the BOT Law useless for what it seeks to achieve - to make use of the resources s of the private sector in the 'financing, operation and maintenance of infrastructure and development projects' which are necessary for have national growth and development but which the government, unfortunately, could ill-afford to finance at this point in time."

Temporary Government

Takeover Without Payment of Compensation

After declaring the Piatco contracts void, the Puno Decision - citing Section 17 of Article XII of the Constitution? - said that the government could "temporarily" take over the operation of Terminal Ill without being "required to compensate the private entity-owner of the said business as there is no transfer of ownership, whether permanent or temporary. The private entity-owner claim affected by the temporary takeover cannot, likewise,and  its just compensation for the use of the said business in the exercise of its police power and not the power of eminent domain." 31 Added the ponencia:

"Piatco cannot, by mere contractual stipulation, contravene the Constitutional provision on temporary government takeover and obligate use the government to pay 'reasonable cost for the le Artic of the Terminal and/ or Terminal Complex'isio ns a XII, Section 17 of the 1987 Constitution env situation wherein the exigencies of the times necessitate the government to 'temporarily takeover or direct the operation of any privately owned public utility or business affected with public interest. It is the welfare and interest of the public which is the paramount consideration in determining whether or not to temporarily take over a particular business. Clearly, the State in effecting the temporary takeover is exercising its police power. Police power is the ‘most essential, insistent, and illimitable of powers.’ Its exercise therefore must not be unreasonably hampered nor its exercise be a source of obligation by the government in the absence of damage due to arbitrariness of its exercise. Thus, requiring the government to pay reasonable compensation for the reasonable use of the property pursuant to the operation of the business contravenes the Constitution."

Regulation of Monopoly

Piatco was granted by the 1997 CA "exclusive right to operate a commercial international passenger terminal within the Island of Luzon" with the exception of those already operating like Subic, Clark and Laoag. Hence, the ponencia held that, under Section 19 of Article XII of the Constitution, the State may regulate and supervise the operation of Terminal III.

Finally, the Decision also extended protection to service providers presently operating in Terminal I, as follows:

"We hold that while the service providers presently operating at NAIA Terminal I do not have an absolute right for the renewal or the extension of their respective contracts, those contracts whose duration extends beyond NAIA IPT III's In-Service-Date should not be unduly prejudiced. These contracts must be respected not just by the parties thereto but also by third parties. Piatco cannot, by this portion of law and certainly not by contract, render a valid and binding contract nugatory. Piatco, by the mere expedient of claiming an exclusive right to operate, cannot require the Government to break its contractual obligations to the service providers. In contrast to the arrastre and stevedoring service providers in the case of Anglo-Fil Trading Corporation v. Lazaro whose contracts consist of temporary hold-over permits, the affected service providers in the cases at bar have a valid and binding contract with the Government, through MIAA, whose period of effectivity, as well as the other terms and conditions thereof, cannot be violated."

I now quote verbatim the summation of Justice Puno's 76-page ponencia:

"In sum, this Court rules that in view of the absence of the requisite financial capacity of the Paircargo Consortium, predecessor of respondent Piatco, the award by the PBAC of the contract for the construction, operation and maintenance of the NAIA IPT III is null and void. Further, considering that the 1997 Concession Agreement contains material and substantial amendments, which amendments had the effect of converting the 1997 Concession Agreement into an entirely different agreement from the contract bidded upon, the 1997 Concession Agreement is similarly null and void for being contrary to public policy. The provisions under Sections 4.04(b) and (c) in relation to Section 1.06 of the 1997 Concession Agreement and Section 4.04(c) in relation to Section 1.06 of the ARCA, which constitute a direct government guarantee expressly prohibited by, among others, the BOT Law and its Implementing Rules and Regulations, are also null and void. The Supplements, being accessory contracts to the ARCA, are likewise null and void."

My Separate Opinion

I wrote a rather lengthy 98-page Separate Opinion concurring in the ponencia's disposition that the five Piatco contracts were null and void. But I took issue with the ruling that the government could take over the new Terminal "temporarily" without having to pay just compensation.

My Separate Opinion no longer narrated the factual antecedents, because these were already adequately presented in the ponencia. Instead, it tackled directly and immediately the two sets of issues: the procedural and the substantive.

The Procedural Issues

I discussed in detail each of the procedural questions raised but, in this summary, let me just say that when the whole country, the Court had in the past waived procedural obstacles. The subject contracts pertain to the construction and the operation of the country's premiere international airport terminal that is expected to play a major role in the country's economic development and international image. These five BOT contracts entailed investments and loans in billions of pesos and contained provisions that tended to violate constitutional and legal prohibitions. Clearly, public interest was inevitably affected. In any event, sending the cases to the regional trial court would just have delayed the final resolution of the cases; after all, the proceedings would ultimately be appealed to the High Court. Thus, it w a s to everyone's benefit that the matter be resolved immediately.

The Substantive Issues

In discussing the substantive issues, I tracked the incidents in the Piatco contracts from their inception up to the filing of the Petitions.I found that the whole process was riddled with significant lapses.

Lapses During the

Bidding Process

1. Questionable Prequalification of Paircargo

To begin with, the Paircargo consortium was not even qualified to participate in the bidding process, as it had failed to meet the financial standard for prequalification: a combined net worth of at least 30 percent of the project cost, which was US$350 million. The consortium had a combined investible net worth of only P558 million, far below the required P2.755 billion minimum equity. Hence, it should have been rejected outright.

Instead, the chair of the Prequalification and Bidding Committee declared Paircargo prequalified. In violation of Section 21-B of the General Banking Act - which provides that a commercial bank's equity investment in any one enterprise, allied or non-allied, should not exceed 15 percent of the net worth of the investing bank — he treated the entire P3.524 billion equity of Security Bank, a consortium member, as investible in the project. In so doing, he likewise contravened the financial capability requirement of the BOT Law, its 1994 Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR), as well as the Supreme Court's consistent position emphasizing the strict application of pertinent rules, regulations and guidelines for the public bidding process.

The essence of public bidding is an opportunity for fair competition and a basis for a precise comparison of bids. Each bidder must bid under the same conditions; and be subject to the same guidelines, requirements and limitation, so that the best offer or lowest bid may be determined, all n other things being equal. Thus, to permit a variance between under the conditions under which bids are invited and those which proposals are submitted and approved is to go against the very concept of public bidding.

2. AEDC's Deprivation of the Right to Match Piatco's Price Challenge

Another problem followed almost immediately. AEDC, being the originator of the unsolicited proposal for Terminal IlI, had the right under the BOT Law to make a reasonably informed bid and match the price challenge of the Paircargo consortium. Yet, it was effectively shut out of the game altogether and deprived of this right. Misapplying a confidentiality provision in the 1994 IRR, the PBAC chair declared that AEDC would be permitted access only to the schedule of proposed annual guaranteed payments, but not to the financial and technical proposals of Piatco - the basis for the price challenge in the first place.

3. Substantial Delay in the Issuance of the Notice of Award

Specific guidelines and clear timetables are prescribed by the IRR for the purpose of discouraging collusion and reducing the opportunity for abuse in the course of the award process. Still, several lengthy and unexplained delays occurred in the process of issuing the Notice of Award and, thus, in executing the 1997 Concession Agreement (CA). Because such Notice had set forth specific timetables - for the winning bidder to submit the prescribed performance security, proof of commitment of equity contributions, and indications of sources of financing — the delay in the issuance thereof undoubtedly benefited Piatco by allowing it more time for compliance.

As the entire bidding process was flawed from the outset, there was effectively no public bidding to speak of. Therefore, the award of the concession to Paircargo was void; so were the Concession and consequent Agreements executed with it likewise void ab initio. For this reason, Piatco, into which Paircargo was later incorporated on February 27, 1997, should not be allowed to benefit from those contracts.

Lapses After the

Bidding Process

The violations did not stop there. After the contract had been awarded, Piatco still negotiated changes therein and ended up with what amounted to a substantially new contract without any public bidding.

1. The 1997 Concession Agreement

Thus, the CA, executed on July 12, 1997, differed from the original Draft Concession Agreement (DCA) in several very material and significant respects. For purposes of easier understanding and later comparison, the CA's provisions may be presented, though not originally classified, under the following headings:

a. The Grant of Exclusive Rights

• Piatco has the exclusive right to airport-related services for international airlines and passengers.

• Certain fees, rentals and other charges (food services; lease/rental charges; transportation utility concessions; concession privilege fees for passenger service

ground handling, catering and other fees) may be adjusted without MIAA's approval or intervention.

b.   Provisions on Piatco's Liabilities

• The government shall answer for the unpaid loans and debts (" Attendant Liabilities") of Piatco in the event the latter fails to pay its Senior Lenders.

• To satisfy the claims of its secured creditors first, Piatco is allowed to suspend payments to the government.

c.   Termination Provision

•    In case of contract termination due to its fault, the government shall pay all expenses that Piatco incurred for the project plus the appraised value of the Terminal.

Obviously, such substantial changes in the original had an existing policy on public bidding as well as the spirit and intent of the BOT Law. The whole point of going through the public bidding exercise was totally subverted. Allowing the winning bidder to renegotiate substantial portions of the original contract was tantamount to permitting it to put in anything it wanted.

2. The Amended and Restated Concession Agreement

Not satisfied with the 1997 Concession Agreement, Piatco - once more without bothering with public bidding - negotiated with the government for still more substantive changes. The result was the ARCA, which was executed on November 26, 1998. Again, for purposes of easier understanding and comparison, ARCA's features are grouped as follows:

a. The Grant of Exclusive Rights

• The exclusive rights of Piatco were extended: DOTC/MIAA may no longer intervene when the former's non-public utility revenues become exorbitant or excessive.

b. Provisions on Piatco's Liabilities

• The definition of Attendant Liabilities was further revised, such that the unpaid loans of Piatco for which the government may be required to answer are no longer limited to those recorded in the former's books or to those that were actually used in the Terminal Ill project.

• The parametric formula for the computation of adjustments/ increases in the public utility revenues (from the aircraft parking and tacking fees and check-in counter and terminal fees) was revised to permit Piatco to input its more costly short-term borrowing rates, thus lessening the revenues that should go to the government.

• The insurance policies Piatco obtained previously intended for the repair and rehabilitation of the facility in case of damage - are now assigned to the Senior Lenders as security for its loans.

• The government's right to appoint a comptroller for Piatco in case it encounters liquidity problems was removed.

c.   Termination Provisions

• In case of breach of contract by Piatco, it shall not under certain circumstances be liable to pay liquidated damages to the government; but in case of the latter's breach, liquidated damages not only have to be paid in a substantially increased amount.

• Even if the government is entitled to terminate the ARCA on account of Piatco's breach, if the termination occurs before the in-service date, it shall be liable to pay Piatco the appraised value of Terminal III or the latter's attendant liabilities - whichever is greater.

• The government shall waive the defense of illegality of contract in the event a case arises; if the contract is nevertheless declared illegal, it shall be liable to pay damages to Piatco.

d. Special Obligations Imposed on the Government

• The government shall be liable for incremental and consequential costs and losses in case it fails to comply with the special obligations imposed on it by Piatco.

Patently, the foregoing revisions and amendments as embodied in the ARCA constitute very material alterations of the CA and manifestly give further undue advantage to Piatco at the expense of the government. Although Piatco reasoned that the changes were necessitated by the demands -    of its foreign lenders, no proof whatsoever was adduced to transacting with banks and foreign lenders, it is quite evident that the sum total of these changes resulted in drastically weakening the position of government to a degree that was excessive.

After Piatco had managed to breach the protective rampart of public bidding, it boldly went on a rampage of further assaults on the ARCA.

3. The First Supplement

In the First Supplement, executed on August 27, 1999, the following changes were made to the ARCA:

a. Additional Provision on Piatco's Liabilities

• The amounts payable to the government were reduced by allowing additional exceptions to the gross revenues in which it was supposed to participate.

b. Special Obligations Imposed on the Government

• The government was obligated to construct and/or maintain at its expense an access road, a taxi lane and a taxi way for Terminal III, even though these were previously part of Piatco's obligations.

• MIAA was required to provide funding for the maintenance and repair of airports and facilities owned or operated by it and by third persons under its control and would be liable to Piatco in case of breach.

• Imposed on the government were ten "additional special obligations," which included the following: working for the removal of the general aviation traffic from the NAIA complex; providing the land required by Piatco for the taxi lane and the taxi way at no cost to the latter; implementing the government's existing storm drainage master plan; coordinating with DPWH the financing, the implementation and the completion, inter alia, of the following before the in-service date: three left-turning overpasses and a road upgrade and improvement program.

4.   The Second Supplement

The Second Supplement, executed on September 4, 2000, called for Piatco - acting as a public works contractor - to clear, remove, demolish and dispose of improvements, subterranean obstructions and waste materials at the project site. Though denominated as Second Supplement, it was an entirely new public works contract.

5. The Third Supplement

The Third Supplement, executed on June 22, 2001, passed on to government some of Piatco's obligations as occupants, improvements and obstructions from the site where the surface road connecting Terminals Il and III was to be constructed, as well as the repair and maintenance thereof.

It must be emphasized that the three Supplements are void, inexistent and inoperative.First, they are mere amendments to and wholly dependent upon the ARCA, which is itself void and incapable of being ratified or amended. Second, even assuming arguendo that the ARCA is valid, still, in imposing significant new obligations upon government, the three Supplements altered the fundamental terms and stipulations of the former, thus necessitating a public bidding all over again; as there was none, the latter are consequently also void and inoperative.

Constitutional and Legal Provisions Violated

Terminal III: A Monopoly

1.   Operations

The ARCA granted to Piatco, for the entire term of the concession agreement, "the exclusive right to operate a commercial international passenger terminal within the Island of Luzon," with the exception of those already existing (at Subic, Clark and Laoag City). This provision was in violation of Section 11, Article XII of the Constitution, prohibiting the grant of a franchise, a certificate, or any other form of authorization for the operation of a public utility that is "exclusive in character."

What was granted to Piatco was not merely a franchise, but an exclusive right to operate an international passenger terminal within the Island of Luzon. In short, a monopoly. The government is now prohibited from exercising its inherent power to grant any other person another franchise to operate such a public utility or to develop new airports in the event public interest in Luzon requires it. This move is highly detrimental to the government and to the public interest.

That a public utility enterprise may constitute a monopoly - on account of the nature of its business and the absence of competition - does not justify a violation of the constitutional prohibition on the grant of an exclusive franchise or exclusive right to operate it. Of course, granting a franchise may result in a monopoly, but making it exclusive is expressly proscribed by the Constitution.

Actually, the ARCA did not just guarantee exclusivity; it also guaranteed that the government would not improve or expand the facilities at Clark. The latter would, instead, put a cap on Clark's operations until after Terminal III shall have been operated at its peak capacity for three consecutive years. But since the rate of influx of international passengers can vary substantially from year to year, it may be many more years before Terminal III operates at peak capacity for three consecutive years. Clark may thus end up stagnating, and the economic progress of a region limping, for a long time — for Piatco's benefit.

2. Services

Aside from the monopoly of operating an international airport terminal in Luzon, the ARCA created another one for Piatco within the NAIA - in the business of providing international airlines with airport-related services (ground handling, in-flight catering, cargo handling, and aircraft repair and maintenance services). The ARCA makes Terminal III the only international passenger terminal at NAIA, and therefore the only place where airport-related services for international airlines may be rendered.

At the same time, the ARCA required the government not to allow service providers with expired MIAA contracts to renew or extend their contracts. Further the ARCA required the government not to allow service providers with ongoing services and operations at Terminal I to carry over their concession agreements, services, and operations to Terminal III, without first entering into an agreement with Piatco.

3. Pricing

Piatco also gained effective control over the fees, the rentals and other charges that these service providers would can regulate its exercise of discretion or ensure free and f "oversight function" the government might be able and minded to exercise is less than sufficient to protect the public interest.

The Piatco provisions fall far short of the standard set by the BOT Law. The latter specifies that the project proponent is allowed to charge facility users appropriate tolls, fees, rentals and charges not exceeding those protected in its bid or as negotiated and incorporated in the contract.

4.   Impairment of Contract and Deprivation of Property Without Due Process

By the in-service date, Terminal III will be the only terminal where the business of providing airport-related services to international airlines and passengers may be conducted at all. Consequently, pursuant to the ARCA, government through DOTC/MIAA will be compelled to break its existing contracts with service providers after that date, as they will no longer be allowed to operate in Terminal III. And because they cannot operate in any other international airport outside Luzon, and the government is not in a position to require Piatco to accommodate them, they will lose their businesses; and their respective employees, their employment.

Thus, the Piatco contracts not only impair the obligation on the deprivation of property and of liberty without due process of law.

Direct Government Guarantee

According to Section 4-A of the BOT Law as amended, unsolicited proposals such as the Terminal III project may be accepted by the government, provided inter alia that no direct government guarantee, subsidy or equity is required. Direct government guarantee is defined as the assumption by government - or by any of its agencies or any local government unit - of responsibility for repayment of debt directly incurred by a proponent in the implementation of its project, in case of a loan default.

1.    Indefinite Postponement of Payments to Government

The contract provisions on Piatco's liabilities and termination compensation contravene the above provision. The ARCA provides that in case Piatco is unable to meet any payment obligation to the Senior Lenders, the latter have the right to regulate the use of its cash resources to ensure payment of obligations to them. Only when their claims have been satisfied may any excess cash be used for payments of its obligations to the government. Such indefinite postponement precisely constitutes the direct government subsidy prohibited by the BOT Law. That Piatco will pay interest thereon does not validate the prohibited subsidy.

2.  No Financial Comptrollership by Government The foregoing, combined with ARCA's provisions, removes government's right to appoint a financial comptroller to manage Piatco's cash position during situations of financial distress. The Senior Lende rs are effectively given the right to exercise financial comptrollership over Piatco and places in the hands of foreign lenders the power and authority to determine how much - if at all —the Philippine government (as grantor of the franchise) may be allowed to receive from Piatco, and when. The government will then be at the mercy of foreign lenders, contrary to public policy. Such a situation is an affront to the sovereignty of the Republic and to the national honor.

3.  Compensation Even in Case of Piatco's Default

Section 7 of the BOT Law as amended allows payment of compensation in instances in which contract term occurs without fault on the part of the project proponent, but not otherwise.

This provision is clearly violated in the contracts. The ARCA stipulates that the government must pay termination compensation to Piatco, even when termination is initiated by the former for a breach of contractual commitments by the latter.

Moreover, instead of allowing recovery only of actual have sure expenses incurred for the project plus a reasonable rate of  return - or recovery of the proportionate contract cost, as permitted by the BOT Law - the ARCA specifies that termination compensation shall be equivalent to the amount guaranteed by government: either the appraised value of the terminal facility or the aggregate of the Attendant Liabilities, whichever is greater. In fine, a direct government guarantee for the aggregate amount of the money owed by Piatco - any of its debts under the sun. Thus, since that amount may greatly exceed the appraised value of the facility, the government may end up paying very much more than the real worth of Terminal III.

Worse, even if the government pays millions of dollars in termination compensation, the Senior Lenders may still foreclose the mortgage liens on the facility.

4.   No Insurance Coverage for Government Interest

Furthermore, where the BOT Law authorizes termination compensation, a prerequisite is that the interest of government be duly insured. The ARCA stipulates all insurance coverage for the terminal facility, but all insurance policies are to be assigned, and all proceeds payable, to the Senior Lenders. In effect, the government's interest is uninsured; it is Piatco's interest that is given another unauthorized government guarantee.

In the final analysis, the ARCA provisions in question are diametrically at odds with the spirit and intent of BOT Law. This law was meant to mobilize private resources to take on the burden and the risks of financing the construction, the operation and the maintenance of relevant infrastructure and development projects, which the government is not in a position to undertake. To the extent that the project proponent is able to obtain loans to fund the project, it shares those risks with its banks and other lenders. But where the proponent or its lenders manage to cajole or coerce the government into extending a guarantee of payment of the loan obligations, the risks are passed right back to the government.

Disbursement of Public Funds

for "Special Obligations"

Even Without Valid Appropriation

Clearly prohibited by the Constitution is the disbursement of public funds out of the Treasury except in pursuance of an appropriation made by law. Executive Order 292 of the Administrative Code of 1987 provides that no contract involving expenditure of public funds shall but entered into without an appropriation therefor; or without a certification by the proper accounting official of the ag concerned, stating that funds have been duly appropriated for the purpose, and that the amount needed under the contract is available for the current year. These twin requisites have been held to be the conditio sine qua non for the execution and the validity of government contracts.

The three Supplements to the ARCA imposed on the government additional burdens that involved spending public money without prior appropriation. Piatco's justification - that the contractual stipulations were for d;     compliance on a best-efforts basis only - does not hold water. Many of the additional special obligations set forth in the FS are obviously critical to the success of the Terminal III project, so failure to deliver on any of those obligations may result in a material breach of the Piatco contracts. The concessionaire may then terminate those contracts and seek payment of liquidated damages from the government. Hence, the obligations in the Supplements are not to be performed on a best-efforts basis only, but are mandatory in character.

No Time Limit on Franchises

The ARCA has an innovative way to circumvent Section 11 of Article XII in its prohibition on a franchise or certificate for a period longer than fifty years. Full payment by the government of specified liquidated damages to Piatco was made a condition precedent for turnover of the Terminal III facility to the former in the event of contract termination. Until such full payment, the latter shall continue to operate the Terminal, withhold all payments to the government, and apply these to the liquidated damages.

This easy payment scheme will allow the government to avoid outright payment of its obligation, which will likely run into billions of pesos. Nevertheless, such a scheme would cost the government considerable lost income that it would earn if it were to operate Terminal III by itself. And since payments to Piatco will be on "installment basis," interest charges on the remaining unpaid balance would cause the total outstanding balance to swell, such that Piatco would be entitled to keep operating the Terminal for an indefinite length of time.

To repeat, my Separate Opinion reached the same conclusion as the ponencia that the five Piatco contracts were all void. However, on the decisive question of what to do with the terminal, Justice Puno ruled that the government, invoking its police power, may "temporarily" take over the facility, without need of paying any compensation therefore. In my case, I opined that just compensation based on quantum meruit was needed to be paid to Piatco and its investors, funders and contractors. I consider this a significant distinction. Hence, I would like to quote in its entirety my "Epilogue" which explained my position, as follows:

"Epilogue:

"What Do We Do Now?

"In the final analysis, there remains but one ultimate question, which I raised during the Oral Argument on December 10, 2002: What do we do with the Piatco contracts and Terminal III? (Feeding directly into the resolution of the decisive question is the other nagging issue: Why should we bother with determining the legality and validity of these contracts, when the Terminal itself has already been built and is practically complete?)

"Prescinding from all the foregoing disquisitions, I find that all the Piatco contracts, without exception, are void ab initio and therefore inoperative. Even the very process by which the contracts came into being - the bidding and the award - has been riddled with irregularities galore and blatant violations of law and public policy, far too many to ignore. There is thus no conceivable way, as proposed by some, of saving one (the original Concession Agreement) while junking all the rest.

"Neither is it possible to argue for the retention of the Draft Concession Agreement (referred to in the various pleadings as the 'Contract Bidded Out') as the contract that should be kept in force and effect to govern the situation, inasmuch as it was never executed by the parties. What Piatco and the government executed was the Concession Agreement, which is entirely different from the Draft Concession Agreement.

"Ultimately, though, it would be tantamount to an outrageous, grievous and unforgivable mutilation of public policy and an insult to ourselves if we opt to keep in place a contract - any contract - for to do so would assume that we agree to having Piatco continue as the concessionaire for Terminal III.

"Despite all the insidious contraventions of the Constitution, law and public policy Piatco perpetrated, keeping Piatco on as concessionaire and even rewarding it by allowing it to operate and profit from Terminal III instead of imposing upon it the stiffest sanctions permissible under the laws - is unconscionable.

"It is no exaggeration to say that Piatco may not really mind which contract we decide to keep in place. For all it may care, we can do just as well without one, if we only let it continue and operate the facility. After all, the real money if Piatco was deemed come not from building the Terminal, but from actually operating it for fifty or more years and charging whatever it feels like, without any competition at all. This scenario must not be allowed to happen.

"If the Piatco contracts are junked altogether as I think  they should be, should not AEDC automatically be considered the winning bidder and therefore allowed to C operate the facility? My answer is a stone-cold 'No.' AED never won the bidding, never signed any contract, and never built any facility. Why should it be allowed to automatically step in and benefit from the greed of another?

"Should the government pay at all for reasonable expenses incurred in the construction of the Terminal? Indeed it should; otherwise, it will be unjustly enriching itself at the expense of Piatco and, in particular , its funders, and investors - both local and foreign. After all, there is no question that the State needs and will make use of Terminal III, it being part and parcel of the critical infrastructure and transportation-related programs of the government.

"In Melchor v. Commission on Audit, this Court held that even if the contract therein was void, the principle of payment by quantum meruit was found applicable, and the contractor was allowed to recover the reasonable value thing or services rendered (regardless of any agreement as to the supposed value), in order to avoid unjust enrichment on the part of government. The principle of quantum meruit was likewise applied in Eslao v. Commission on Audit, because to deny payment for a building almost completed and already occupied would be to permit the government to unjustly enrich itself at the expense of the contractor. The same principle was applied in Republic v. Court of Appeals.

"One possible practical solution would be for government - in view of the nullity of the Piatco contracts and of the fact that Terminal III has already been built and almost finished - to bid out the operation of the facility under the same or analogous principles as build-operate-and-transfer projects. To be imposed, however, is the condition that the winning bidder must pay the builder of the facility a price fixed by the government based on quantum meruit; on the real, reasonable -  not inflated - value of the built facility.

"How the payment or series of payments to the builder, self at the funders, investors and contractors will be staggered and actors   scheduled will have to be built into the bids, along with the annual guaranteed payments to government. In this manner, this whole sordid mess could result in something truly beneficial for all, especially for the Filipino people."

The Vitug Dissent

Justice Vitug, joined by Justices Quisumbing and Azcuna, submitted a four-page Separate Opinion, which argued for a dismissal of the Petitions solely on a procedural ground: that the Supreme Court was bereft of jurisdiction over the consolidated cases. He contended that the Petitions, "although denominated to be petitions for prohibitions, actually pray for the nullification of the Piatco contracts and to restrain respondents from implementing said agreements for being illegal and unconstitutional." Thus, he concluded that the Petitions were really disguised complaints for declaratory relief, over which the Supreme Court had no original jurisdiction. The law gives this authority initially to regional trial courts, and to the Supreme Court only on appeal.

I now quote in full Justice Vitug’s Dissent:

"Section 2, Rule 65 of the Rules of Court states: 'When the proceedings of any tribunal, corporation, board, officer or person, whether exercising judicial, quasi-judicial or ministerial functions, are without or in excess of its or his jurisdiction, or with grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction, and there is no appeal or any other plain, speedy and adequate remedy in the ordinary course of law, a person aggrieved thereby may file a verified petition in the proper court, alleging the facts with certainty and praying that judgment be rendered commanding the respondent to desist from further proceedings in the action or matter specified therein, or otherwise granting such incidental reliefs as law and justice may require.'

"The rule is explicit. A petition for prohibition d, may be filed against a tribunal, corporation, board, officer or person, exercising judicial, quasi-judicial or ministerial functions. What the petitions seek from respondents do not involve judicial, quasi-judicial or ministerial functions. In prohibition, only legal issues affecting the jurisdiction of the tribunal, board or officer involved may be resolved on the basis of undisputed facts. The parties allege, respectively, contentious evidentiary facts. It would be difficult, if not anomalous, to decide the jurisdictional issue on the basis of the contradictory factual submissions made by the parties. As the Court has so often exhorted, it is not a trier of fact

"The Petitions, in effect, are in the nature of actions for declaratory relief under Rule 63 of the Rules of Court. The Rules provide that any person interested under a contract may, before breach or violation thereof, bring an action in the appropriate Regional Trial Court to determine any question of construction or validity arising, and for a declaration of his rights or duties thereunder. The Supreme Court assumes no jurisdiction over petitions for declaratory relief which are cognizable by regional trial courts.

"As I have so expressed in Tolentino vs. Secretary of Finance, reiterated in Santiago vs. Guingona, Jr., the Supreme Court should not be thought of as having been tasked with the awesome responsibility of overseeing the entire bureaucracy. Pervasive and limitless, such as it may seem to be under the 1987 Constitution, judicial power still succumbs to the paramount doctrine of separation of powers. The Court may not at good liberty intrude, in the guise of sovereign imprimatur, into every affair of government. What significance can still then remain of the time-honored and widely acclaimed principle of separation of powers if, at every turn, the Court allows itself to pass upon at will the disposition of a co-equal, independent and coordinate branch in our system of government. I dread to think of the so varied uncertainties that such an undue interference can lead to.

"Accordingly, I vote for the dismissal of the petition."
