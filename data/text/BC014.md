## Faith in God and in Ourselves

I would like to thank  your President, Congressman Alberto J. Lopez, my long-time friend and generous former client, for inviting me as your commencement speaker and for conferring upon me the degree of Doctor of Laws (honoris causa). My wife Leni, a professor at the Asian Institute of Management - who is with us now - and my children can at last be proud of me! You see, my wife and my five children - four daughters and one son - each has at least one postgraduate degree from a leading university abroad. Until today, I was the only one in my family who had no master’s or doctor’s degree. But, finally and at last, thanks to Congressman Lopez and the University of Iloilo, I now have a postgraduate degree - a doctor of laws at that!

Since my wife and I were not moneyed, we thought that the best legacy we could leave our children was a good solid education. Education gives fulfillment not only in term provide for our daily needs, but ins of being able to terms of being equal to anyone else in the world, and being useful not only to our fam in terms of as well. That is why I congratulate all of you graduates who will now pass from being students to being active participants in your chosen careers and in the mainstream of our nation. 

I especially greet the honor graduates for their extraordinary achievements. Special maligayang bati to cum laude graduates Noli Barcelona, Maricel Gange, Jonalyn Geroy and Feliza Tanalbon; and honor graduates Maryann Gimoto and Rosalie Malacapo. I am sure their parents stand proud and feel fully rewarded in their long struggle to help their children. Like them, I am impressed by excellence in school work. 

After I received Congressman Lopez's kind invitation, I began thinking about what topic I could discuss with you today. I thought that perhaps many of you graduates were like me when I finished my law course thirty-seven years ago - poor, struggling but full of energy and ready to battle life’s challenges. So, instead of delivering a pompous lecture, I decided just to tell you my story, my experiences; to share with you my life, its ups and downs, its victories and frustrations, its ebb and flow; and to extract therefrom a few lessons you may find useful as you begin your own careers.

Like many of you, my parents were poor. My father was a lowly government employee and my mother a dutiful housewife. Due to poverty, I studied in public schools during my elementary and secondary years. I had to peddle cigarettes to jeepney drivers, hawk newspapers in the streets of Sampaloc, Manila, and shine shoes to support my studies. After graduating with honors from Mapa High School, I was given a scholarship in U.P., but I could not enrol there because my father did not have enough money to pay for the fifteen-centavo bus fare then from C atalura Cataluña Street in Sampaloc, Manila, where we lived to the U.P. campus in Diliman, Quezon City. I envied my classmates who all enrolled at U.P. In my case, I had to make do with FEU which was walking distance from our house. 

While on scholarship at FEU, I sold bibles to my professors and the university officials for my pocket money. I also sold textbooks to my classmates in the Institute of Law to be able to get a book as m y commission. I engaged in extracurricular activities and, during my sophomore year, became the youngest president of the Central Student Council. I co-founded the National Union of Students of the Philippines where I met and fell in love with a cloistered colegiala, Leni Carpio, who is now my wife. She was then editor of their college newspaper in St. Scholastica's College in Manila. 

At the FEU Institute of Law, I was fortunate to meet and be taught by a brilliant lawyer who had just been appointed dean — Jovito R. Salonga who later on became, as you know, a major leader of our country. Although I was active in extracurricular activities, I did not forget my academic studies, because Dean Salonga took a liking for me and monitored my grades all the time. During the bar examinations, Dean Salonga told me that he expected me to be one of the topnotchers. I was pressured so much by his expectations that I got sick. On the first day of the exams, I was confined at the FEU Hospital and had to get special permission from my doctor to walk over flooded streets to the nearby University of the East where the exams were being held. 

After taking the bar examinations and, by the grace of God, landing in the top ten, I was invited by Dean Salonga to join his prestigious law firm. His law partners at the time included Pedro L. Yap who later became Chief Justice, Sedfrey Ordonez who later on became Secretary of Justice and Neptali Gonzales who is now senator and former senate president. 

At the recommendation of Dean Salonga who was an alumnus of both Yale and Harvard, I was admitted to the Yale Law School, but again I did not have enough money for my transportation fare. I could not enjoy the privilege of education at an ivy league school because of my lack of means. I was embittered. I envied the rich and the powerful, those who seemed to have no problem buying what they wanted and wanting nothing that could not be bought. 

But I continued doing my best and striving for excellence in practising and teaching law. I also engaged in business and vowed to myself that travel and education would never be a problem for my children. Many years after my graduation, I witnessed the ultra-rapid amassing of incredible wealth in Hongkong. As a lawyer, I participated in transactions involving billions of Hongkong dollars. For many years, I enjoyed first-class travels, private yachts, five-star hotels, Rolls Royce limousines, Piaget watches and mansions of the rich and famous. But during the stock market crash of 1982 in Hongkong, I lost all that I had earned abroad. I was heart-broken. What I lost was enough to cause me a heart attack. In fact, many were reported to have committed suicide because of losses due to that devastating stock market crash of 1982. 

Forlorn and poor again, I decided to concentrate on my law practice in the Philippines. In 1983, I joined the Rotary Club of Manila where I met then chief of the Philippine Constabulary, Gen. Fidel V. Ramos who — as fate would have it - became our President in 1992 and appointed me to the Supreme Court in 1995. This is not to say that my appointment to the Court was easy. In fact, it was extremely difficult. You see, before the President can appoint members of the judiciary, including those of the Supreme Court, the candidates need to pass meticulous screenings by the Judicial and Bar Council. From 1992 to 1995, there were at least seven vacancies in the Supreme Court by reason of the compulsory retirement of its members who reached the age of 70 years. Given priority to these vacancies were career justices of the Court of Appeals. Only once in a while are outsiders nominated and appointed to the Court. For three years in all these seven vacancies, I was bypassed by the Judicial and Bar Council, because either I was thought to be unqualified; or even if I was qualified, there were others who were thought to be even more qualified.

What lessons can we learn from this short narration of my life experiences? There are at least three: 

**Lesson Number One.** Sometimes, adversities and misfortunes in life really preludes to greater victories and successes. I had thought that my poverty during my childhood was a curse because I could not enroll in a school of my choice, namely, UP. But because of that, I grudgingly studied at FEU, not knowing that I would encounter a very brilliant mentor - Dean Salonga. I was embittered because I could not enjoy my scholarship at Yale University; I could not raise enough money to pay for my air fare. But because of that, I organized Baron Travel Corporation which enabled me to provide transporation for all our five children who went on to finish graduate degrees at some of the best schools in the world - our eldest, Maria Elena, finished her Master in Business Administration (MBA) at the University of California; another girl, Maria Jocelyn, obtained also an MBA at the University of Chicago and the University of Navarre in Barcelona; a third girl, Maria Theresa, finished her MBA at the University of Michigan and Erasmus University in Holland; our youngest girl, Maria Evelyn, her Master in Economics at Boston University, and her Master in Public Policy at Harvard; while our only son finished his Master in Music in Munich, Germany, and his Master in Operations Research "with distinction" in Stanford. Right now, he is taking his Ph.D. in Engineering Economic System - whatever that is - also at Stanford. 

The point is, while I did wallow in self-pity when I could not study at Yale, I feel more triumphant now that my children have accomplished five times over what I had failed to do. Also, I shed tears of self-pity as I slept on the sidewalks to await the morning newspapers which I sold to our neighbors in Sampaloc, Manila. I wrote in my diary: "Today, I am a newsboy. But someday, I'll be president of a major newspaper." I thought that was just a pipe dream of an ambitious poor boy, but in March 1991, by the grace of God, I became president of the Philippine Daily Inquirer. Finally, I was devastated when I lost my worldly earnings and investments during the Hongkong stock market crash of 1982. But God had other plans for me. I settled back home and was later on rewarded with a membership in the Supreme Court - the fulfillment of every lawyer's dream, an achievement that money cannot buy nor can any stock market crash take away. 

**Lesson Number Two.** Whatever happens, do not lose heart. Keep on working, keep on doing your best. There is no substitute for devotion to excellence. Believe in yourself and your infinite capacity to ride over tragedy and defeat. Some of us lose heart with a few disappointments and failures: a failed subject in school, a failed love affair, a lost job, a missed opportunity. Author Anthony Robbins reminds us of the story of a famous American hero who:

At age 31, failed in business

At 32, was defeated in a legislative election

At 34, failed again in business 

At 35, was overcome by the death of his sweetheart

At 36, had a nervous breakdown

At 43, 46 and 48 lost in three successive congressional elections

At 55, was defeated in a senatorial race

At 56, failed in his attempt to become Vice President

At 58, ran for the Senate and lost again

But at the age of 60, was elected President of the United States. 

The man's name is Abraham Lincoln, one of the greatest American presidents. 

Remember, no matter what happens, continue working hard, continue doing good; persevere in your faith, in your power to conquer any past mistake. Believe in yourselves. Believe in your ultimate victory. 

**Lesson Number Three.** Have faith in God. Many times we pray and ask God for what we want: for a good career, for a passing grade, for a reciprocated love, for a healing of some illness, for a car and even for a house. Sometimes when we do not get what we want, we feel embittered and doubt God's power and providence. But our God is an infinitely intelligent and powerful God. He knows our needs better than we do. He will always provide all things good for us —in his own time and way. As God good for has himself said in the Bible, in the book of Isaiah (55:8-9): 

My thoughts are not your thoughts, 

And my ways are not your ways; 

As high as the heavens are above the earth,

So high are my ways above your ways,

And my thoughts above your thoughts. 

So we trust God. As long as we have faith in Him, He will answer all our prayers in His own way, not our way, and in His own time, not our time. Let me assure you that in all my life, all my struggles, my frustrations, my victories, God has never failed in His promise, as heralded also in Scriptures, book of Romans (8:28): 

God makes all things work for the good of those who love Him and who are called according to His purpose.

Let me repeat that "God makes all things work for the good of those who love Him and who are called according to His purpose." 

These three lessons for today can be summed up in one sentence: Work as if everything depended on you, and pray as if everything depended on God. 

To help you remember today's three lessons, let me in closing relate a little story. 

During the Middle Ages, there lived a farmer, his wife, and his only son who was twenty years old. One day, the farmer's horse ran away to the nearby mountains. So, his neighbors went to him and told him, "Bad luck for you, your horse is gone. Now your family will starve because you cannot till the fields." The farmer replied, "Good luck, bad luck, who knows?" Three days later, the horse returned with nine wild horses from the mountain. The villagers again went to the farmer and said, "Good luck for you. Now you have ten horses." The farmer replied, "Good luck, bad luck, who knows? A few days later, the farmer's son mounted one of the wild horses. But the horse threw him out and he broke his left leg. Again, the villagers told the farmer, "Bad luck for you. With a broken leg, your only son is useless." The farmer replied, "Good luck, bad luck, who knows?" A week later, the kingdom went to war and the king conscripted all the able-bodied young men in the village except, of course , the disabled son of the farmer. The kingdom lost the war and all the young men from the village died. And so the villagers again went to the farmer and chorused, "Good luck for you. You are the only villager with a son to inherit your farm." And the farmer replied, "Good luck, bad luck, who knows?"

Graduates, parents, ladies and gentlemen, the farmer in our little story is asking, "Good luck, bad luck, who knows?" Who indeed really knows? Only God knows! And if I must leave you anything to remember today, it is this: Regardless of anything that happens, whether it is victory or defeat, fulfillment or frustration, sadness or joy, let us always have faith in God and in ourselves. Every day we shall move on, exclaiming: "We will work as if everything depended on us. And we will pray as if everything depended on God." I thank you.
