__Catholic Lay Leader __

The ardor with which Art responded to God’s love found expression in civic and community engagements\. Outside of his responsibilities as personal adviser of Senator Salonga and chief legal counsel of the Liberal Party, he took on the duties of Special Minister of Holy Communion in his parish of San Antonio in Makati\. 

He hungered to know more about God and read the Bible voraciously\. Art pored over Scriptures from cover to cover several times, from the Old Testament to the New Testament\. He had a burning desire to share about God and understood that it was necessary to learn more about Jesus if he was to speak to others about the Lord\. He went through volumes of commentaries and researched the teachings of the Holy Father, whose encyclicals became part of his vocabulary\. “In my practice of law, in my home and in my lifestyle, the Scriptures became my guide and my light\.”

 	Art was sharing the story of his life’s transformation with different audiences and was making an impact\. One of those who heard about him was Henrietta de Villa, who was then president of the Council of the Laity of the Philippines\. It was an association created by the Catholic Bishops Conference of the Philippines for a consultative and collaborative engagement with the lay population of the Church\. 

Tita, as she was widely known, was preparing for the celebration of the Laity Week during the feast day of San Lorenzo Ruiz, the first Filipino martyr\. She was organizing an assembly of laypersons and priests, both secular and religious to raise an awareness that the Church was composed, not just of bishops, cardinals, clerics and members of religious communities, but included every church\-going member of the Catholic Church\. Tita had seen Art several times in various activities and events and knew he was the president of the Rotary Club of Manila\. “I invited him to give a testimonial to the assembly on how he was practicing his faith,” she said\. He accepted without hesitation\. 

Came the day of his talk\. Tita was astounded to hear him unabashedly declaring himself for God\. “Here was a man of the world who openly proclaimed Jesus,” she said\. “I was listening to him declare boldly that we were all sinners and that our salvation was only through the mercy of God, that God used even sinners to carry out his saving work\.” He talked about how the programs of the Rotary Club were premised on God and about his work among the underserved of society\. Tita listened and was convinced that he should be included in the Council of the Laity\. 

It was also around this time that the CBCP President, Archbishop Leonardo Zamora Legazpi, wanted to call for a Second Plenary Council of the Philippines\. He gave her the task to set into motion the preparations for the highest formal gathering of all local churches in the country\. What made his directive extraordinary was the inclusion of the lay faithful, non\-ordained Catholics like Tita and Art\. “Archbishop Legazpi was a great and formidable man in mind and heart who had great insight,” she said of Archbishop Legazpi\. “Usually, in a Plenary Council, only the ordained participated\. This time, he wanted to include laypersons\. The Vatican had done the same\. For the very first time, the Synod of Bishops invited lay people to be auditors in the Council, and so I invited Atty\. Artemio Panganiban\. 

“During the Council, he was very active,” Tita continued\. “Art is not a lamp you hide under the bushel\. He was very participative\. He always made sense, always had substance\.” She described the Second Plenary Council as a historic breaking away from tradition\. “It was a time of grace,” she said\. “The bishops and cardinals were listening to us\. When the decrees were put to a vote, we voted first\. Ours were “consultative votes” while those of the Church hierarchy were “deliberate votes,” yet the outcomes of both were always in agreement\. 

__Among Bishops and Cardinals __

The Second Plenary Council was Art’s first major encounter with the ecclesiastical hierarchy\. “For over a month, I had the opportunity to converse at length with Catholic Church leaders and learn from many of them,” Art recalls\. 

Tita believed Art had the skills and competence to make valuable contributions to the work of the Church and enlisted him again when the Parish Pastoral Council for Responsible Voting \(PPCRV\) was organized in 1991\. The PPCRV was created after the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines \(PCP II\) had called for reforms in the electoral exercise in anticipation of the upcoming elections in 1992\. Together with the Archbishop of Manila Jaime Cardinal Sin, Commission on Elections Commissioner Haydee Yorac, Bishop Gabriel Reyes and Bayani Valenzuela, Tita and 30 other lay leaders conceived the idea of a non\-partisan, non\-sectarian, non\-profit volunteer organization

established for the sole purpose of ensuring fraud\-free, fair and free elections in the Philippines\. 

The PPCRV would be affiliated to the Catholic Church as an advocate for electoral reforms engaged in manual auditing during automated elections, poll\-watching in coordination with parishes, reporting violations, and securing legal help as it pertained to the elections\. It was launched in October 1991 with the attendance of about a thousand members of the laity representing parishes in the Archdiocese of Manila\. The month after, participation expanded nationwide to more than 300,000 people, following the express support of the Catholic Bishops Conference of the Philippines\. Tita was one of the founders together with Jaime Cardinal Sin, Commissioner Yorac, Bishop Reyes and Bayani Valenzuela\. 

Art accepted Tita’s invitation to serve with her and became the national vice\-chairperson and chief legal counsel of the PPCRV\. When hackles were raised over the increasing role of the church in the affairs of the state, Art provided sound perspective based on objective facts\. He spoke in different fora addressing the question of why the Catholic Church was in politics\. He had effectively become a champion of the Church, applying his legal knowledge and understanding to respond to accusations that the church was meddling in government matters\. A summary of his points was turned into an article that saw print in the leading dailies, putting into perspective the issues raised\. 

“Prior to 1965, Church involvement in politics was largely unheard of,” he wrote\. “Since then, however, the Philippine Church has increasingly become visible in the political sphere\. During the peaceful revolution of 1986 which saw the dismantling of the Marcos government and during the snap elections just before that, the Philippine Church, represented by the Catholic Bishops’ Conference of the Philippines\. assumed a proactive role in a ‘Joint Pastoral Exhortation on the Snap Elections’ dated January 25, 1986\.

“The CBCP, through its then president Ricardo Cardinal Vidal, exhorted the faithful to ‘vote into office the persons you believe God wants you to put into office as president and vice president of this land’ and ‘to exercise unwavering vigilance in organized collaboration with others so that your votes are cast, counted, tallied and reported properly and honestly\.’ More strongly, in a post\-election statement, the CBCP condemned the snap elections as ‘unparalleled’ in its fraud as attested to by the ‘systematic disenfranchisement of voters \. \. \. widespread, massive vote\-buying \. \. \. deliberate tampering with election returns \. \. \. and intimidation, harassment, terrorism and murder\. 

For the forthcoming 1992 elections, the Church had become even more involved in nonpartisan political activities\. Whereas in 1986, the CBCP merely endorsed the efforts of Namfrel, which was a civic organization, in this election the Church directly sponsored the PPCRV\. To be sure, the PPCRV was created out of a confluence of three major events: \(1\) the Second Vatican Council \(1962\-1965\), \(2\) the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines \(Jan\.20\-Feb\.17, 1991\) and \(3\) three pastoral letters issued recently by the CBCP\.” 

It was unlikely for Art to have presumed that his legal career might be placed in the context of a greater plan beyond the practice of his profession\. By his willingness to serve in the PPCRV, he found himself in the company of Church leaders who were forthright in addressing electoral concerns\. While not in the forefront of the contentions, he was providing a solid basis for the position taken by the Philippine bishops\. His association with Tita de Villa and the prelates proved to be auspicious in the events that would unfold in the forthcoming months\. 

__Adviser to the Pope __

Tita de Villa had earned the respect and confidence of the

Bishops and was consequently appointed by Pope John Paul II to

the Pontifical Council of the Laity in the Vatican\. It was an unqualified honor to be part of the dicastery that “assists the Holy Father in the exercise of his supreme office for the good and the service of the universal Church and the particular Churches, as regards the promotion and coordination of the lay apostolate and, in general, the Christian life of lay people\.” \(Vatican\.va\)  

Tita completed the five\-year term and was re\-appointed for another five years in 1995\. However, before she could complete her second term, she was appointed as Philippine ambassador to the Holy See in 1996\. Having worked well with Art, she thought of him as her replacement\. She asked Eduardo Francisco Cardinal Pironio, who was in charge of the Pontifical Council of the Laity, if she could nominate someone else to assume her place for the rest of her term\. He said she would have to get the approval of either the CBCP or the Archbishop of Manila, who at that time was Jaime Cardinal Sin\. Between having to talk with all the bishops or to call Cardinal Sin, Tita opted for the latter, who immediately approved\. “He knew Art by then, from his participation in the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines in 1991\.” This was Art’s entry ticket into the Pontifical Council of the Laity in the Vatican\. Tita lost no time in recruiting Art to also be part of the preparations for Pope John Paul II’s visit to the Philippines in

1995\. “I invited Art to be in my committee as the executive secretary of the Papal visit\. He accepted at once\.” She continued, “Art became a member of the Pontifical Council of the Laity in the Vatican, adding the richness of his faith experience\. His legal know__\-__how was also helpful to the dicastery in Rome\.” 

Tita lauded Art’s abilities as a coordinator in managing the Papal visit\. The World Youth Day was being held in Manila\. “It was a pastoral visit,” she said\. “Even if the government invites the Pope, like in a state visit, what takes precedence is the pastoral visit; and in a pastoral visit, it is the CBCP that takes the lead\. However, Jaime Cardinal Sin was the leader designated to manage the operations\. 

“Many people were involved\. Delegations from all over the world were coming for the World Youth Day\. I had been making so many configurations for the organizational chart but felt it was not good enough so I called Art to help me\.” He astutely considered the requirements, the human resources, and the organizations before creating a structure that had a Central Committee led by the CBCP head and an Operations Committee under Jaime Cardinal Sin\. His model was the solution Tita needed for an organized and efficient system to

successfully manage an event of that magnitude\. 

She also described his contribution to the Pontifical Council as significant\. It was not just his legal know\-how but his ability to translate legalese into a language that laypersons could understand\. “Art explains the law in a way that is informative, educative and readable,” she says\. “He draws you into the conversation to enable you to participate and to make an informed judgment\.” 

Her observation was validated when Art was appointed to the Pontifical Council for the Laity after he had completed Tita’s term\. From 1996 to 2002, he was the lone Filipino member assisting the Roman Catholic Curia in concerns pertaining to the lay people’s contribution to the life and mission of the Church\. 

__A Prophecy __

When Art became a renewed Catholic, he embraced his role as a lay member of the faithful with an in\-depth understanding born of his readings and the teachings of his mentors\. When he was asked to address the National Convention of Dominican and Siena Schools in the Philippines, he talked about lay empowerment and defined the role of believers baptized into the Church with functions different from those of the ordained\.

He said, “The lay faithful are members of the Church as much as they are of the hierarchy\. For many decades, lay involvement in the Church was regarded only as a participation in the apostolate of the hierarchy\. But the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines or PCP II is clear: the lay faithful are not second\-class members\. They are not mere deputies or assistants or helpers of priests\. They are co\-workers and collaborators in the service of the Church\. In fact, they are equal to the clergy in dignity and mission\.

“This equality does not do away with the ecclesiastical hierarchy\. But this hierarchy is a hierarchy of service and not of excellence\. As the PCP II document says, ‘A person is not a better Christian simply because he is a bishop, presbyter or deacon\. The Christian dignity of the ordained is measured by the sincerity and genuineness of their service rather than by the office they hold\.’ \(PCP II, 96\) 

“Because of this equality, lay people no longer need any specific mandate from the hierarchy in order to fulfill their mission as Christians – which is, to be evangelized and thereafter to evangelize others\. They are no longer merely the objects but are themselves the subjects or participants of evangelization\. \. \. \.” 

“The second fundamental aspect of the lay vocation is its secular character\. Lumen Gentium \(31\) and Christifideles Laici \(15\) require the lay faithful ‘to seek the Kingdom of God by engaging in temporal affairs and ordering them according to the plan of God \. \. \. \(hence\) the world becomes the place and the means for the lay faithful to fulfill their Christian vocation\.’ There are many areas, particularly in the temporal sphere, where an empowered laity could do wonders and where, by their peculiar circumstances, the laity are restricted\. These areas include politics, government, business, industry and finance\. It is in these fields where Christ can more effectively be brought by the witness and fervor of lay people\. Because this is their world, this is their milieu\.” 

Art could not have known how prophetic those words would be for him when he spoke them to his audience\. His journey–from a boy with negligible knowledge of catechism, much less of Jesus Christ, to a student leader who came under the wing of a priest and later a Protestant pastor, went on to become a lawyer and successful businessman who lost his wealth but found God on a mountain retreat and became immersed in the service of the Lord–all this was about to take a dramatic turn\. He would find himself on a road leading to one of the most painful and purifying episodes of his life\. It would transpire through one person with whom he had crossed paths in a politically charged environment that he had steered away from, but which he nevertheless had to navigate in response to the God he had chosen to follow\.
