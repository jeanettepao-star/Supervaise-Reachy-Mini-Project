# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Ch. 8: Republic v. Meralco — Oral Argument
Source:     The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary (book)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

Chapter 8.

Republic v. Meralco: When May Oral Argument Be Called?

Section 1 of Rule 49 of the Rules of Court provides that "[a]t its own instance or upon motion of a party, the court may hear the parties in oral argument on the merits of a cas or on any material incident in connection therewith. xxx."

In trial courts, face-to-face confrontation amongst the parties and verbal skirmishes amongst counsels are very common. In fact, there is a general public impression that litigations are won or lost on the basis of the oral eloquence of lawyers.

While verbal tussles are common in trial courts, they are rare in appellate tribunals. Instead of listening to oral argumentation, appellate jurists prefer to read, reflect on, study and decide cases on the basis of the case records; and the parties' pleadings, briefs, memoranda and written arguments.

The Supreme Court rarely calls for oral argument. Most of the time - in my view, more than 95 percent of the time - cases are resolved on the basis of written pleadings only.

There are no specific rules as to when the High Court may call for Oral Argument.Being completely discretionary on the part of the Supreme Court, it is usually scheduled on the Court's own volition, sometimes, upon motion of the parties.

My own observation and experience tell me that oral argument is resorted to in cases that are of "transcendental importance" to the nation, or that involve novel or complicated issues. Recent examples (decisions promulgated from October 11, 2002 to October 10, 2003) include Macalintal v. Comelec (constitutionality of the Absentee Voting Law), Agan v. Piatco (validity of the contracts for the construction and the operation of the new Manila International Airport at   Terminal), PLDT v. City of Davao (validity of certain local taxes on PLDT), and Government of the United States v. Purganan (availability of bail in the extradition of Rep. Mark Jimenez).

As in all questions, the propriety of calling for oral argumentation is decided by a majority vote of the Court, whether in the Banc or in a Division. In the examples I listed above, the Court unanimously agreed to call for Oral Argument. But in Republico. Manila Electric Company, despite my request, the Court refused to hear the parties orally.

Let me summarize the background of this case.

The Facts

On December 23, 1993, the Manila Electric Company (Meralco) applied with the Energy Regulatory Board (ERB) for a rate increase of an average of P 0.21 per kilowatt-hour (kWh) in its electric distribution charge.

On January 28, 1994, ERB granted a provisional increase of P 0.184 per kWh subject to this condition: "If, after hearing and submission by the Commission on Audit [COA] x x x [Meralco] is entitled to a lesser increase in rates, all exc amounts collected from the applicant's customers x x x shall be refunded to them or correspondingly credited in their favor for application to electric bills covering future consumptions."

On February 11, 1997, COA recommended to ERB that income taxes paid by Meralco should not be included as part of its operating expenses for purposes of rate determination It further suggested the use of the "net average investment method for the computation of the proportionate value of the properties used by Meralco during the test year for the determination of the rate base."

In its Decision, ERB adopted these COA recommendations. As a result, it authorized a rate adjustment of only P .017 per kWh effective February 1994. Furthermore, ERB ordered Meralco to refund its customers or correspondingly credit them for the amount of P .167 per kWh, which was the difference between the provisionally authorized rate of P. 184 and the finally approved one of only P .017.

ERB held that income taxes should not be treated as operating expense, but should instead be "borne by the stockholders who are recipients of the income or profits realized from the operation of their business." In adopting the net average investment method, ERB said that in computing the rate base, "only the proportionate value of the property should be included, determined in accordance with the number of months the same was actually used in service during the test year."

On appeal, the Court of Appeals (CA) reversed the ERB insofar as the latter directed the reduction of the Meralco rates by an average of P.167 per kWh.

However, in a ponencia written by Justice Reynato S. Puno, the Supreme Court (Third Division) struck down the CA reversal and upheld the original ERB Decision on the following grounds:

1. The findings and conclusions of the ERB, the specialized agency charged by law with electric rate determination, should be respected, because there is no showing that these were arbitrary or capricious. "The principle of separation of powers dictates that courts should hesitate to review the acts of administrative [offices], except in clear cases of grave abuse of discretion."

2.  "Income tax is imposed on an individual or entity as a form of excise tax or a tax on the privilege of earning an income." By their nature, income tax payments are not expenses which contribute to or are incurred in connection with the production of profit of a public utility. Meralco has no "license to indiscriminately charge any and all types of expenses” to its customers.

3.   American case law allowing income tax payments to be deducted as operating expenses is not applicable here. "Suffice to state that with regard to rate-determination, the government is not hidebound to apply any particular method or formula. x x x. The Court cannot give in to the importuning of Meralco that we blindly apply the rulings of American courts on the treatment of income tax as operating expenses in rate regulation cases."

4. The net average investment method is reasonable, because it takes into account only properties used in electric distribution "in accordance with the actual number of months" the property has been used. if, as proposed by Meralco, the "trending method" is adopted, a public utility "may easily manipulate the valuation of its property entitled to a return (rate base) by simply including a highly capitalized asset in the computation of the rate base even if the same was used for a limited period of time during the test year."

To this Decision, Meralco filed a Motion for Reconsideration which included new arguments. It contended that "(1) the deduction of income tax from revenues allowed for rate determination of public utilities is part of its constitutional right to property; (2) ti corr used the 'average investment method or the simple average' in computing the value of its properties entitled to a return xxx; and (3) the Decision of the ERB x x x should not be given retroactive effect."

The Office of the Solicitor General (OSG), which appeared as ERB's counsel, opposed the Motion. But it nonetheless informed the Court that the Energy Regulation Commission (ERC), which took ERB's place, had reversed its position and now agreed with Meralco on the following points:

Income taxes should be included as part of operating expenses.

By insisting on the non-inclusion of income taxes in operating expenses, the Court would effectively be reducing the allowable income of Meralco to only 8 percent per annum, which was way below the 12 percent allowed by law.

Because of this volte face of ERC, I asked the Court during the deliberations on the Motion for Reconsideration to refer the Motion to the full Court and to call for Oral Argument, preferably before the Banc, to clarify these new developments.

The four other members of the Third Division, however, refused, and proceeded to deny the Motion for Reconsideration on the basis merely of the OSG's Comment. Hence, I had to content myself with writing a Separate Opinion, arguing that I could not cast my vote on the Motion for Reconsideration because of "lingering questions" I had. Here are excerpts from my Separate Opinion:

Effect of ERC’s

Self-Reversal

"First, this case reached this Court because the Energy Regulatory Board (ERB), now known as the Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC), appealed to us the Decision of the Court of Appeals (CA), which upheld Meralco. In its Comment [on] Meralco's Motion for Reconsideration, however, the OSG - as counsel for ERC - informed this Court that ERC has reversed its position and now believes that 'income taxes' x x x are reasonable costs that may be utilities from recovering its income tax payments will effectively lower the return on rate base enjoyed by a public utility to 8%.

"1. By reversing itself, is the ERC effectively abandoning its appeal before this Court? If so, is it still proper for this Court to uphold the old ERB Decision? Be it remembered that our own Decision is anchored on the theory that ERB should be affirmed, because it is the knowledgeable and specialized government agency tasked with electric rate determination, and thus its findings and opinions - unless ring    obviously faulty - merit full faith and credit. fer

"2.   Is the OSG, as counsel for the ERC and the government, authorized to argue against its own clients' position and thereby leave them without any lawyer?"

Effects of the New

EPIRA Law

"Second, in its Comment, OSG informs us that a new law, RA 9136 - the Electric Power Industry Reform Act (EPIRA) - was enacted on June 16, 2002. This law allegedly authorizes ERC to determine rates that will 'allow the recovery of a just and reasonable return of rate base (RORB) to enable the entity to operate viably. On this basis, ERC opines that actual income taxes paid should now be deemed 'reasonable costs' of operating a public utility.

"1. Does this mean that effective June 16, 2002, ERC may allow the deduction of income taxes from operating expenses? Does this render our Decision obsolete?

The Reduction of Earnings

to Only Eight Percent

"Third, citing the report of the Commission on Audit (COA), the OSG originally opined that Meralco - after the infusion of the provisional rate increase of 18.4 centavos would still earn 13% RORB if income taxes [were] not treated as operating expenses, and 20% if they [were deducted as operating expenses.

"1. If this is so, why is Meralco still complaining that the old ERB Decision, which this Court is affirming, bars it from earning the maximum allowable profit of 12%? How accurate are the OSG and COA computations? Or is Meralco just misleading the Court?

"2. In any event, despite the COA figures, the OSG concedes that - at least theoretically - Meralco's profit would be reduced by our Decision to a maximum of only 8% RORB, instead of the allowable 12%. At the same time, it justifies the 8% RORB by arguing that the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank consider a public utility earning of 8% RORB still viable (p. 42 of the OSG Comment). Which is which?"

Special Privilege

to Meralco

"Fourth, in its Comment, the OSG argues that other public utilities are not allowed to deduct income taxes as operating expenses. Why then should Meralco be given this special privilege, it rhetorically asks]

"1. Is this true? If so, why has the ERC changed its position? Why is it now allowing Meralco to deduct income tax payments as 'reasonable costs' of operation?"

The Propriety

of an Oral Argument

"The foregoing [were] the more important questions I posed when I asked the Third Division to refer this case to the Court en banc and to conduct Oral Argument on the Motion for Reconsideration of Meralco. These questions were not fully taken up by the pleadings of the parties. Thus, it would be pretentious for me to render an opinion on them. On the other hand, I believe that a decision that does not take up these questions would be incomplete.

"Hearing the parties [in] Oral Argument before the entire Court, or even x x x the Third Division, prior to resolving with finality the motion for reconsideration on a very important matter such as the present case is not unusual.

Be it noted that Republic v. Meralco was a case decided by the Third Division. Is it still possible and proper for the Banc to call for Oral Argument after a decision has already been rendered, and thereafter to resolve the Motion for Reconsideration? The answer is clearly " yes."

Very recently, in PLDT v. City of Davao, the Court en banc conducted Oral Argument on the Motion for Reconsideration challenging the unanimous Decision of the Second Division. That case involved the issue of whether the City of Davao may impose local taxes on the Philippine Long Distance Telephone Company. The amount involved therein was only about P4 million. On the other hand, the present case involves the refund of about P2.5 billion per year starting 1994 or about P30 billion up to the year 2003.

Apart from the monetary consideration, I believe the issues raised - including the foregoing questions - are also important enough to merit a hearing. May I stress that this  case will affect, not only Meralco and its customers but, all the electric utilities and all their customers all over the Philippines, which means this case will affect everyone in this country.

It is interesting to note that the unanimous Second Division Decision in the above-cited PLDT case was upheld by the Banc, with some dissents led by the ponente herein, Mr. Justice Reynato S. Puno himself, but only after a full hearing by the full Court.

There have been a few other high-profile Division judgments that were brought to the Banc, granted Oral Argument and resolved by the full Court, as follows:

1. Marcos v. Sandiganbayan in which the Third Division convicted Mrs. Imelda R. Marcos of violation of the Anti-Graft Law. However, her Motion for Reconsideration was referred to the Banc which - after conducting Oral Argument - reversed the Third Division and acquitted her.

2. Firestone Ceramics v. Court of Appeals in which a unanimous Third Division Decision dismissed the appeal of petitioner. However, by a vote of 8-7, the Banc decided to take up the Motion for Reconsideration. After Oral Argument was called, the Motion was denied unanimously by the Banc.

In the present case, however, and despite my plea for Oral Argument to clarify new questions raised on a matter which I believed was of "transcendental importance" and which affected almost the entire country, the Third Division resolved to deny the Motion for Reconsideration without any verbal hearing or referral to the Banc.
