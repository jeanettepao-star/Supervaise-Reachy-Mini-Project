# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Appendix A: Life Technologies and the Rule of Law
Source:     The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary (book)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

Appendix A

Life Technologies and the Rule of Law

Dr. Franklin M. Zweig, Ph.D., JD

President

Einstein Institute for Science, Health & the Courts

Maryland, USA

Please accept my heartiest congratulations on the occasion of the Philippine Supreme Court's 100th Anniversary.

I know I bring the Einstein Institute's concurring best wishes, shared by all men and women of goodwill and caring, that your distinguished Court will independently prosper and rule for many centuries ahead as the protector and guarantor of the rule of law.

Acknowledgment of the role of the Philippine Judicial Academy is also appropriate. As the affairs of nations, laws and economies intermingle to become ever more complex, the continuous education of judges gains in importance. It falls upon the courts' education arms to provide the foundation for judging the 21st century's cases. Only an educated, up-to-date judiciary can retain public confidence and enhance their judicial systems' independent status. In the United States, our Judicial Branch leaders deeply appreciate the Philippine Supreme Court's commitment to the highest quality education and program.

Your decision to memorialize the Supreme Court's centenary anniversary with a Lecture Series is commendable. It is also imaginative.

Albert Einstein is reported to have said in a scientific context that "knowledge is important, but imagination is more so." Knowledge, and its academic celebration in the legal context, builds and reinforces institutional memory, imaginatively preparing us all for the future while at the same time observing and honoring your court system's institutional history.

A lecture series is also a bold commemoration of past values presented in such a way to foreshadow the future. It permits an American lawyer who manages a non-governmental judicial science education agency to raise with you the challenges that high courts and trial courts around the world face in the near future. The principal theme I would like to discuss with you tonight is this:

Imposed upon our global society by the unprecedented rise, expansion and pervasiveness of biological technologies we should anticipate, analyze and deliberate cases and controversies for which historically we have not only no precedent, but no analogy.

Let's explore together a few scenarios connecting life technologies and the rule of law.

The Biology of Violent Conduct

Our scientific adventures in human genetics, fortified by the use of computer science, may well report in the next few years, for example, significant limitations to human abilities to exercise free will. If scientifically valid studies from Iceland, Sardinia and Estonia, among a growing number of indigenous genetic databases, concur in finding the genetic levers and limiting gradients for conduct, particularly violent conduct or addictive disorders, our global legal institutions may well be required to revisit longsettled principles of jurisprudence.

Among those principles is the foundation concept of individual responsibility for harmful actions freely taken. Many prominent scientists believe that national databases capable of analyzing thousands of genes simultaneously will soon disclose the chemistry of free will. And many expect to find significant variations in the degrees of freedom that chemistry confers upon individuals. In the United States, we have sometimes bantered that criminal and civil cases alike will be geneticized, and provide new claims in matters of criminal conduct and civil liability. "My genes made me do it" seems to be the underlying metaphor.

How knowledgeably and imaginatively national and international jurisprudence will harness such science remains to be seen. Some cases may be less direct, however. "My genes made it impossible for me to prevent myself from doing it" may be a more sophisticated, more scientifically respectable claim in criminal justice proceedings.

In the United States, we have experienced one famous case that may be a harbinger of the future. See Mobley v. State 455 S.E.2d61(ga.) cert denied 516 U.S. 942 (1955). In Mobley, the chemistry of disabled non-violent behavior caused by a genetic mutation was raised in defense of a murder suspect. Let's look at the facts and then review the science.

Defendant Mobley was accused by the State of shooting to death a pizza parlor manager and of armed robbery. The murder was particularly grizzly. Observed by other employees, the manager was forced to a kneeling position following the robbery and shot, execution style, in the back of the head. Mobley was apprehended in a police chase following the suspect's escape from the crime scene by automobile. The gun used in the murder was thrown away during the chase, but later found. Ballistics tests indicated that the recovered weapon had been used in previous robberies, although none of the previous discharges resulted in death.

At trial, defendant Mobley's attorney attempted to introduce scientific evidence reported by Dr. Brunner and associates derived from a large family of Dutch felons apparently predisposed to intergenerational acts of violence. Mobley, his counsel argued, suffered from the absence of an essential brain chemical; that loss rendered Mobley incapable of controlling his murderous rages and murderous conduct. In short, Mobley was unable to exercise reason and control over his impulses and should not have been held responsible legally. Let's look at the scientific background.

Brunner, a well-regarded scientist, noticed that many forms of violent, anti-social behavior ran in a large family he studied in the Netherlands. Dutch authorities recorded the family's violent, criminal history among five generations of males. Using a combination of laboratory tests, Brunner determined that the family exhibited in common a deprivation of an important neurotransmitter, Monomine Oxidase A.

Earlier, but relatively recently, studies had demonstrated that that Monomine Oxidase A is an essential protein for the breakdown of serotonin, a primary neurotransmitter whose extended presence in brain synapses can cause hyperactivity, excessive irritability, combative attitude, and may be associated with aggressive, anti-social behavior. One minor change in the gene responsible for Monomine Oxidase A shut off supply of the chemical. As a result serotonin remained in its most powerful form in the brain to excite brain activity for abnormally long time periods and with abnormal behavioral results.

Defendant Mobley's counsel petitioned the judge presiding in the murder trial for genetic tests to determine that Mobley indeed exhibited the genetic mutation that deprived him of Monomine Oxidase A. The petition was denied, accompanied by the judge's reason that such DNAbased genetic tests (in the early 1990s) were too expensive for the State to bear. The defendant's father, a millionaire businessman, volunteered to pay for the tests, and counsel renewed the petition. It was again denied, based on the judges assessment that such tests were too experimental, unproven and unreliable to be used in justice proceedings.

The matter moved to trial. The defendant was convicted of murder. He was sentenced to death after the chemically mediated loss of free will argument failed to convince the court that he warranted a lesser sentence.

In a post-conviction remedy motion, habeas corpus, Mobley moved for a new trial based on his contention that he had been ineffectively represented by his counsel at his first trial. Failure to obtain and introduce the genetic tests lay at the foundation of Mobley's motion for a new trial. The motion was denied and the matter was appealed to the Georgia Supreme Court. The court ruled that Mobley's counsel had represented him effectively in requesting genetic and other tests, and that the trial judge properly denied the petition based on the then known validity of gene testing technology. The court opined as well that the MOA theory might well not apply to Mobley at all, since the original Dutch family studied evidenced inherited mental retardation as well as violent tendencies. The court noted that Mobley had been tested and found to possess normal intelligence. An appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court failed to grant a hearing. Mobley is currently incarcerated on death row in Georgia.

The Mobley case is but the tip of the iceberg of new legal arguments that will be based in neuro and behavior genetics. As large databases replicate and combine scientific studies and monitor genetic tests administered in large numbers, gene mutation profiles will emerge. Those profiles hold great hope of identifying genes that predispose people for risk of mental diseases. High among the national genome database projects currently underway, for example, is the search for genes that cause or contribute to the scourge of schizophrenia. The hope is to find candidate genes through tests, and then to perfect the gene therapies that can restore people to full mental vitality. But such medical findings may also disclose the biological basis for personal violent cond If so, violence causation may gain a new paradigm, that is, as a biomedical, rather than a social condition.

From tests alone, courts may well insist that the capacity to form a reasoned action will continue to be the legal standard, but that insistence could relent if new technologies provide cures to inherited violence. Then the courts could be confronted with not only conduct-mitigating biomedical defenses. The demand to treat violence with gene therapies likely would follow. Here the administration of justice could face a simple problem and several complex ones.

If reliable gene therapies applied to individuals could "cure" violent conduct, or addictions, or tendencies to embezzle property, for example, courts would be faced merely with the question of sentencing offenders to treatment. Genetic treatments presumably would be enduring and non-obvious. Once the mutations were "repaired," permanent rehabilitation could be expected. In most law-abiding societies, courts would only worry about side effects. Gene therapies might trigger other medical conditions, and a presiding judge would find herself weighing the therapeutic value against side effect's health risks. (Neurotransmitter chemical treatments would be far less reliable as a treatment alternative; courts routinely confront defendants and probationers who have abandoned their medications.)

Two kinds of complex problems confront courts from slightly different technologies. The first could be a demand to modify inherited behavioral traits permanently. That is, in addition to treating the individual with genetic technologies, policies could be advocated to prevent the unwanted conduct in future generations. There is every scientific basis to accomplish germ-line gene therapies, although the technologies have not yet moved from laboratory to the clinic. Here body cells are not the treatment target; sex cells are. How should a Constitutional court rule in the face of public demands, say through legislation, to modify the germ line?

A second problem concerns the biomedical prediction of violent criminal behavior. Let's assume that computergenerated profiles of thousands of genes indicate the likelihood that a particular family or individual is predisposed to violence. Should they be treated preventively d      for a statistical similarity to dangerousness? How would a court develop laws so as to balance society's predicted benefit against protected personal rights? If behavioral profiles are statistically questionable, should courts endorse universal genetic screening for offensive genetic components? The technologies will soon exist to measure thousands of genes at one time, at birth, for example. Should such screening results be routinely made available to law enforcement? Will a demand surface for biomedical courts?

All such inquiries will provide jurisprudential headaches aplenty by this decade's end. They illustrate the coming clash between life technologies and the law. It is difficult to conceive of a scenario in which independent courts of any nation will be spared a role in adjudicating these high-impact decisions. In fact, some may directly be aimed at judges, the professional core of effective dispute resolution systems.

Genetic Testing of Judges for Dementing Illnesses

Judges are public officers, and the public often demands to know their officer's health histories. Judges mediate and adjudicate heated conflicts, and their own deportment is constantly subject to scrutiny. Such observations can be used to justify pre-appointment and pre-election as well as reappointment and re-election genetic tests applied to judges.

The main question that judicial screening commissions and the public clamor to know is whether a candidate is likely to experience a serious dementing condition during service on the Bench. Alzheimer's disease and stroke are the disabling dementias most commonly feared. Both can generate emotional turmoil in opposition to expected judicial temperament. Both rob their victims of memory and language. So if it were possible to estimate a judicial candidate's risk for such conditions, why would it not be in the public interest to predictively diagnose such conditions by simple genetic tests before onset? It is far easier to deny admission to juridical robes than to remove a sworn judicial officer based on disability allegations.

At present, prediction of dementing illnesses is a flat enterprise. Even though four genes have been found to be linked to Alzheimer's disease, for example, they are predictive only half the time. And 50% of persons actually afflicted with Alzheimer's disease test negatively for the f genetic markers currently available. There are obviously unseen genetic and environmental causes, promoters, and enhancers at work. It is possible that a developmental cascade of 30 genes will be found to play a role. Those pathways will be found by the end of this decade. The court's challenge now is to understand the state and direction of the science, to assess emerging gene technologies, and to determine how these forces should be channeled into legal domains supportive of dispute resolution institutions.

A Science and Technology Primer

With your leave, it might be helpful to take a short break from the judicial policy issues engendered by life technologies. Let us explore a brief overview of the scientific basis for human biotechnology. It is not mystical and is well within the reach of the average person, even lawyers, as I can attest. Science's amazing feature is that the code of life extends across all of nature, and the same basics underpinning human life are relied upon by all animals, plants, and bacteria.

Following this primer, I shall outline case scenarios prominently poised to surface in national and international dispute resolution forums. And then I will confide to you a few capacity-enhancing steps I believe court systems - unable to escape the fray in light of legislative fears about genomic policymaking - need to take in this extraordinary age of genetic engineering.

Life technologies underpinning ideas are simple.

A. All life is embodied in and expressed by cells. These are small biochemical factories that build up and break down wed   proteins, the molecules of life. They are numerous; we may each have a trillion cells in our bodies. As we sit in the majesty of this hall of justice, we are elegantly configured masses of our   cells performing different tasks to bring us our humanity.

B. In each cell are all the biological machines - called organelles - to survive, grow, reproduce, communicate and supervise the production of proteins. In (Your Favorite Cell's) YFC's center is an organelle called the Nucleus and within each nucleus are the 46 shipping packages of inheritance, the chromosomes, along which are strung all our genes, about 100,000 of them. The Human Genome. Project has almost completed identification of all our genes

C. What do all these genes do?

They simply instruct organelles in the cell, outside of the nucleus, to make proteins. The basic idea is that one gene sends an instruction to the cell's protein assembly machine, the Ribosome, to assemble a protein from amino acids. Remember the gene that instructs the production of MOA? It is a single protein that works on serotonin breakdown and removal from the brain's firing structures. It takes one gen to instruct the right cells to make that protein.

D. Finally, life technologies are significantly based upon the fact that cells can be engineered in numerous ways using microscopic instruments. Whole animals can be cloned. There is no technological reason why humans cannot be cloned as easily as sheep, cows, mice or pigs.

Judges' Science Tools for Case Management

of a Wide Range of Case Scenarios

I would like to congratulate you for your successful mastery of these life science and technology basics. They are the foundation of evidence that will soon be presented to courts everywhere on our small planet.

Our courts need the tools with which to manage such evidence in cases of all kinds. The Philippine Courts, I am told, currently consider biotechnology issues. Before long. biotechnology evidence will be marshaled not only for controversies concerning medical, environmental and agricultural disputes, but in indirect proofs of civil liability and criminal intent and decedents' estates and personal competency as well.

At EINSHAC, we have compiled a working list of case scenarios. For many of these cases we have written fact patterns giving rise to trial or policy adjudication, state of the science, state of law, and issues facing judges. These considerations compromise the foundation of judicial handbooks that we will begin to publish this year. We do not recommend or even propose outcomes. Decision is the Judiciary's province. Our quest is to develop clearly the landscape of considerations that might be considered as judges reach their decisions. The following case scenarios list is presented for your consideration. It suggests the important, changing role that bioscience and biotechnology evidence and issues will play in many courts before this decade ends.

The Eugenics Shadow for Case Scenarios

Mention should be made of eugenics, the last century's discredited social doctrine based on false biological information manufactured for the justification of discrimination and individual and population group harms. I need not catalogue eugenics world-wide, dreary history. It will suffice to note that as we enter the age of the New Biology, all case scenarios are shadowed by the fact that gene technologies provide a new basis for favoring some heritages and eschewing others. Courts must be especially sensitive to elevating our conception of human rights above the formulas that will be advanced by some to deprive people of their humanity based on genetic arguments. While life technologies can be expected to improve the quality of life, the rule of law must trump all enterprises and place in proper context that each human person has equal rights under law.

The International Perspective

for Life Technology Disputes

Biotechnology offers the best hope in human history for cost-effective health care, remediation of degraded and dangerous natural environments, and a sufficient and nutritious food supply for ten billion who will inhabit this planet by the quarter-century mark.

From molecular medicine, we will soon inherit treatments for human illnesses once thought entirely impossible. They include cancers, diseases of combined genetic and environmental causation. From veterinary biotechnology, we may be able to assure biological diversity of the world's species and the conservation of endangered species. From microbial biotechnology, we soon could harness the means to use engineered bacteria for environmental restoration. From agricultural biotechnology, we can expect nutrition supplements and vaccines to be built into health-promoting foods. The economies of many nations are now directed to biotechnology as a hope for future trade, wealth and security. From these hopeful perspectives, there is no better time to be alive and there never has been a better time for children's advent into the world.

At the same time, these life technologies spawn disputes that place courts front and center in the resolutions that will enhance their benefits and contain their risks. Courts could anticipate them in the course of conferencing and judicial education activities without diverting too much time or effort from the normally daunting responsibilities of case processing and management.

Three life technology issues arise all over the world. They provide a strong reason for courts to develop a global community of discourse. The first is human experimentation. The second is the nature and status of biological property. The third is the release of genetically modified organisms into the environment.

Human experimentation. Courts will hear cases of direct and indirect human experimentation. Direct human experimentation is conducted through clinical trials based upon people. While laboratory and animal studies guide technology development, the only certain way to assess biological tests and therapies intended for humans is to test them on humans. Gene therapy to repair Monomine Oxidase A deficiency, for example, can only be proved safe and efficacious in human clinical trials. But who conducts these trials, and with what ethical safeguards is of paramount importance. Courts will soon get cases in large alleging breach of human subject protections or breach of human rights conventions in the absence of explicit human subject protections. Unless courts understand the scientific and technological basis for these trials, conditions exist for important errors and harms.

Many nations have laws, regulations and standards governing human clinical trials. Judges must understand the legal causes and biomedical causation in disputes alleged to violate national standards. This matter holds international implications because much human experimentation will be conducted by international teams, especially transnational corporations seeking new drugs, vaccines, and medical technologies. Because the world's legal domain is so varied as to the nature of human subject policies and remedies, it. will be important for judges to know the span of legal doctrines, and what their colleagues in other nations apply as law in such cases.

Indirect experimentation is conducted by monitoring the effects upon human populations of life technologies directed initially at environmental remediation and agricultural products. Our interdependent, interactive world assures multiple impacts from intervention intended for one purpose but resulting in several outcomes. Cleaning a waterway from bacteria seeping from agricultural livestock, for example, may result in some unanticipated consequences for humans using it. Fashioning remedies in such complex, indirect cases will be common tasks in law courts as engineered biology is used to promote life. Judges will be asked to rule on evidence generated by new scientific techniques, such as meta-analyses and risk analyses. Public confidence in rulings will depend in large part upon the judiciary's confidence in managing such evidence.

The nature and status of biological property is a matter for international treaty, but some nations or constituencies feel left out of considerations. They will take their cases to court. They will plead for equity and argue to invalidate treaty provisions. To deal with such claims, judges will need to know the various schemes of intellectual property protection.

Finally, food and agricultural disputes can be expected to expand. Changes in the nature of food deeply affect cultural perspectives. Control over food is one of human kind's most dearly protected privileges. We can expect legal attacks upon new technologies that threaten to control. Judges, again, will fortify confidence in the rule of law when their decisions and the opinions supporting those decisions disclose an understanding of the biological elements brought to the Bench as essential ingredients of their disputes.

The Prospects for Effective Adjudication

of Life Technology Disputes

Never in prior human history have we had the ability to plan and engineer our biological future. Life technologies now place that future squarely in our hands. That power used for human good could produce unlimited benefits - cure of disease, remediation of handicaps, supplies of vital replacement organs, extended life spans among them. Never in human history have we had the opportunity to squander our biological resources and distort life technologies for unlawful purposes.

Judicial capacity can be built to apply the rule of law to the optimistic prospects and dispute scenarios promised by life's new technologies. But judicial branches can no longer expect national borders to confine disputes or decisions. Judicial capacity can best be enhanced by establishing and maintaining a community of discourse, an interacting judicial core from every nation boasting an independent judicial system.

To effectively apply and enforce the law under these new, volatile, promising and fear-generating conditions, courts will benefit from collegial consultation and exchange. Effective adjudication never depended so much upon shared jurists' perspectives. Respectful differences in those perspectives can be bridged by the enrichment provided by shared judicial experiences. EINSHAC's International Program will continue in 2001 with a working conversation in Hawaii for Common Law and Pacific Rim courts, in the Hague, and in Rome. We hope that the wonderful knowledge and legacy of the Philippine Supreme Court and its constituent forums can be shared with the world's judiciary.

EINSHAC and its judicial and legal leadership look forward to the Philippine Supreme Court's legacy for the next hundred years, a legacy that will include leadership in harnessing burgeoning life technologies to the rule of law.
