Chapter 13 

__21st Chief Justice of the Philippines__

__		“I, the Lord, have called you for the victory of justice\. __

__				I have grasped you by the hand; __

__		I formed you, and set you as a covenant of the people,__

__				     A light for the nations,__

__			         To open the eyes of the blind,__

	 		__To bring out prisoners from confinement,__

__		And from the dungeon, those who live in darkness\.”__

__  							\(Isaiah 42: 6\-7\)__ 

It was almost eight o’clock in the evening\. Most people had gone home and the halls were empty, save for an occasional janitor\. Justice Art sat behind his desk\. It was quiet in his chamber\.  He had stayed on to review other tribunals’ decisions assigned to him\. The work was mainly affirming, modifying, or reversing orders of lower courts\. It was work that suited him and he did not mind the long hours, or the weekends spent reducing the backlog of the Court\. 

There were other adjustments he made upon his assumption of office\. He divested himself of all his interests in the law firm he had established and sold off, completely and absolutely, all shares he owned\. Art also resigned from his business involvements, barring none\. There were 15 organizations where he had been an active member but from which he disengaged following his appointment\. He no longer took part in any of these professional, civic, religious, charitable and nonjudicial associations, except for three\. Art remained a member of the Pontifical Council for the Laity, the Rotary Club of Manila, and Bantayog ng mga Bayani Foundation, all of which were allowed for incumbent justices\.   

__The Lonely Perch__

The distancing from his circle of friends, relatives and associates was quite contrary to Art’s nature\. He was a people person who liked to engage and interact with others\. Removing himself from the company he enjoyed and found stimulating was one of the costs of his designation\. He severely limited his social life\. He only went to family get\-togethers, anniversary and birthday celebrations of close friends\. He likewise continued to take part in golf and tennis competitions with colleagues in the judiciary or friends with whom he had already been hitting the greens and the tennis courts prior to his appointment\. He was mindful to stay clear of those who had any known litigations before the Court\. 

It was not just about making himself physically scarce in normal social settings; he also refused non\-official visits and phone calls to his office\. Written instructions to the Supreme Court security force stipulated that no one was allowed into his chamber without his express permission\. Should any written material concerning Court matters be addressed and delivered to him, these were submitted for proper notation and included in the Court’s agenda\. No exceptions were made for those marked “personal\.” He held that there was nothing personal about matters pending before the Court\. He took the opportunity, during speaking engagements and whenever appropriate, to ask his audience the favor of not writing or calling him to discuss any matter pending before the Court\.  Strictly adhering to this code of conduct, he risked the alienation and disappointment of others\. It weighed on his heart, but his commitment to duty was ironclad\. 

Remarks he delivered at the retirement of Justice Antonio Martinez included a quote from Anthony Lewis’ book *Gideon’s Trumpet,*__ __which intimated his understanding of what the position entailed__: “__\[A\] justice brings to the task only his character, his intellect, his education, his experience, his human understanding, his imagination\.” 

Art went on to say that, premised on a quote from Lewis, “a justice relies on his creativity, innovation, reflection and prayers\. Career jurists who ascended to the Supreme Court through well\-earned promotions in the judicial hierarchy are in a sense lucky, because their adjustments to the lonely perches on this tribunal were not sudden or unexpected, unlike those of us who were picked straight from private practice or from the academe or from the nonjudicial branches of government\.”

__The Good Judge et al\.__

__	__There was no sentimental attachment to or longing for the social whirl to which he had been accustomed\. Without reservation, he accepted the norm that went with his office\. He spoke of the attributes of a good judge in an inspirational message delivered during the closing ceremonies of a career development workshop for first\-level judges\.  “Those who wear the black robes are enrolled in a noble mission and become different persons\.  They forfeit their past activities, friends and even relatives and devote full time, attention and effort to the rather reclusive and exclusive world of decision\-making\.

	“\[M\]agistrates must live in a world of reason where logic, evidence and conscience are their only yardsticks\.” He held that it was necessary for jurists to be able to carry out their responsibilities and fulfill the role of the Supreme Court, which was “to uphold the basic norms of society and defend the fundamental rights of the people as enshrined in the Constitution and the laws, notwithstanding tyrants, lynch mobs and even best\-intentioned but ill\-informed crusaders\.”

	The conscientious application of his responsibilities produced 1,200 full\-length decisions over 10 years\. Justice Antonio T\. Carpio, who served in the Supreme Court together with Art, described him as “undoubtedly the most prolific writer in the Court, bar none\.”  It was an extraordinary  stint made even more remarkable by the publication of 10 books since he began his term in 1995\. There was a book every year written in a style lauded by his fellow justice Angelina Sandoval Gutierrez, who praised his “preeminent judicial craftsmanship, social philosophies and literary style\.” These volumes were Art’s way of reporting on his magistracy\. 

	Opinions he penned more than intimated a pro\-poor stance\. The deprivations of his past had widened the scope of his understanding\. Without prejudicing facts, he could contextualize cases to render decisions that resonated with President Ramon Magsaysay’s slogan: “Those who have less in life should have more in law\.”

	His faculties were not confined to the august chambers, sometimes referred to as the Olympus of the gods\. He was a good speaker and was often tasked to speak on behalf of the Supreme Court, covering diverse legal controversies over varied topics\. With knowledge that was extensive, he could tackle multiple subjects, from mathematics and economics to business and accounting; and, unsurprisingly, canon law–the legal system of the Catholic Church\. 

 	Art was invited to speak on different occasions to address local and international audiences\. Speaking engagements brought him to different parts of the world to talk on various subjects other than law\. When he was invited to participate in two international fora held in Chile in 2005, he took to the podium to give five lectures on bioscience\. Chief Justice Davide would describe his colleague’s “mental dexterity” as an ability to “extricate the possible from the hypothetical, the emerging from the established, the literature in science and the law in art\.”

__Interrupted Speech__

	Art was in a good place, grounded in an acknowledgement of being a steward for all that he now regarded as blessings from God\. He found satisfaction in his work and his family life\. There was nothing more to ask from his Supreme Benefactor\. Gratitude welled up when he considered the station he had not even imagined for himself\. It was so much more than his father’s dream or his own\. That God would continue to outdo the dream was something he did not foresee\.

	When Chief Justice Davide was due for retirement in the latter part of 2005, the Judicial and Bar Council was tasked to submit to President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo a list of names for his successor\. Art, being one of the senior members of the Court, was in the short list of three nominees from whom the chief executive would select the next chief justice\.  Unlike his experience with President Ramos, he had no interaction, much less any conversation, with the sitting head of state\. Prayer was his only course of action and it was a supplication for God’s will, and God’s will alone\. It would have been untrue to posture as if the inclusion did not stir some hope and aspiration in his heart\. That feeling, however, was tempered by his belief that the choice would be God’s own to make and administered by the powers that be\. 

	It was on the 7th of December, his 69th birthday, when the president’s decision reached Art\. He was behind a rostrum addressing employees of the Supreme Court at the Pan Pacific Hotel\.  The short speech to thank his audience for their support and cooperation during his terms as chair of several committees was interrupted when someone hastily slipped something into his hand\. He read the scribbled message and was thunderstruck\. The slip of paper with the handwritten line spelled out what would become the highest point of his career: “I heard on DZBB that the president had appointed you as chief justice\.”	

	He blinked and read it again\. It was the only time in his life that Art was at a loss for words\. He stared at the people who had been listening to his remarks\. As he looked at them, they sensed that something was afoot\. The momentary pause in the middle of his address seemed a stretch to him as he took in the significance of the information\. He collected his thoughts and resumed speaking, apologizing that he would be unable to finish his speech, then excused himself and left the stage\.  

Art looked for Leni who had been watching him quietly\. When he motioned her to join him, she left her seat and walked beside him\. His gait was a little quicker as he avoided media reporters waiting to interview him\.  The Panganiban couple headed to the hotel entrance where their driver pulled up\. When the couple settled inside the car, he told his wife the news and showed her the note\. Art was about to begin a new chapter in his storied life, and this time he knew who was the Author writing it\. Instructions were given to the driver to bring them to Santuario de San Antonio, their parish church\. Once there, they made their way to the prayer room within the church grounds\.  It was there in that quiet sanctuary, a few hundred meters away from the main church, where he thanked the Lord for what he regarded as a magnanimous blessing and offered himself once more to God\. He prayed on bended knees, with Leni by his side, for the grace to carry out the mandate of the office of the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court and to give all the glory to God\.

He stayed in the softly lit room, feeling embraced by a deep sense of his Divine Father’s presence\. Thoughts of his life journey flashed through his mind, and gratitude welled up even for the failures that he now saw were part of God’s way to lead him onto his course to the Supreme Court\. Had he pursued his business enterprises abroad in Hong Kong or the United States, he would most likely have been too preoccupied to give God any attention and consequently would never have taken the road that would lead to the Supreme Court\.    

__Lobbying in the Church__

	Art succeeded Hilario G\. Davide Jr\. and became the 21st Chief Justice of the Philippines\. He had, by then, disengaged from his numerous civic, social and religious associations, save for his service as a Special Minister of Holy Communion\. Upon his assumption as Chief Magistrate, the parish priest Fr\. Antonio Rosales approached him as he had previously done years before when Art became a member of the high tribunal\. The priest asked if he would continue to distribute the consecrated Bread and Wine to the parishioners, and if special arrangements were needed to accommodate security and seating protocol\.

	Art replied that while he was the highest judicial official of the land, 	he remained a regular parishioner of the Santuario de San Antonio\. Expressing his desire to continue as a Eucharistic minister with the prelate’s permission, he dispelled the notion that extra arrangements or protocols were necessary\. Additionally, he assured Fr\. Rosales that the security personnel assigned to him would remain as inconspicuous as possible\.

	Art continued as if nothing had changed\. There were occasions when some parishioners would give him folders and notes while he was in church\. They would entreat him to make favorable decisions on their cases that had been languishing in the courts\. One elderly woman was particularly forward and reproached him for not acting on her behalf\. “Chief Justice, I take communion from you every Sunday, but you never seem to remember my petition against a big bank that is pending before the Supreme Court,“ she said\. “Have pity on me\. I am a poor widow\. I pray for your favorable decision\.”

	Unfazed by the play on his sympathies, he responded by advising her not to pray to him or use the church premises to lobby for her cause\. He enjoined her to pray to the Lord, instead, and to put her trust in God’s might and providence\. He concluded by saying that he, too, was a praying man who invoked God before rendering his decisions and was confident that the Almighty would grant him enlightenment to render a just judgment in her case\.

__The Role of the Chief Justice__

__	__The standard of conduct Art had set was not just for himself, but for the entirety of his colleagues in the profession\. He held himself to lead by example, steering judiciary by adhering to unquestionable behavior or speech\. As Chief Justice, he was the first among equals, *primus inter pares, *in the highest court of the land\.  

He also stood as leader of a judiciary that counted an estimated 2,000 justices and judges, and an estimated 26,000 personnel serving the courts nationwide\. This amalgam of public servants needed an exemplar to emulate and to inspire them\.  It was not only before them that he upheld the bar of integrity but, as a public official, he was answerable to the people as a whole\. Public esteem factored into the credibility and success of the Supreme Court\. 

Had he not already assimilated the values of his Christian faith, the task of being a model of upstandingness and probity would have been burdensome\. Art made no claims to possessing moral ascendancy\. He was aware of his finiteness and his need to stay connected to God, who he believed had brought him to the position of chief justice\.   

 This consciousness exacted the best of what he could give in his dual roles of *primus inter pares* and head of the judiciary\. He was making a return for the good his Lord had given him\. There were no overt spiritual overtones in managing the flow of Court proceedings or shaping the Court’s agenda, opening the discussion of cases or summarizing arguments\. These were duties attendant to his position, but he performed them with deliberate mindfulness to honor God\. It was the same when he addressed administrative, management and financial concerns of the entire judicial department of government\. He saw himself as a steward accountable to and in service of the Master\. It was a position of privilege that demanded nothing less than excellence as a response to an excellent God\. That was the standard he held himself to and the standard that he likewise enjoined upon others\.
