I.

Risks and the Promise

What Lies Ahead in Asia?

Biotechnology is one field where the law appears to lag behind science. Yet modern biotechnology is now a booming multi-billion dollar industry worldwide. Food biotechnology, in particular, holds the promise of abundance. But it is mired in controversy. Much of it concerns genetically modified organisms and the dangers they pose, when released to the environment or ingested as art of food products for people or feeds for livestock.

Bishop Jesus Varela, an adviser of Biotechnology Foundation, recently underscored the essence of the debate about GMO and food products:

"Society has a moral responsibility to feed the world's burgeoning population. The solution is not population control, but rather food production . . . food biotechnology promises us this is possible though there are risks . . .

"Whether GMO can and will deliver its promise to alleviate world hunger remains to be seen. It has enormous potential. But the risks have to be addressed." (Phil. Daily Inquirer, "Ex-bishop urges caution on GMOs," 12/08/03, p.14, c.6. See also his paper at the Conference on the "Ethics of GMO: Its Application to the Philippines.")

Last month, Bishop Dinualdo Gutierrez of South Cotabato touched on a current feature of the controversy: ongoing street protests. Streamers in churches and cathedral in Mindanao denounced products containing GMO. This appeared to be part of the struggle against transgenic crops and foods entering the country. The religious sector in Koronadal City launched a campaign to boycott certain staples, particularly Bt corn. A parish priest there, Fr. Bo Parreño, averred the boycott aims "to secure the health of humans from the alleged hazardous effects of genetically altered crops." An advocacy officer of the diocesan justice and peace desk, Gloria Sabit, added that the local church was urging parishioners "to boycott the products containing GMOs because they have not been proven safe to human health and the environment." (Today,'"Boycott of GMO food launched," 22/07/03, p. 3, c.2).

What is happening in Mindanao is not isolated. Violent demos, mass rallies, and hunger strikes have also been staged in Metro Manila, Northern Luzon, and the Visayas, against Bt corn and other GMO crops.

The response of the government to the boycott demanding a moratorium on biotech crops such as Bt corn partakes of a forensic challenge to anti-biotech forces. Secretary Luis "Chito" Lorenzo, Jr., has stated that a moratorium would mean "reversing more than five years of scientific studies by the government and the private sector." The government, he stressed, would consider a moratorium on Bt corn planting in the country only after there is a showing by "overwhelming" scientific evidence that the crop poses health and environmental dangers. This response flies in the face of the assertion of some scientists, including Dr. Chito Medina of U.P., Los Baños, that Bt corn and other genetically altered crops were not yet "scientifically proven to be safe for consumption." The forensic battle of the two "Chitos" is reflected in media debates involving not only activist laymen but highly trained scientists and technocrats, such as a hunger strike leader, Engr. Ben Bersola. It is safe to say that the GMO controversy is not limited to scientific fora and journals abroad. It has now intruded into public decision-making here as well.

Yes, GMO is now in Asia. Independent of foreign aid, China has built up its biotechnology capacity. It has been noted that China has done better than most developing countries to promote use of GM crops. (UNU/Intech I:2:2002). The worry in the developing world, according to Kiran Mazumdar-Shaw, chairwoman of India Biocon company, "is this: Why has Europe banned it? It must be for " Yet, she believes that India must enhance a reason. biotechnology to overcome widespread poverty, malnutrition, hunger, and disease. (IHT, "From Indian taboo, biotech is born," 8/19/03, p. 11, c.2).

At the outset, we must acknowledge the historical contributions of two monks, Mendel and Malthus, to the present debate. GM coincidentally are the initials of Gregor Mendel, 1822-84, the recognized father of the science of modern genetics. It was this Moravian monk who cultivated different kinds of peas and recorded the effect of their crosspollination on hereditary traits. The other clergyman, Thomas Malthus, 1766-1834, is remembered for a theory named after him. His essay, On the Principle of Population, posited a crowded planet where food supply is outstripped by population growth, so that starvation and death result. At the time, 1798, earth’s population was about 800 million only. Now, the same number, if not more, go to bed hungry every night. The world population now exceeds 6 billion, but acreage of arable land actually diminished for various reasons like war, urbanization, climate change, and desertification. It is said that in some southern African countries, e.g. Zambia, Zimbabwe and Mozambique, some 15 million people face starvation yearly because of drought and flood. In northern Asia, a vast expanse of mainland China is being overrun by dust and sandstorm, destroying pastures and croplands, obviously without regard to ideology.

Expanding population and dwindling resources, sparking local wars and widespread violence, are not a novelty in our midst. That is why those in government including the judiciary must take heed of what lies ahead. Let me just call attention to one forecast:

"By 5050 or so, the world population could start to level off at about 9 billion. Most of the increase in population will be in the developing w o r l d . . . All the signs point to a future in which the world's poor will not be able to afford enough food to live on and in which the distribution system in undeveloped countries will not improve sufficiently to distribute [food] that is available." (Peter Pringle, Food Inc., p. 20.)

Forewarned is forearmed. Could recombinant DNA technology or bio-engineering (gene splicing or genetic modification) of staple crops and food products come to the rescue? If so, at what risks, and what price?

II.

Patent on a Life Form: Microbes Win in America?

During a round-table conference with judges of the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit and patent attorneys in Washington, D.C., this spring, we had a chance to discuss whether a living creature or life form could be the subject of a valid patent. Attention was called to the American case of Diamond v. Chakrabarty, 447 US 303 (1980).

It appears that in 1972, Dr. Ananda Chakrabarty, a GE microbiologist, applied for a U.S. patent on "human-made, genetically engineered bacterium" capable of breaking down multiple components of crude oil, a capability not possessed by ordinary bacteria. The patent examiner denied his application on the ground that "micro-organisms are products of nature" and "living things were not patentable." The Patent Board of Appeals upheld the examiner's decision. But the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals reversed the Board. So Commissioner Diamond went on certiorari before the U.S. Supreme Court.

In a split decision, 5 to 4, the U.S. Supreme Court in 1980 held that Chakrabarty's bacterium is patentable. The majority reasoned that Congress meant the patent law to be of wide scope, so as to cover anything under the sun made by man's ingenuity. The fact that the applicant's microorganism is alive is without legal significance for purposes of the patent law, said the majority, citing In re Bergy, 563 F2d 1031. What is decisive is the fact that the applicant's microbe bore markedly different characteristics from any found in nature, with the potential for significant utility. The relevant distinction is not between living and inanimate things but between manmade inventions and products of nature. Chakrabarty's claims were upheld; his bacterium passed the triple tests of utility, novelty, and non-obviousness upon which patentability of an invention depends.

This development, according to the minority view, went against the 1970 law known as the Plant Variety Protection Act, which specifically excluded bacteria. The dissenters argued that only the U.S. Congress, not the Supreme Court, could broaden the reach of patent laws. In 1930, it was recalled, the Plant Patent Act covered only species (whether fruits, nuts or flowers) that are asexually propagated. Breeders' monopoly control over staple crops was rejected, particularly over potatoes. The 1970 statute on plant variety protection gave only patent-like protection to food crops that sexually reproduced (eg. corn, wheat and rice). The 1980 decision, from the minority point of view, looked novel indeed. This radical development was further bolstered soon by actions taken by the patent office itself. It granted a series of patents to another microbiologist (Kenneth Hibberd) on a new line of corn, covering the process of creating the new variety as well as the product - namely the DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) sequence, genes, cells, tissue culture, seed, specific parts of the plant, and the entire plant itself.

Dr. Chakrabarty was pleasantly surprised by his victory in court. All he did, according to him, was to make what amounted to a soup of different strains of bacteria hoping they would interact. What he got was a new life form, with a patent to his credit, assigned to GE. At Wall Street, the risk takers were jubilant: stock prices of biotech firms shot up dramatically.

Thereafter, it is said the patent office started granting patents on "products of nature," or functional equivalents. Products of skill rather than invention received patents. Apparently the American biotech industry was in a race against time to assure its pre-eminence globally. Even university researchers, under the Bayh-Dole Act of 1980, could take patents on federally funded projects. Later, colleges and individual academic researchers entered into deals with corporations engaged in biotechnology and related sciences. Companies were created just to buy other companies possessing patent rights.

The result? Fear of US monopolies or oligopolies in the biotech industry, agribusiness and research. Anxious Europeans responded with regulations that apparently built a grand trade barrier against American ingenuity.

III.

Labelling and Traceability:

A "Frankenfood" in Europe?

Genetically modified organisms (GMO) may be defined as organisms in which the genetic material (DNA) has been altered in a way that does not occur naturally by mating or natural recombination. The technology of alteration is known as genetic engineering, recombinant DNA technology, or modern biotechnology. This technology allows selected individual genes to be transferred from one organism into another, as well as between non-related species. (See Brussels, Memo 02/160-Rev., 01/07/03.)

Considering the complexity of GMO debates, care must be taken to make a distinction between scientific questions requiring analysis and ideological questions raised for nonscientific reasons. But pardon me if sometimes I get carried away and forget the distinction.

In 1998, the EU closed its doors to GM crops. The GM controversy devastated the US farm-products from Europe, for instance, halving the sale of soya pro engineered herbicide-resistant soybeans. Recent scare stories like the "mad cow disease," also shook public GMO food products.

Antibiotech forces fall into 3 groups: rejectionists, reformists and moderates. For safety and environmental as well as religious and ideological reasons, rejectionists believe plant biotechnology is wrong and dangerous. Reformists dislike new and alien foods for humans as well as feeds for livestock. They believe scientists, business and government mishandled the problem of GMO products. The moderate organic group of growers and consumers wanted immediate labeling of GMO products. GM crops must be isolated, to reduce cross-pollination. Tracers must be significantly used to prevent genetic pollution.

In 1996, the first GM product - a paste of tom bought fish gene - appeared in Britain. The Britons bought it enthusiastically as it was cheaper than usual. But by Christmas 1998, sales of that tomato sauce paste reportedly dropped to almost zero. Large European market chains had joined forces to eliminate GM ingredients from made it clear, product brands. Reason: US companies had made it clear, rather proudly, that if Europeans wanted American soybeans their only choice would be the GM variety. Another reason: Prince Charles of England joined the fray and encouraged protests. The public became fearful and refused to buy GM food. NGOs like Greenpeace succeeded in halting the use or import of biotech crops. "Frankenfood" became a by-word to denigrate GMO and food products as monstrous and dangerous.

In practice, the EC thru Directive 2001/18/EC continues in place a step-by-step process of approval on a case by case assessment of the risks to human health and the environment before any GMO or product consisting of or containing GMO, such as maize and tomatoes, or GMM (microorganisms) can be released to the environment or placed in the market. Directive 90/219/EEC, as amended, regulates the continued use of GMM for research and industrial purposes. Regulation (EC) 258 on Novel Food and Novel Food ingredients sets out rules for authorization and labeling of novel foods/ food products containing, consisting or produced from GMO. There are rules for genetically modified seeds and forestry reproductive material as well as medical and veterinary products. Since 1997, labeling is mandatory to indicate presence of GMO as such in a product. There are proposals now for new regulations on traceability and labeling of GMO and GMO products, including foods and feeds.

Noteworthy is the EC proposal on the regulations of exchange of GMOs with countries outside EU. This proposal appears to be in line with the UN agreement to establish by common rules for transboundary movements of GMOs in order to ensure globally the protection of human health and biodiversity (the UNEP Cartagena Protocol). GMOs for export have to be identified. Notification to those concerned must be made regarding exported GMOs intended for deliberate release into the environment, and even their accidental release. Information must also be provided to international partners of EC concerning Community legislation, decisions and practices on GMOs.

Needless to emphasize, the Europeans have been widely chided for over-regulation as well as for undermining globalization and free trade. Like rallyists before the Seattle WTO meeting, they seem to say loudly "NO, NO to GMO!"

IV.

Randy Rape Seed Case:

A Gandhi in Canada?

Several cases are now on record among the classics of the court battles between opposing forces in biotech/GMO warfare. These include subjects like Bt toxin in transgenic potato; Star Link in taco shells; RiceTec and basmati rice; Delta-Pin eland and cotton; Bt pollen and monarch butterflies. But my favorite court saga is that of a Canadia prairie farmer and the so-called Round-up Ready Canola, also known as the randy rape seed.

Past retirement age, Percy Schmeiser was one of hundreds of farmers in Canada as well as the USA sued by the Monsanto company for illegally using its proprietary seeds. Allegedly, Percy planted in his 1400-acre family farm those seeds without Monsanto's prior consent and without him signing a technical use agreement with Monsanto. At that time, some 20,000 farmers had signed up so that 40% of canola grown in Canada used Monsanto's seeds. Known popularly as Round-up Ready Canola, they contain an alien gene which made them resistant to a herbicide called Roundup, another product of Monsanto company. Monsanto's seed police found that Percy had used its proprietary seeds in 90% of his farm area.

In his defense, Percy contended that he was a victim of Monsanto's new technology which invaded his farm. He claimed the genetically modified seeds of Monsanto were blown by wind and carried by bees from Monsanto's contract farms to his own. Seed trucks of Monsanto trespassed into his land, and Monsanto's seeds fell from those trucks to his land. When the GM seeds sprouted and grew they contaminated his organic rape seeds, his plants and his harvest, he added.

Percy counter-sued, demanding $4.2 million from Monsanto for defamation, as well as for genetic pollution. According to him, Monsanto's canola plants had spread pollen all over the countryside. They cross-pollinated with conventional plants like his own. Organic farms like Percy's stood to lose their organic certificate, because of contamination by Monsanto's GM seed. These certificates take years to acquire and are prized possession. Once contaminated by GMOs, Percy's produce could no longer n   be sold as "GM-free." While the case was pending in court, Percy traveled to several countries with a message against GMO and Monsanto. India gave him a Gandhi award, and he was considered a folk hero.

The federal court, however, found in favor of Monsanto. It did not matter, said the judge, where in this case the rape seeds actually came from and how they got to be in Percy's farm. The source of the seeds is not decisive in the issue of liability for illegal use of proprietary seeds. Percy ought to know or have known that planting those seeds and growing the genetically modified canola plants without prior leave or license given by Monsanto is an infringement of the company's patent. Hence, Percy is liable to pay royalty as well as damages to the company. (Percy has elevated his case to the Canada Supreme Court, whose decision is now awaited.)

V.

Of Transgenic Mice and Men:

Life Beyond Harvard's Creation?

The last controversial case I would like to bring to the attention of our judges is that of the oncomouse, also known as the Harvard mouse case decided recently by the Canadian Supreme Court. (See Canada Com. of Patents v. Harvard College, 2002-12-05, SCC at http://www.canlii.org/ca/cas/scc/2002/ 2002cc76.html). Debated by that tribunal was the patentability of higher life forms. Could transgenic mammals like mice and even humans be patented?

Previously the USA, Japan, and many European countries have granted a patent on Harvard's mouse, which is used as a platform for the study of cancer cells. But the Canadian Supreme Court in a split decision, 5 to 4, sustained the decision of the Commissioner of Patents which denied Harvard's application to patent the oncomouse in Canada. The majority of the Court reasoned that the words "composition of matter" in the Canadian Patent Act do not the word "manufacture" in the context of said Act as commonly understood denotes a non-living mechanistic product or process, not a higher life form. (By way of distinction, Chakrabarty's patent in the USA involves a single cell microbe, a lower life form.)

Four justices, including C.J. McLachlin, dissented.

In their view, the oncomouse is a patentable product or subject matter. "The extraordinary scientific achievement of altering every single cell in the body of an animal which does not in this altered form exist in nature, by human modification of the genetic material of which it is composed is an inventive 'composition of matter' within the meaning of s. 2. of the Patent Act."

It may be noted, for emphasis, that only the claim for the product -oncomouse - was rejected by the Patent Examiner, the Patent Commissioner, and the Supreme Court's ruling. The claims for the process by which the oncomouse was produced won a patent from the Patent Office. This distinction concerning process and product is significant in patent claims.

New Zealand has patented another mouse, one injected with HIV genes. Certainly more news will be heard about mice, if not men, as transgenic mammals. Can we say that genetic modification of life forms continues to advance or desecrate our idea of life itself?
