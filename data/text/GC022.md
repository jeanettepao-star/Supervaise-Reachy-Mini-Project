CHAPTER 2

__Boyhood Years __

While his occupation as a bootblack was short\-lived, Dayunior pursued another means of earning money by peddling newspapers\. This was something he liked to do, displaying an innovative spirit coupled with hard work and determination\. He thought of ways to outsell other newspaper boys and built a wagon that he loaded with the dailies\. The future president of a national broadsheet distributed them to customers, slipping it under their doors and returning after his paper run to collect the payment\. 

“I hawked newspapers in the streets of Sampaloc, running around in quite a big area\. We woke up early in the morning, ready to pick up the fresh delivery of dailies and be first to run to the streets where customers were usually awake by 5 AM to buy their favorite paper\. 

“Later on, we decided to sleep on the sidewalk near the newspaper dealer’s apartment so we would get first crack when the delivery trucks from the publishing houses arrived\. Together with Frankie and the other newsboys, we tried to outrun one another to reach the waiting customers who paid cash – 10 centavos, and later 15 centavos per copy\. We would shout at the top of our voices: *“Manila Times\! Manila Chronicle\! Philippine Herald\! Manila Bulletin\!” *And in the afternoon, it would be *“Evening News\! Daily Mirror\! *We made two centavos per copy sold \(three, when the price was increased to 15 centavos\)\. 

*The Manila Times *was the largest circulated daily, larger than all the others combined and did not accept returns\. When we took the copies, they were considered sold\. Because I could not carry enough copies, I built a wooden *cariton *to deliver them\. And to beat the boys, I initiated a credit system in which I just handed the copies to the customers or slipped them under their doors and then came back later to collect the payments\. In this enterprising way, I became the dealer’s best newsboy\. 

“Because I sold the most copies, the news dealer, whom we simply called “Mister,” liked me\. He gave pep talks to the newsboys using me as an example of an innovative seller\. Years later, when I became one of the bar topnotchers, he gave a blow out in my honor, right there in his apartment \(also on Cataluña\), with all his newsboys at the time as guests\.” 

Artemio recalls memories of little pleasures\. “After selling our morning papers \(about 8 a\.m\.\), Frankie and I, and sometimes with two other poor relatives who were newsboys, too–Vicente Lacanilao, now deceased \(son of my maternal cousin Gregoria Lacanilao\); and a cousin, Dante Panganiban, now deceased also \(son of my paternal uncle Delfin Panganiban\)\-\-would reward ourselves at the corner Chinese *sari\-sari *store where we bought sweetened *saba *bananas in syrup with ice at 5 centavos, but at 7 centavos when mixed with Alpine or Carnation evaporated milk\. 

“At times, though, I would not be able to sell all the *Manila Times *copies I took and was inconsolable when I came home because I was *lugi\. *My father would encourage me by buying the remaining unsold copies\. One morning, due to my inability to sell all my newspapers, I tried peddling them to jeepney passengers on España and Lerma Streets\. As the passenger who bought a copy had difficulty finding loose change to pay me, I climbed the rear of the jeepney and stood on the platform\. When the light turned green, the vehicle would start to move\. After the buyer paid me, I jumped from the jeepney but lost my balance and fell on the hard, paved street\. An onrushing 6x6 military truck nearly crushed me to death, but I was able to lie down flat and clear the high wheelbase of the truck\. I cheated death and swore never to jump from a running vehicle again\.” 

The near\-death incident did not deter him from peddling newspapers\. Besides providing funds to support his education, hawking dailies was something he liked to do\. Still, when he chanced on other children playing basketball or having fun during school breaks, the boy ached inside\. Discontent over dispossession stirred in his heart\. He was deeply aware of the difference between his lot and those of classmates whose families had the means to send their children to school\. Because of their circumstances, they had the privilege of time for leisure, which was normally part of childhood, but was denied him\. Playtime was an indulgence that had to be foregone, just so he could pursue his schooling and contribute to the family’s meager resources\. Days were spent either studying or working to earn money\. 

“I enjoyed selling newspapers even if I sometimes cried my heart out comparing myself to classmates whose parents could afford their education,” AVP recalls\. “Many times, as I lay awake on the cold pavement, waiting for the break of dawn, I had to fight back tears and feelings of self\-pity\. During those times, there was no faith to comfort me\. Although born a Catholic, I did not know my religion\. I had a purely secular education at Candaba Elementary School during the war and later at the Juan Luna Elementary School and then at Mapa High School\. 

“I would think of my schoolmates as they slept on their beds, simply resting and letting the hours pass until it was time to wake up for school\. But I had to work at an early age to be able to go to school\. I would tell myself, “Today I am just a newsboy\. Someday, I will be president of the *Manila Times*\. It was then the largest\-circulated daily in the country\.” 

Years later, he would declare that God granted his wish when he became president of the *Philippine Daily Inquirer, *even if it was just for a short while since he had to give up the position when he joined the Supreme Court\. His association with the *Inquirer *resumed after he retired as Chief Justice in 2006\. Marixi Prieto, the chairperson of the PDI, invited him to write a regular column for the broadsheet in 2011\. The retired Chief Justice came on board and became a regular read in the Sunday edition of the widely circulated national daily\. His affinity with the paperboys came to the fore when he donated an entire year’s professional fee to the “Inquirer Newsboys Foundation\.” He never forgot the hardships he himself experienced selling newspapers\. 

Being poor certainly brought difficulties, but out of this poverty also came big dreams and a recognition that hard work was necessary for a good college education\. “By persevering in my studies in high school, I was able to get scholarships for college\. I continued to prioritize studies despite numerous extracurricular activities\. My strict regimen of study and training also prepared me for the painstaking bar examinations in 1960, in which I placed No\. 6 despite getting sick and hospitalized during the first week of tests\.” 

The enterprising young man continued to hawk newspapers all through secondary school\. Artemio was keen on studying engineering\. In fact, during his last two years, he chose to enroll in “Electricity” over “Journalism,” which were both optional subjects\. He dabbled in projects that stoked his interest in technological innovation\.” During my third year, I assembled a radio set partly with money partly supplied by an aunt, Arsenia Panganiban Reyes, and partly

from my newsboy earnings\. I was so happy with my achievement that I gave the radio to her in appreciation of her support\.  

The gesture of gifting his aunt opened him up to the joy of giving\. Thereafter, even in his circumstances, AVP began to buy little presents for his family and extended family\. Some of his newsboy earnings would be spent on Christmas gifts for his parents, siblings, and even his cousins, aunts and uncles\. Whatever money was left was put in an *alkansya*, which he kept long after he had become a professional\. Later in life, when he became well\-established as a lawyer, he showed the coin bank to his wife and children as a trophy of accomplishment\. 

Such a position of stability was still years away from his student days at the Victorino Mapa High School\. The struggle for funding to complete high school was constant as he balanced his paper\-distribution work and his academic requirements\. He resolved to succeed in life and recognized that a solid education was necessary to achieve his goal\. Artemio applied himself diligently to his studies at the V\. Mapa High School\. It was considered a good school with a reputation for performing well in competitions and achievement tests\. Founded in 1923, the learning institution in the district of San Miguel is one of the oldest public high schools in Manila\. Besides its high academic standards, the former Manila East High School was cited in 2007 as one of the cleanest schools in the city\. 

V\. Mapa High School counts a notable roster of alumni that included, among others, a Senate President who became the 9th Vice President of the Philippines \(Arturo M\. Tolentino\); Washington SyCip, accountant and co\-founder of the SGV & Co\. accounting firm; Jose Melo who was a member of the Supreme Court and chairman of the Commission on Elections; and jurist Artemio V\. Panganiban, who became the 21st Chief Justice of the Supreme Court\.  

Recalling his secondary school days, he shared, “During my stay at Mapa High from 1950 to 1954, there were only four major public high schools in Manila, which were all named after four eminent deceased jurists; namely, Chief Justice Cayetano Arellano in the north, Chief Justice Manuel Araullo in the south, Chief Justice Victorino Mapa in the east, and Associate Justice Florentino Torres in the west\. Of the four, Mapa High was the largest, with over 6,000 students\. Our fourth\-year batch alone was composed of over 1,200 students\.

“Apart from its wooden three\-story main building in Mendiola, which has now been replaced by a concrete structure, Mapa High rented several annexes in many parts of Manila\. I remember we went to the Guipit Annex on G\. Tuason Street, Sampaloc, for our first year; to the R\. Hidalgo Annex in Quiapo, where we had to squat on the cement floor for lack of school desks for our second year; and to the main building for our third and fourth years\.” 

Artemio finished with “Honorable Mention” from the V\. Mapa High School, ranking among the top 10 in a graduating class of 1,000 seniors\. It was no small feat for the working student who plied his daily route early in the morning before attending his classes\. The academic accomplishment entitled him to a full scholarship at the University of the Philippines\. Most of his high school teachers had graduated from UP and encouraged their best students to pursue college at the state university\. He recalls, “My high school classmates and I used to go to the university campus in Diliman\. Before the UP Oblation, we promised one another that we would study hard so we would be accepted in UP\.” However, despite qualifying for a UP scholarship, the family resources mitigated this option\. His father could not afford the daily 15 centavo fare to Diliman\. AVP was frustrated and sorely disappointed\. His father instead encouraged him to apply for scholarships at the University of Sto\. Tomas or at the Far Eastern University, which were both walking distance from their Cataluña apartment\. 

Artemio Sr\. had a wish for his son which he expressly impressed on his namesake\. He wanted Dayunior to pursue a career in law\. Becoming a lawyer was not on the young adult’s bucket list, but the son of a man who had never gone to college acquiesced in his father’s wishes\. Artemio narrated how he ended up taking a path divergent from his inclination\. “About a month prior to my high school graduation, my father noted an entry in our school’s yearbook\. Under “Ambition,” I indicated that I wanted to be a chemical engineer\. He took me aside and spoke to me seriously\. He asked that I, his youngest son, should do what he had wanted to do for himself, which was to become a lawyer\. Since I carried his name, I would be fulfilling his unrealized dream\. Besides, he added that I was a *pilosopo* who tended to argue with him and my siblings\. Our father’s word was law and, during that time, it could not be broken\. So, towards a law career I reluctantly veered my sight\.

“He himself never reached college\. Being the eldest in his orphaned family, he had to work double time at two jobs at an early age to support his seven siblings and four children\. Since I was his youngest child in a brood of four, he probably felt that I was his last chance to fulfil his frustrated ambition to be a lawyer\. 

“I was doubly disgruntled at not being able to enroll at UP or to study chemical engineering, but out of respect, I agreed to enroll in pre\-law on one condition\. If, after the prerequisite two years of Associate in Arts, I found that I didn’t like law, I would change courses and pursue chemical engineering\.” This condition was overtaken by the unexpected event of Artemio Sr\.’s demise while his Jr\. was still in his first pre\-law year\. “To honor him and his last wish, I completed my law studies and dropped the “Jr\.” from my name when I became a lawyer, so that he would truly live in me\.” 

The young Artemio followed his father’s suggestion and went to UST to apply for a scholarship\. He narrated how the old Dominican priest who interviewed him took note of his public\-school background that did not include catechetical instruction\. “I had no idea of Jesus Christ or of catechism\. I did not know my religion, “ Artemio says\. “Though baptized, I did not know about Christian faith\. My parents themselves were not steeped in their faith, so they could not teach me much\. They and our relatives hardly went to mass except during fiestas, baptisms and confirmations\. Even during the Japanese occupation of Candaba, we hardly went to church, except when my face was slapped by a visiting *obispo*\. My parents and my *ninong*, Mayor Sagcal, gave me a few Japanese currency as a gift to quell my cry when I was slapped along with about three hundred other young children who were lined up, all crying along the main Church nave\. No one explained why we were slapped by the man in robes\. “ 

The deficit of religion classes seemed to stack the odds against him as he faced the old priest\. His UST scholarship was hinged on correctly answering three questions on the Holy Trinity posed by the Dominican interviewer\. The opening salvo was a query on how many Gods there were\. The interviewee quickly replied correctly that there was only one\. The priest continued and asked, “How many persons are there in one God?” Artemio replied there were three\. Immediately he was asked to name the three as the final question\. The applicant was

stumped\. He did not know the answer and tentatively proffered, “Jesus, Mary and Joseph\.” That wrong response cost him the scholarship and a numbing pain\. He was dejected by his failure to secure a grant that would support his college education\. 

It would appear that being disadvantaged by a lack of familiarity with religious information contributed to the pursuit of his last option five minutes and two streets away\. Defeat can muster qualities within an individual that intimate something regarding a person’s character and attributes\. Still, Artemio, licking his wounds and picking himself up to apply to study in FEU, was only part of the story\. It wasn’t just a turn of events that he got the scholarship with no questions to hurdle\. The unrealized education in UP and the failure to secure a scholarship from UST would not only be overshadowed by developments at FEU\. Besides becoming a student leader who topped the academic honor roll with straight 1\.00s, his years in FEU were auspicious for his faith journey\.

__SIDE BAR 1: __

__In Grade IV at Juan Luna Elementary School, our teacher was a strict disciplinarian\. I cannot forget her name–Mrs\. Isabel Fajardo\. She always carried a ruler which she would use to tap our hands when they were dirty or to whip our buttocks when we did not study well and could not answer her questions\. Under those strict conditions, I studied hard and became one of her favorites\. Looking back, I think Mrs\. Fajardo could be credited with my being disciplined the rest of my life\. And I said so to one of her sons, Ernie Fajardo, whom I met at a dinner hosted by our neighbors in Dasmariñas Village \(Raffy and Jovie Yupangco\) after I had retired from the SC\. Jovie later formed a regular dinner group that included Leni, me, Ernie and his wife, Ning\.__
