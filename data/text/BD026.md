# The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary -- Ch. 6: Veracious and Venerable Magistrate
Source:     The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary (book)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

Chapter 6

Veracious and Venerable Magistrate

On behalf of the Supreme Court, I welcome all of you to this newly renovated, awesome, serene and majestic temple of justice in our country. You may be pleased to know that this Session Hall where we are holding our program has just been reopened last Tuesday, April 1, 2003 after being refurbished under the direction of our honoree, Mr. Justice Vicente V. Mendoza. The renovations of this Hall and several other parts of our building, including the construction of a new Division Hearing Room on the ground floor, are part of our honoree's legacy to the Court.

I am greatly honored at being chosen by Mr. Justice Vicente V. Mendoza to deliver the Opening Remarks in today's program commemorating his retirement from the Supreme Court. To fulfill my delicate task of setting the proper theme, tone and cadence of this afternoon's proceedings, I decided to speak from the heart and to relate my personal impressions of our honoree. After all, the official appreciation of the entire Court is elegantly expressed in a Plaque that will be read later on during these ceremonies.

Initial Impressions

Prior to my joining the Court in 1995, Mr. Justice Mendoza was a distant star. True, I had telescoped him from afar and learned of his solid reputation as a respected Court of Appeals justice, a hardworking assistant solicitor general and a scholarly professor of constitutional law. But we  had no personal dealings or close friendship to speak about. However, a few months into the Court, I quickly learned to look up to him as a model of how a magistrate of  the High Court should comport himself in public and in private. In my esteem, he has become one of the brightest, calmest, most diligent and most trustworthy jurists I have ever met.

True to his humble, almost self-effacing nature, Mr. Justice Mendoza does not actively volunteer his views on every question placed on our agenda. But he is always ready, when asked, to render an in-depth and a well-studied advice. He speaks in a slow, modulated baritone that exhibits a natural sense of order emanating from a logic-driven mind. The deliberate manner in which he delivers his carefully d, chosen words inspires confidence and concurrence. Indeed, he explains with the methodology of a teacher, the clarity of a savant, the finesse of an artist and the passion of a love all of which he is.

On the other hand, his written opinions, whether representing those of the majority or of the minority, are always carefully phrased, grammatically precise, and minutely calibrated to express as accurately as possible the principle expounded and the limitation within which they can be used.

He is always mindful of the advice of his fellow scholar and co-author, Professor Paul A. Freund of the Harvard Law School, who said: "Judging is a high art, and the judge, like the artist, must feel that the shoddy is an offense against his medium, as a lie is a sin against language.” Indeed, in his judicial pronouncements, Mr. Justice Mendoza is always prudent, never shoddy; and in his language, always precise, never tentative. He is moved, never by pique and spite, never by prejudice and vindictiveness, but always by deliberate and well-studied choice.

The Private Person

In contrast to his distant, majestic and almost stoic public image, he is in private a very friendly and congenial person. During our informal lunches and meetings, he would freely exchange jokes and repartees with us. He would begin a conversation with an almost wry affability but would eventually break out into a beaming, infectious and disarming smile.

To his own assistant and staff, he is known to be a caring elder who is always interested in their mundane problems and concerns. He leads them by his example of extensive research, thorough study, and long working hours even during weekends and holidays. A natural mentor, he likes to needle his subordinates for their less than perfect reports; but at the same time, he is informal enough to break bread with them at lunchtime. In short, he is a demanding disciplinarian when it comes to work, but a sensitive and considerate elder with regard to personal relations.

As a young bachelor, Mr. Justice Mendoza was shy and reserved. It took him five long years to court his wife, Thelma. She finally said "yes" only after he had repeatedly but very privately crooned to her his favorite ballad popularized in the 1950s by his tocayo, Vic Damone, entitled "In My Own Quiet Way." I will quote portions of the song that capture the winning, wooing style of Justice Vic:

"I am not the kind who sighs

Every time you wander by

Or weep when you are gone for just a day

But still and all my dear

I really love you in my own quiet way.

"I admit I'm absentminded and forgetful

Does your birthday come in April or May?

There are times when I am likely to be fretful

Or I'll pick darndest moments to be shy

But with all my faults you'll find

I'm the true and steady kind

And it's no lie believe me when I say

Deep down inside I know I'll always love you

In my own quiet way!"

Here are a few more tidbits about the private Mr. Justice Mendoza:

1. Every year during the last 39 years since their marriage in 1963, Justice Vic and Professor Thelma always visited at least 7 churches every Maundy Thursday.

2. Every New Year's Eve, he always relished biringhe, the Capampangan version of arroz valenciana, and nilagang baka with chorizo, as well as hot chocolate drink - no, not instant variety, but that which is beaten in a batirol from round chocolate balls.

3. Whenever a birthday is celebrated, there has to be a pancit to celebrate a long life.

4. His favorite dishes are pesang dalag with buro, inihaw na hito with atsarang kamias, alige ng talangka, tinolang palaka and adobong camaro or crickets. His idea of heaven on a Sunday morning is salted fresh carabao milk mixed with hot steamed rice, scooped with his bare fingers.

In his early years as a father to two sons and one daughter, he tended to be protective, strict and distant, but not confrontational. He had long ago laid down the household rules and expected all of them to observe them strictly. He exacted simple values: respect for elders, honesty, responsibility and observance of the golden rule. He was the stern padre de familia, not a back-slapping barkada to his children. He preferred to talk to them indirectly by asking Thelma to "tell them this" or "tell them that."

Still and all, behind the tough veneer is the soft-hearted father. Up to now he uses his children's childhood pictures as bookmarks and fusses over their juvenile drawings and sketches. He keeps an album of every picture and letter they had sent him until they left the family residence many years ago.

Indeed, underneath his stoic expression is a sentimental father who sheds tears of joy every now and then over a child's great achievement, or tears of compassion over a crushing failure or disappointment. But if there is anyone who can make this giant jurist dance on a rope, it is his eldest granddaughter, the apple and orange of his eye. No one, but no one, dares to interrupt his work - certainly not his Supreme Court session - except this little girl. So, when he is really in a bad mood, when he is under insurmountable stress, there is only one sedative and therapy: a hug or even just a phone call from little Allyn.

The Disciplined Jurist

As a jurist, Justice Mendoza may be characterized as traditional and conservative in the sense that he is a stickler for rules. His mind is very disciplined, methodical and deliberate. His every word and action always carries the authority or backing of some law or precedent. He does not invent or second-guess. He always moves with certainty, predictability and finality. That is why it is easy to concur in his ponencias, but difficult to dissent from them.

From every petitioner and pleader, he demands proof of standing. He does not heed a person who cannot show  any direct and viable interest in a judicial undertaking. He wants to be sure that no interloper or pretender would dilute the purity of judicial proceedings.

By the same token, he ardently believes that "the power of judicial review is premised on the existence of an actual case or controversy." To buttress this belief, he often cites Justice Felix Frankfurter and Professor Alexander M. Bickel, who both said that the concepts of standing and case and controversy tend to ensure that the "hopes and prophecies expressed in legislation [would be] tested in the actual workings of our society."

For these reasons, he rules narrowly - ever careful to excise obiters, extravagance and bravado from his ponencias. Justified by a passion for unfettered fairness, he follows to the letter the wise advice of one of his favorite American magistrates, Mr. Justice Benjamin Nathan Cardozo, who explained:

"The judge, even when he is free, is not wholly free. He is not to innovate at pleasure. He is not a knight-errant, roaming at will in the pursuit of his own ideal of beauty or of goodness. He is to draw his inspiration from consecrated principles. He is not to yield to spasmodic sentiments, to vague and unregulated benevolence. He is to exercise a discretion informed by tradition, methodized by analogy, disciplined by system and subordinated to the primordial necessity of order in social life. Wide enough in all conscience is the field of discretion that remains.”

Unlike our honoree, I do not always abide by Cardozo. I do not always follow tradition. Neither am I methodized by analogy or imprisoned by precedent. Sometimes, I like to blaze new trails, to be eclectic, to be daring, to be revolutionary. However, that does not lessen my admiration and awe of Mr. Justice Mendoza. Strangely enough, it is this very difference in legal philosophy and methodology that attracts me to and causes my unbending esteem for him. I admire his tenacity, consistency and insistence on the welltrodden path, on jurisprudential principles tested by time and opportunity.

Although I do not always concur in all his ponencias, and although he does not always agree with mine, I always - without exception - seek his views on all important issues deliberated upon by the Court. And even during those occasions when I suspect beforehand that his opinion may be opposite mine, I still consciously seek it anyway. And he never withholds it, because we both realize that the Supreme Court thrives in a kettle of free ideas where truth, fairness and justice can be distilled only after all sides, including the most outlandish ones, have been poured out and percolated.

Indeed, we discuss and debate animatedly, even ardently. Although we disagree on ideas, philosophies and other matters of the mind, we never carry those difficulties on a personal plane, much less on an emotional level. In short, our differences are focused on the arguments, never on the person. While our minds may clash some of the time, our friendship is robust and pleasant all the time.

The Greatest Accolades

Verily, I believe that the greatest accolades to Mr. Justice Mendoza comes not from those who always agree with him, but from those who, from time to time, disagree with him honestly and sincerely; yet have no doubt of his single-mindedness of purpose, his unflinching honesty of opinion and his clarity of thought. His most prized commendations come from his erstwhile critics who have become his new converts.

Deeply ensconced among his cherished mementos is the award given to him two months ago by Bantay Katarungan or "Justice Watch" headed by former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga and former Justice Secretary Sedfrey A. Ordoñez. Extolled the former critics and now admirers of our distinguished honoree:

"In April this year, Supreme Court Justice Vicente Mendoza retired without any taint or blemish after forty one (41) years in public service. During the almost nine (9) years when he served on the Supreme Court, the High Court had to draw on his wisdom, his integrity, and independence of mind, in resolving many difficult, controversial cases. He responded with masterful craftsmanship, relying on his deep knowledge of the law and his conscience as his guide. Through his majority and dissenting opinions, he defined the place of judi power in a constitutional democracy, always striking the delicate balance between political majorities that make for popular democracy, on the one hand, and the transcendental claims enshrined in the Constitution on the other."

Today, Mr. Justice Mendoza retires. Now freed of backbreaking workload in the Court, he will no longer bear the heavy yoke of pulling the judicial cart. I am happy for our friend, but sorry for us who will be orphaned. He was a fountain of wisdom that always refreshed us. Now, with neither fountain nor water, how will his orphans quench thirst?

I can talk on and on, all day long, about Mr. Justice my Mendoza until the spoken words are erased from vocabulary. But if my address today is to have any significance, I must in closing leave you two words Justice in my opinion, define the very essence of Mr. Justice Mendoza. These words are veracious and venerable: veracious to mean that he is credible, dependable, truthful and upright; and venerable to mean that he is dignified, knowledgeable, respectable and sagacious.

Friends, ladies and gentlemen, join me in remembering and applauding Mr. Justice Vicente V. Mendoza, the veracious and venerable magistrate of the Supreme Court of the Philippines.

I thank you.
