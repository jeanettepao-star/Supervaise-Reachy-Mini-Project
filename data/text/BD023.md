Chapter 3

A Primer on the APJR

The Action Program for Judicial Reform 2001-2006 (APJR) is the concrete response of the judiciary to the challenge of improving its quality of service and, accordingly, enhancing its image and status as an efficient, trustworthy and accountable dispenser of justice. Founded on Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr.'s vision and mission as expressed in the "Davide Watch," the APJR seeks to build a strong foundation for the long-term development of the judiciary.

The details of the program are found in a 156-page document entitled "Action Program for Judicial Reform 2001-2006." Updates on its reform components were recently incorporated as a supplement. In my book Reforming the Judiciary, I have written about the general framework of APJR. However, due to the length of the latter, I reprinted only its "Executive Summary," which I used as an introductory chapter in the book. In the present volume, I have decided to present a more detailed summary of policies and strategies, projects and accomplishments, and key targets of the AlJR. The following discussion is taken from pertinent documents prepared by the Program Management Office (PMO), the lead office that handles the APJR's day-to-day implementation. I will be intentionally following the presentation format that the PMO used.

Vision and Mission

As mentioned earlier, the APJR operates for the attainment of the judiciary's vision and mission as advocated by Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. in the "Davide Watch," according to which the judiciary is committed to the following vision and mission:

"VISION

"A judiciary that is independent, effective and efficient, and worthy of public trust and confidence; and a legal profession that provides quality, ethical, accessible and cost-effective legal service to our people and is willing and able to answer the call to public service.

"MISSION

"In pursuing the stated vision, the Davide Watch articulates the following goals: (1) delivery

of speedy and fair dispensation of justice to all; (2) judicial autonomy and independence from political interference; (3) improved access to judicial and legal services; (4) improved quality of external inputs to the judicial process; (5) efficient, effective and continuously improving judicial institutions; and (6) a judiciary that conducts its business with dignity, integrity, accountability and transparency."

Reform Challenges and Issues Facing the APJR

Designing a reform program for the judiciary necessarily entailed the evaluation of present conditions affecting its delivery of services. Exhaustive consultations with various stakeholders across the country were conducted in order to identify the issues confronting the judiciary and the needs of all those involved in its administration.

Nine Critical Issues

Addressed by APJR

The result of various consultations revealed nine critical issues, which are now being addressed by the APJR.

First, there is case congestion and delay in the resolution of cases filed. This problem is the most prevalent concern faced by our magistrates. For instance, statistics show that in 2002, there were only 67 metropolitan trial court judges working on 179,449 cases. And while the average output per judge was 871, a total of 121,0834 cases were still pending at the end of the year.

This problem may have resulted from a number of reasons like the increase in the cases filed, inadequacies of judges and trial lawyers, deficient case management systems, loopholes in the law, and procedural rules that invite delay.

Second, there is budget deficiency. In the past three years, the share of the judiciary in the national budget has declined from 1.13 percent in 1999 and 1.20 percent in 1998 to 1.07 percent in 2000. As the bulk of its budget is devoted to expenditures for personnel services, there is thus little flexibility in satisfying the needs for maintenance and other operating expenses and outlays.

Third, there is a need to improve the appointment process for justices and judges and to further insulate it from political influence. A more transparent system is likely to result in the recruitment and promotion of the more qualified and capable ones.

Fourth, there is a need to strengthen judicial autonomy as mandated by the Constitution. In practice, however, the executive and the legislative branches assume administrative and fiscal powers over the judiciary, thus making it vulnerable to political interference.

Fifth, there is a need for re-engineering the human resources development system to support continuing capacity improvement of human resources that are involved in the provision of legal and judicial services. Among the concerns that must be addressed are the uneven quality of         legal education institutions, uncompetitive judicial remuneration, hierarchical and inflexible staffing structure, deficient career pathing, and continuing training and capacity building for nonjudicial employees.

Sixth, the dysfunctional administrative structure and operating systems and inadequate court technologies gravely affect the efficiency and the accountability of the judiciary. There is a need to update these structures and systems in order to respond to the demands of                      decentralization and modernization.

Seventh, public information and collaboration with civil society must be improved and strengthened in order to facilitate people empowerment.

Eighth, the growing perception of corruption in the judiciary must be eliminated by addressing such issues, not only within its ranks but also in other government agencies, through court cases filed against public servants.

Finally, there is a need to improve the poor's access to quality judicial services.

Reform Program Components of the APJR

The APJR recognizes that the issues faced by the judiciary are caused by factors that overlap. Thus, reform efforts are directed not only at a particular issue in isolation of other issues, but as a comprehensive set of solutions that simultaneously and holistically address the concerns.

To this end, the APJR responds through its six-point program of sectoral reforms: [1] judicial systems and procedures, [2] institutions development, [3] human resources development, [4] reform support systems, [5] institutional integrity development and [6] the poor's access to justice.

Judicial Systems and Procedures

Judges have high productivity outputs, considering their volume of work. Despite their efforts, however, they are still saddled with a high number of pending cases. This problem is caused by a number of reasons that include the enactment of different laws redefining the jurisdiction of courts, the variety in the rules of procedure, the increasing number of cases being filed, and the inadequate capability of lawyers and jurists. These factors lead to delay in the delivery of justice, which in turn breeds graft and corruption. And an environment of graft and corruption destroys the people's trust and confidence in the judiciary.

In order to address this concern, the APJR aims to improve court management systems, procedural rules, jurisdictional structures of courts, and alternative dispute settlements.

A milestone pilot project dealing with this problem of delay is the implementation of Case Flow Management (CFM) in the Pasay City trial courts. CFM is the court's supervision of the management of time and case events or of stages in the movement of a case through the court system, from the filing to the disposition of the case. This project aims to provide a systematic tracking system that enables the efficient monitoring of the status of each case. The implementation of CFM began on July 1, 2003 for all cases filed on the same day and onward.

Projects introducing technology intended to modernize the court systems are also underway. The system design for electronic courts was completed in 2001 and will be piloted in five local government units in Metro Manila and Cebu. In line with this project, the installation of new transcription and recording technology is ongoing.

The APJR is also committed to conduct a comprehensive review of the Rules of Court. Of particular concern is the integration of the rules and procedures in the law on e-commerce (RA No. 8792 or the Electronic Commerce Act of 2000).

Another major aspect of this component is the strengthening of Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) mechanisms. These mechanisms refer to several formal and informal processes for the settlement of conflicts, outside of or on the periphery of institutional judicial processes such as arbitration, mediation and conciliation. The promotion of ADR to all the stakeholders is being done through various education and training activities nationwide.

Institutions Development

The institution development component of the APJR is independent of the other branches of government. The judiciary seeks to attain full autonomy while respecting the existing powers and jurisdictions of the executive and the legislative branches.

As an initial step, the full fiscal autonomy of the judiciary, as provided for in the Constitution, is being advocated by the APJR." The current financial management system being adopted in approving the budget of the judiciary limits its freedom to reprioritize and allocate that budget according to its needs. For instance, its annual budget, like the budgets of the other government agencies, undergoes the same revisions by the Department of Budget and Management (DBM). Congress, on the other hand, adopts the revised budget approved by the DBM. The result is a reduced and reconfigured budget that leaves very little room for the judiciary to program its resources.

Moreover, certain matters relating to the internal operations of the judiciary are subject to the DBM's evaluation and approval. These involve modifications in its staffing pattern, position classification and description system, salary grades and rates of positions and tion the incumbents thereof, and the manner in which it may use its income.

Under these circumstances, the lack or absence of judicial autonomy creates a system that is inherently vulnerable to political pressure. Thus, the APJR responds by aiming to improve the organizational capacities and internal management operations of the judiciary. This move will require major shifts in its approaches to planning, budgeting and performance management systems and better internal control and accountability mechanisms.

A study of the proposed improved systems procedures had already been completed. A "Detailed Design Report," which had resulted from various consultations and roundtable discussions, was presented to key officials of the Supreme Court, the Court of Appeals, the Sandiganbayan, the Court of Tax Appeals and the Philippine Judicial Academy. The Report is currently being revised, taking into issues raised and the recommendations made during the presentation. Once completed, the Report Final will be endorsed to the Supreme Court en banc for approval.

Also, an information system using the latest technology will be enhanced in order to achieve the desired operational efficiency and organizational effectiveness. Projects include the automation of case management systems, judicial libraries and judicial archives; and their integration into an enterprise-wide network, with the Office of the Court Administrator as operator of oversight management applications.

Another element of the institutional development reforms is the judicial infrastructure program. It focuses on the creation of an integrated master plan for the construction of new halls of justice and the maintenance of existing ones. The Committee on Halls of Justice (CHOJ) takes the lead in implementing this program. Directly under the supervision of the Chief Justice, it is a committee tasked to ensure tha basic utility services are adequately delivered to the halls of justice.

Human Resource Development

The success of the reform efforts of the judiciary depends largely on the people who will implement them. Thus, human resource development (HRD) has been made an integral component of the APJR.

HRD aims at improving and strengthening the capacities of those who are responsible for performing the various component operations of the judiciary. They include justices, judges, internal personnel of the judiciary, and those in the law profession. HRD also involves the cooperation of institutions like personnel management units and appointing authorities in the judiciary, the Philippine Judicial Academy (Philja), the Judicial and Bar Council (JBC), formal legal institutions, and the continuing legal education service providers.

HRD reforms will focus on judicial education, capacity building and training of nonjudicial personnel, judicial appointments, career development, and position classification and remuneration.

1.     Judicial Education

The institution responsible for programming judicial education is the Philippine Judicial Academy or Philja. It was first organized in 1996 and later granted legislative charter in 1998. It has been tasked with providing a systematically planned and structured regimen of courses to judicial officers as well as to candidates for judicial office.

The APJR is committed to enhance the institutional capacity of Philja. As a first step, the latter has already completed an assessment of the environment or conditions of directions for change. Reform efforts will be directed at improving the profile of Philja's educators, changing the method of instruction from law-school type to adult professional instruction, regionalizing training programs,  and improving the curricula to respond to current trends and emerging perspectives.

Another area of concern is the improvement of legal education. The APJR recognizes that many problems in the profession can be traced to the system of basic legal education and the quality of learning derived from law schools. There is thus a need to review today's law curriculum to make it responsive to changing cultures and legal trends. Specific projects under this sector include the incorporation into the curriculum of a public internship program for law students in order to promote public interest in lawyering, as well as the review of the conduct of bar examinations.

2.     Capacity Building and Training for Nonjudicial Personnel

The APJR is committed to develop not only judicial but also nonjudicial personnel, who are key components in an efficient and  effective judicial organization. The program aims to design a comprehensive human resource development plan that will identify the training needs of the personnel in the context of a reforming judicial organization.

3.   Judicial Appointments and Career Development

The JBC is responsible for screening, assessing and positions of justices and judges. It was created to insulate the appointment process from political pressure and thus ensure that the appointees are the most qualified.

Reforms in judicial appointment will be directed toward the re-engineering of the appointment process for better transparency and accountability. To be pursued are steps for clarifying, simplifying and publicizing procedures for screening, selection and nomination; as well as results of evaluations by the JBC and, if possible, by the President.

4. Judicial Remuneration

Advocacy for adequate and competitive compensation for justices and judges is the core of this reform.

Remuneration, particularly basic salaries in the Philippine Civil Service, is governed by Republic Act No. 6758, otherwise known as the "Compensation and Position Classification Act of 1989." The basic salaries of judges and justices are set in accordance with this law. Compared with remuneration in the private sector, that for the justices and judges is low. It is thus difficult to attract qualified professionals to positions in the judiciary.

Various schemes have been attempted to increase the overall take-home pay of justices and judges, but these have not been satisfactory. However, a strong advocacy to remedy this problem has recently generated a concrete solution. The Twelfth Congress of the Philippines passed on second reading bills granting "[a]ll justices, judges and all other positions in the judiciary with the equivalent rank of justices of the Court of Appeals and judges of the Regional Trial Court" a "special monthly allowance equivalent to one hundred percent (100%) of the basic monthly salary specified for their respective salary grades under Republic Act No. 6758."

Taking into consideration the financial limitations of the government, the proposed law seeks to provide that special allowances be given in four equal installments over a period of four years. The first 25 percent of the compensation package is to be drawn from national government funds, while the succeeding installments are to be sourced from the collections of new fees imposed after the law's effectiveness."

Reform Support Systems

The Reform Support Systems component of the APJR involves efforts that harness the symbiotic relationship of the various stakeholders in the judiciary's reform program in order to sustain its smooth, timely and effective implementation.

This reform component requires advocacy work and consensus-building activities. The target areas of development are organizational culture, public information, education and communication, and networking and collaboration with other branches of government, civil society and the business sector.

1. Organizational Culture

Organizational culture is the system of shared meanings that are held by the members and that distinguish their organization from other organizations. The judiciary has defined its desired organizational culture and provided directions for its attainment through its vision and mission as expressed in the "Davide Watch," which is supported by the Blueprint of Action for the Judiciary.

The reform projects that are aimed at strengthening the judiciary's organizational culture involve the conduct of dialogues and orientation seminars for all court officials and employees, the development of a court identity manual, and the establishment of a performance awards system for judicial and nonjudicial employees.

2.   Public Information, Education and Communication (IEC)

A system of public information, education, and communication is a key component of a well-functioning system of justice and judicial process. Such system is mutually beneficial to the judiciary and the public, for it is a significant factor in organizational improvement and people empowerment.

Efforts at public lEC aim to achieve a consensus among the judiciary's external stakeholders such as the executive department - particularly agencies in the criminal justice system - the legislative department, local government units, the legal profession, business and industry, civil organizations such as nongovernmental organizations and local communities, as well as the mass media.

Specific projects under this sub-component are the production and the dissemination of information materials regarding the APJR, the creation of a manual of court processes and of a mass media-judiciary manual, surveys of awareness and opinion, the establishment of a speakers bureau, legislative tours of legal schools, and multi-sectoral conferences on critical judicial issues.

3.  Networking and Collaboration

Networking is the establishment of working relationships with other sectors and organizations for collaborative and coordinated IEC activities. Networking and collaboration facilitate information exchange, lities, co technology transfer, sharing of equipment and facilities, cost sharing, access to other types of manpower (expertise), and reinforcement and maximum dissemination of messages. They promote better understanding of the problems confronting the judiciary and produce various alternatives for addressing issues and generating commitment to the  resolution of those issues.

The thrust of this reform component, therefore, is to establish strong linkages and to organize community-based or multi-sectoral groups in order to remove any communication gaps and thereby build trust and confidence in the judiciary. Specifically, this component endeavors to assist in the creation of watchdog organizations, multisectoral support groups and inter-agency bodies.

With regard to media relations, friendly and formal contacts with media organizations have been initiated nationwide. Formal relations with media practitioners s assigned to judicial beats have also been established. News bulletins and backgrounders on Court decisions and activities have been issued to the media on a regular basis Such information is also available through the Court's website, www.supremecourt.gov.ph.

Integrity Infrastructure Development

The level of the people's faith and confidence in the judiciary is a gauge of how well it performs its functions or how effectively it attains its purpose in society. For this reason, the judiciary makes it a major component of its reform program to aggressively address the issues that affect people's trust in the justice system.

Thus, the thrust of this component is to eradicate graft and corruption, not only within the ranks of the judiciary, but also in other government agencies through the court cases filed against public servants. This effort is in line with the national government's drive to improve governance, build public trust and confidence locally and internationally, and strengthen the global competitiveness of the economy.

The judiciary recognizes, however, that addressing graft and corruption requires a comprehensive approach. This approach involves not just the strengthening of the disciplinary system or the policing mechanism but, more importantly, removing the institutional weaknesses that render the system and its personnel vulnerable. In the judiciary, the operational areas where opportunities for graft and corruption may arise include the appointment of judges, litigation practices of lawyers, adjudication practices of judges, the procurement and disposal of assets, revenue administration, auditing, and resource support from the local government units.

The implementation of the integrity infrastructure development program necessarily involves institutional and procedural reforms, civil society education and information, and public sector inter-agency collaboration.

Particularly, the following projects are being pursued: 1) establishment and adoption of a comprehensive judicial code of ethics for justices, judges, court personnel and lawyers; 2) streamlining of judicial systems and procedures or to reduce case congestion and delay and to speed up judicial is its services; 3) redefinition of the role of the Office of the Court Administrator to correspond to the decentralization of administrative and financial management operations of the judiciary; 4) adoption of an appropriate disclosure policy that will guide the public as to what information is available and what will not be disclosed; and 5) strengthening of the institutional capacity of the Sandiganbayan to improve its efficiency and clearance rates.

The implementation of these projects is already ongoing. The details of the program designs have already been presented to key officials of the judiciary. These are now being revised,     taking into consideration the recommendations that were raised during the presentation

The Poor's Access to Justice

One of the goals of the judiciary is to ensure the accessibility of the judicial system to everyone, especially the marginalized and disadvantaged sectors, which are vulnerable to injustice and to diminished access to basic services. Thus, the APJR explicitly addresses this issue by ensuring that the poor have equal access to and treatment under the law, within a judicial system that ensures impartiality.

Access has several dimensions. It includes convenience (level of ease in physically reaching the starting point of service, as well as level of ease and speed in receiving the necessary service), as well as availability (presence of the service and its geographical proximity and accessibility) and affordability of several services in the justice system. The APJR aims to respond to the requirements of each dimension.

As an initial activity, a survey was conducted to evaluate the sufficiency of existing programs and to establish preliminary indicators to be used in a baseline study of the poor's access to justice. Baseline studies on inmates, private legal practitioners, the Public Attorney's Office and expanded caseloads have also been completed.

Projects are likewise being directed at reviewing substantive laws and their impact on the access to justice by the poor and disadvantaged. The areas of concern include gender mainstreaming, strengthening of the legal protection of children, and responsiveness of the Code of Muslim Personal Laws.

The APJR also recognizes the need for collaborative work with other agencies involved in providing improved access to justice. Specifically, it is committed to assist in the design and the implementation of the Department of Justice's own reform agenda. Likewise, it will endeavor to strengthen investigative, prosecutorial and legal assistance services rendered by the Department of Interior and Local Governments; and the legal or quasi-legal services provided by other national government agencies.
