__Chapter 7__

*“My thoughts are not your thoughts, And my ways are not your ways;As high as the heavens are above the earth, So high are my ways above your ways, And my thoughts above your thoughts\.”       *__*Isaiah 55: 8\-9*__

Lawyer in the Market Place

Art worked hard and gained much experience in the law office of Salonga\. The demands and responsibilities of providing for a growing family led him, however, to consider setting up his own practice\. Three years after apprenticing in Salonga, Ordoñez, Sicat and Associates, he put up his own firm with the blessings and help of his mentor\. Over the years, theirs would be a long and deep association marked by mutual respect and esteem\. Through different shifts and turns in Art’s career, his former dean would provide perspectives or opportunities that widened his horizon\. 

While the realities of economics triggered Art to establish his own practice, there were motivations fermenting in his heart\. He was still an angry man, and the deprivations of poverty remained an unhealed wound\. Sometimes he would remember what he had missed, and the memories would gnaw at him\. He could have gone to a prestigious university abroad or gotten better medical attention for his parents if he had the means\. These thoughts churned in his mind, casting a long, dark shadow that threatened to eclipse the remarkable strides he had made and the uncanny occurrences that had spared his life on different occasions\. 

He was determined that his children would not experience the deprivations he had gone through\. The goal was to become prosperous and be able to control the lives of other people\. His competence and hard work had given him successes, but these were not enough\. He

envied the affluent and the powerful and aspired to their stations, not just for himself but for his family as well\.

Excellence was still his hallmark and it was not remote that with his acumen and drive, Art could attain his ambitions for prosperity and influence\. He continued applying his best to the practice and teaching of law\. He took on additional load, working in the evenings as a professor in two law schools: one in the Sampaloc university belt and the other in an exclusive convent school for privileged students\. The experience gave him insight into the differences in thinking and lifestyle of the affluent in the private school from those of the poor students in Manila’s university belt\.

He also engaged in business and vowed to himself that travel and education would never be a problem for his children\. That was how he got involved in the travel industry\. He organized Baron Travel Corporation in 1967 with a ₱5,000 participation\. The additional ₱100,000 was provided by friends who wanted to help him\. His father\-in\-law, Joe Carpio, put in P20,000, and one of his wedding sponsors also infused several thousands more to set up the travel company with a starting capital of ₱105,000\. Every one of his benefactor\-investors later sold their shares back to him at book value, but never less than par\.

Baron Travel had a slow start and initially lost money\. The tourism business was not as good as Art had anticipated\. Forestalling the embarrassment of a losing proposition, he devoted more time to the corporation\. He didn’t want to face his family and friends, who were now his stakeholders, empty\-handed\.

The hard work and extra hours paid off\. The business venture became a successful enterprise\. As his agency grew, so too did his reputation\. Art came to be known not just as leader among travel agents but as a pioneering spirit in the tourism industry\. It positioned him to connect with major investors in tourism– the likes of Benigno Toda, chairman of Philippine Airlines; Alfredo Montelibano, chairman of the Sheraton hotel; Antonio Delgado, chairman of the Hilton; Enrique Zobel, chairman of InterContinental; and John Gokongwei, who had

bought the property of Ateneo de Manila and Assumption in the Padre Faura/Pedro Gil area, which he later developed into what is now known as Robinsons Place Manila\. They were the directors of the Philippine Chamber of Tourism, of which Art was a part\-time Executive Director\.

	Art continued to expand his network and influence not just locally but abroad\. One of his major achievements came about on November 15, 1985, when he got voted as chairman of the International Division of the American Society of Travel Agents\. Though American in name and beginnings, it was the world’s largest travel organization then, with over 20,000 members in 128 countries\. 

During the ASTA convention in Rome, delegates elected Art to the top post, making him the first and only Filipino, and the first Asian, to hold the position\. In his inaugural address, he said that the honor was given not just to him personally, but to his country and his people\. He went on to say, “I’d like to believe that my election is really a triumph of the American dream where everyone, even the lowliest in society,

can aspire for and be given the opportunity to lead, to serve and to succeed\.” It was a sentiment that harkened to his own beginnings\.

Art expanded Baron Travel Corporation and put up a branch in California\. It was this business that set him on track to fulfilling the objective of providing transportation and at the same time the means for his five children to pursue graduate degrees at some of the best schools abroad\. There were slips and slides but his legal practice managed to adequately sustain his family\. He had become a director in a commercial bank, The Continental Bank; an insurance company, Overseas Insurance Corporation; and a mining company, Pentagon Mines, Inc\. The controlling stockbroker of these three companies was Vicente Tan, a client of his law office\. 

The company directorships, borne of his law practice, were eclipsed by his deep involvement in the high\-profile tourism industry\. However, during the martial law years, the law office, as well as many other professional firms and commercial companies, did not do well\.

The three companies on whose boards he sat were closed by General Fabian Ver in 1973 and 1974 and taken over by the government with neither due compensation nor due process\. 

Vicente Tan was detained indefinitely on suspicion that he was a front for Communist China\. Though the suspicion was totally false, he was detained anyway and released only after several years\. He never recovered his company or his wealth\. The business climate in the country stagnated for years\. It was then that Art explored ventures in Hong Kong\.

# The Hong Kong Opportunity

Mao Zedong’s Cultural Revolution in mainland China in the 1960s became the catalyst for the acceleration of the then British territory’s industrialization\. Hong Kong was not directly governed by the Chinese Communist Party, but the close proximity to and economic ties with mainland China impacted significantly on the city’s economy\. The  

closure of borders, as well as the severance of transportation and communication links, made it difficult for Hong Kong businesses to conduct trade with their counterparts in China\. It limited the flow of goods and people between the two regions\.

Mao’s political movement led to a shift in the structure of Hong Kong’s economy\. Hong Kong had traditionally been a manufacturing hub, producing low\-cost goods for export to the rest of the world\. The disruption brought about by the Cultural Revolution forced many manufacturers to look for new markets and diversify their products\.

This led to the emergence of new industries such as finance, tourism and services which became increasingly important to Hong Kong’s economy\.

During the 1980s and the 1990s, Hong Kong emerged as a major player in the global financial industry, with its stock market growing rapidly in size and importance\. This boom in the Hong Kong bourse was fueled by a range of factors, including the city’s strategic location and its role as a financial hub, the increasing openness and liberalization of China’s economy, and a surge of foreign investments into the city\. Geographically, it was ideally situated as a gateway between East and West, favorable for businesses looking to expand in Asia\. In particular, Hong Kong’s status as a free port with low taxes and few restrictions on trade made it an attractive destination for foreign investors\. The Crown Colony developed into a capitalists’ haven with remarkable growth that historians and economists marveled at\.

Art keenly observed the ultra\-rapid amassing of wealth in Hong Kong\. When he became counsel to one of its rising conglomerates, an opportunity to invest in the stock market opened up\. His client was involved in transactions worth billions of Hong Kong dollars and he benefited financially from those dealings\. He began to experience perks once unattainable, afforded by the principal who had engaged him\. He had to travel frequently to the United States and to Europe to evaluate the legal, political and financial impacts of the project the conglomerate was investing in or buying, the Philippines included\.

It was a quick study for the ways of the world\. He also had the growing means to live well\. The compensation for his legal services provided him the capital to procure shares in the Hong Kong stock exchange\. It was an auspicious move\. As the robust market continued its upward trend, the value of his shares reached as much as $2 million\. It was a considerable amount even then, the equivalent today is easily 10 times more or about $20 million\. 

The poor boy became rich beyond his imaginings\. He had developed an acumen for business and looked to increase his investments beyond Hong Kong\. Art studied world trends and businesses on the upswing and saw the possibilities of profitable returns in real property developments\.

The 1990s was a period of significant growth in the global real estate market, with property values soaring in many countries around the world\. The upsurge was boosted by low interest rates, increased availability of credit and a general sense of optimism\. 

Art studied the movements of prevailing tendencies before deciding on what would be most favorable for maximum returns and

minimal risk\. He allocated earnings from his dividends for the acquisition of a 10\-acre property with 182 townhouses in Texas\. It was a source of pride to have been able to buy a prime development beside a flowing river in the second largest US state\. He calculated it was a sound investment, as the South Central region was experiencing significant growth\. The property was valued by an American appraisal company at over five million dollars\.

The late 1970s and early 1980s brought an influx of Mexican investors to Texas, attracted by the region’s favorable tax laws and growing economy\. It became even more enticing as developers constructed large\-scale projects, building commercial establishments, shopping centers and residential communities\. This was the time of a worldwide property boom as economies were on an upturn\. Service sectors were experiencing substantial growth, and liberalized financial systems made credit easily accessible\. Consumers were spending much more than previously\. Art was able to procure his investment by making a down payment of $800,000, with the rest covered by three mortgages, which was allowed in the US\. He invested an additional $500,000 for a total investment of $1,350,000\. The property was valued by an American appraisal company at over $5 million\.

# Man of the World	

Art began to live it up\. He was tasting the privileges of wealth and enjoying these immensely\. He traveled frequently to different continents, spending at least half a year abroad\. He could now afford first\-class plane tickets, accommodations in five\-star hotels and dinners  in celebrated restaurants\. He became accustomed to yachts and Rolls Royce limousine services, sporting Piaget and Patek Philippe watches, which were given by the conglomerate’s major owner who was a watch collector\. 

Art circulated with famous names and faces in society and was entertained in the mansions of the ultra\-rich\. He hankered to be famous himself, and it did happen\. His photos regularly appeared in pages covering social events, and he became fodder for high\-society gossip\. It seemed that he had finally achieved the pinnacle of his boyhood’s glory dreams\.

Meanwhile, the flipside to the dissipated life he was living was the absence of peace and quiet\. He felt dissatisfied, always wanting more but not finding satisfaction in the acquisition\. He felt no fulfillment in the overindulgence of his appetite and ego\. The pursuit of temporal gratifications fed a sense of meaninglessness and loss of purpose\. The greater price for the lifestyle he had taken up was alienation from his wife and children\. He was spiraling away from them but couldn’t get himself to disengage from the fast life he had coveted\.

# The Shadow of Failure

The heady days of high living came to an abrupt end when the Hong Kong stock market crashed in the early 1980s\. Suddenly, then Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher announced the signing of the Sino\-British Joint Declaration that declared Hong Kong would be

returned to China in 1997, effectively ending the British colonial rule in the region\.

There was an immediate impact on the Hong Kong stock market\. The Hang Seng index, which tracks the largest companies listed in the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, fell by more than 20% as uncertainty loomed over the future under Chinese rule\. Worries were rife that the new government would not be able to maintain the economic stability that had been achieved under British rule\. It was a painful catastrophe that purportedly led several people to commit suicide\. 

Art was heartbroken\. He lost more than two million U\.S\. dollars which he had earned from his Hong Kong business ventures\. The conglomerate he was counsel for, where he had heavily invested more than that amount, became insolvent because of heavy debt\.

His misfortunes continued when the Mexican dollar was devalued by the Mexican Central Bank\. The reduction of the currency’s value led to financial contagion beyond the country’s borders\. Many Mexican businesses and investors pulled out of the state, resulting in a sharp

drop in demand for Texas real estate\. Prices of properties plummeted and many people lost their homes in bank foreclosures\. Investors were unable to sell their land assets, which led to a surplus of unsold homes\.

The majority of Art’s tenants who were renting his apartments were Mexicans\. They left, and the lost revenue from their rentals caused Art to default on his mortgage payments\. The bank automatically foreclosed the property after three consecutive months of unpaid installments\. There was no right to repurchase\.

It was a very low point in his life\. After having experienced what seemed like the heights of living well, Art felt the loss more acutely\. He felt dismally poor again\. 

Such vagaries of life can bring to the fore qualities that define a person’s character\. It may be that the grim developments stirred the newsboy within, the one who had slept on sidewalks clutching dreams, hawked cigarettes, and cleaned shoes while tenaciously clinging to aspirations for a better life\. They were not phantoms of a distant past but part of who he was\.

Besides ambition, there, too, was a deeper hunger yet unrealized which he had not yet put a name to or acknowledged\. Failure was not part of his life plan but it would become part of a redirection towards that which he had yet to discover\.

# Service Above Self

Art was at a crossroads, and he weighed his options\. He could remain in the United States and explore other possibilities there with the hope of regaining his lost fortune from dollar\-generating investments\. The alternative was to make a go of his local business endeavors and his legal practice\. Leni tilted the decision for the latter\. She did not want to live abroad\. 

Consequently, Art reverted full time to the Philippines and applied himself to growing his practice and his other enterprises in the country\. He remembered that his father had told him to be a good lawyer, and once again his parent’s voice prodded him in that direction when all else seemed bleak and lost\. He focused on building up his practice, which had been relegated behind his business pursuits\.

Art joined the Rotary Club of Manila in 1983\. It was an association that brought out his civic\-mindedness and expanded his network\. That was where he met the then Chief of the Philippine Constabulary, General Fidel V\. Ramos, who joined the club at the same time as Art\. Ramos would become President of the Philippines nine years later and play a significant role in Art’s career\.

The Rotary Club of Manila was the oldest club in Asia and the largest outside the United States where it first began\. The organization promoted the ideals of service, contributing to the improvement and development of community and high standards in occupational and career practices\.

The Manila Rotary was just the beginning of his business and professional resurrection\. Art’s acumen in navigating his métier gained the trust and respect of industry colleagues and associates, subsequently leading to his election as a major officer and director in leading organizations including the Philippine Chamber of Commerce and Industry and the Management Association of the Philippines\.

When the country regained its political and economic freedom in 1986 following the peaceful EDSA Revolution, his law practice picked up and flourished as many of his friends assumed top government posts during the presidency of Corazon C\. Aquino\.

By then, he was actively participating in the undertakings of the Manila Rotary\. Spending his time and energy on the meaningful projects of the club, Art rediscovered a sense of purpose in those programs benefiting the underserved\. He was, as he had been during his NUSP days, helping others who had so much less\. His concern then was for the rights and well\-being of the students; now it was for the marginalized sectors\. Rotary was into medico\-surgical missions, free eye operations, health and nutrition education for disadvantaged communities, street children, and deserving but financially challenged students\. 

Refocusing his attention on others in need also became a remedy for the pain from the crushing devastation of his losses\. He knew the

poor man’s challenges, having been there himself, and understood what would be most helpful to them\. Members took note of the lawyer\-businessman and, not surprisingly, installed him as president in 1990\.

During his induction at the Manila Hotel Fiesta Pavilion he declared, “\[O\]ur most important commitment is for the Manila Rotary to be for the poor, the afflicted and the weak\. This is the base, the most important component, the driving force of our vision, our triad and our pyramid\. As your president, I vow to channel our major treasures, time and talent in helping our less fortunate brothers\. To me this is the *raison d’etre, *the reason for being, of Manila Rotary – to help those who cannot help themselves\.”

He added, “Feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, comforting the afflicted, sheltering the homeless, and protecting the oppressed are not an option but a duty and responsibility of the well\-to\-do, the talented and the privileged\. Indeed, when we, in our utter nakedness, face our Creator and Ultimate Judge on that inevitable day, He will not ask how many digits are in our bank accounts; or how many diplomas have been conferred on us; how many plaques were awarded; or even how many times we have gone to church\. Rather, our Creator and Master will ask us whether we fed the hungry, clothed the naked, sheltered the homeless, comforted the afflicted, healed the sick and protected the oppressed\. Jesus said that whenever we do these acts of charity and love for the needy, we really do them for God\. He said, “I assure you, as often as you did it for the least of My brothers, you did it for Me\.”

Rotary provided an environment that nurtured his spirit of service\. Art was getting his groove back with an emerging sense of responsibility for others\. It may have been cultivated from exposure to persons like his father, who had supported the education of others besides his own children; and Fr\. Nolan, who took the time to provide him basic awareness of God and to teach him to pray\. Certainly, Dean Salonga’s principled conduct founded on a faith life deeply impressed him\. His own wife Leni was a powerhouse of prayer from the day they first met, and, with considerable certainty, was a spiritual stronghold in their home and constant intercessor for her husband\.

Undoubtedly the devastation of loss from failed ventures, the feeling of emptiness amidst worldly gain and recognition, the feeling of being stripped of attachments and reduced to humbler circumstances–all this could have factored in the palpable shift expressed in his induction speech\. All of the above and more were part of an inner transformation that was as dramatic as it was real\.
